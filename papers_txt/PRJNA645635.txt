Abstract
Aims: Infantile eczema, usually coupled with a range of hypersensitive
phenotypes, has come into notice with its rising prevalence and unclear
pathogenesis. Recent studies show close ties between eczema and an infant’s
intestinal ﬂora. To gain a further understanding of the interactions between
microbiota and eczema, we studied the breast milk ﬂora as a new factor and
present the links among breast milk ﬂora, infant intestinal ﬂora and infantile
eczema through a cohort study in Northeast China.
Methods and Results: Fifty-two families were recruited with either an eczema
or healthy infant younger than 6 months. Analysis and predictions using
amplicon
sequencing
of
microbiota
found
that
Biﬁdobacterium
and
Bacteroidetes were enriched in healthy and eczema infant stools, respectively,
consistent with previous reports. For breast milk ﬂora, more ‘positive’ bacteria
such as Akkermansia were enriched in breast milk from healthy infants’
mothers. Further, higher bacterial delivery efﬁciencies were found in pairs of
breast milk ﬂora and infants’ stool ﬂora of families with eczema infants
compared with families with healthy infants. Bacteroidetes, a widely known
indicator of eczema, was found delivered more in eczema pairs. Further
metagenomic predictions revealed that the breast milk microbiota participated
signiﬁcantly less in metabolism and immune system pathways, particularly in
antigen processing and presentation and in Th17 cell-related pathways.
Conclusions: In conclusion, as with other components of breast milk, the
breast milk microbiota closely associates with infants’ health via mother–infant
bacterial delivery and metabolic functions.
Signiﬁcance and Impact of the Study: Our research aimed to ﬁll the gap
between the eczema and breast milk ﬂora and describe the connections among
breast milk and intestinal ﬂora and eczema.
Introduction
Eczema, also known as atopic dermatitis, is a worldwide
epidemic of chronic inﬂammatory skin of children and
adults. An increasingly high prevalence has made eczema
a common chronic disease worldwide, affecting about
10–20% of the population (Weidinger et al. 2018). The
prevalence of eczema is higher in infants. In China, for
example, 30% of 1-year-old infants are affected by
eczema compared with 13% of children aged 1–7 years
(Guo et al. 2016; Guo et al. 2019). Skin itching and
immunoreactions caused by eczema and associated aller-
gic symptoms undermine the healthy growth and quality
of life of infants and their families. Simultaneously,

although the immune mechanism of eczema is well
explained, the trigger of eczema is unknown (Weidinger
et al. 2018).
Recent developments in studies the human micro-
biome provide new insights to explore infant eczema and
related allergic symptoms. Extensive research of associ-
ated microbiomes reveals an association with eczema. For
the skin microbiota, the loss of diversity and enrichment
of Staphylococcus aureus is related to eczema (Baurecht
et al. 2018). Further, for the intestinal microbiota, the lar-
gest microbiome of humans, considerable effort has been
devoted to identifying the connection between the intesti-
nal ﬂora and eczema (Zimmermann et al. 2019). The rel-
ative
abundance
of
Bacteroidaceae,
Clostridiaceae,
Enterobacteriaceae,
Lactobacillaceae
and
Biﬁdobacteri-
aceae is related to eczema. Probiotics such as Lactobacil-
laceae and Biﬁdobacteriaceae are added to infant formula
to relieve eczema’s symptoms (Szajewska and Horvath
2018; Schmidt et al. 2019). However, the correlation
between intestinal ﬂora and eczema is controversial
because of insufﬁcient data. Moreover, little is known
about the role played by microbes in breast milk. Studies
published as recently as 5 years ago report that breast
milk is devoid of bacteria (Mcguire and Mcguire 2017).
But more recent, evidence shows that breast milk ﬂora is
associated with the microbiota of the mother’s gut, skin
and the infant’s oral cavity, which contribute to an
infant’s health (Toscano et al. 2017). Here, as breast milk
is the only original food for babies, we are intrigued by
the association between breast milk ﬂora, infant intestinal
ﬂora and eczema.
Therefore, to further identify the characteristics of
the breast milk ﬂora, infant intestinal ﬂora and eczema,
we collected stool and breast milk samples from 1- to
6-month-old infants and their mothers. The V4 region
of 16S rRNA was sequenced, and downstream analysis
was applied according to OTU clustering. According to
this analysis, our research describes the features of
breast milk and the intestinal ﬂora of infants with
eczema using OTU statistics and linear discriminant
effect size (LEfSe) analysis. We then compared the
mother source microbe ratios in eczema and healthy
infants’ gut. Further, A SparCC network was built for
all samples divided by different groups, and a func-
tional prediction subsequently made showed the relative
inﬂuence on pathway levels between different groups.
The breast milk ﬂora had an intimate connection with
the infant intestinal ﬂora. More species of bacteria
from breast milk were delivered to eczema infants vs
healthy infants. Further, according to metagenomic pre-
dictions, the ﬂora of eczema breast milk was less asso-
ciated with human metabolism pathways than ﬂora of
healthy breast milk.
Materials and methods
Sample collection
The present project was approved and supervised by the
Institutional
Research
Board
of
Harbin
Children’s
Hospital (reference number HRYLL201609). Volunteers
were recruited at hospitals and communities, and their
characteristics recorded by researchers during winter in
Harbin City and Daqing City, Heilongjiang Province,
China. All samples fulﬁlled the features as follows:
mothers
and
infants
did
not
take
antibiotics
for
≥1 month and probiotics within 2 weeks. The included
infants
received
exclusive
breastfeeding
and
were
younger than 6 months. The designations of eczema
and healthy babies were based on the diagnoses of pae-
diatricians.
Samples were collected, including breast milk and
infant stool, from 52 pairs of mothers and infants
(n = 104). Samples were divided into four groups accord-
ing to body sites (breast milk or stool) and infants’ health
(healthy or eczema), including stool samples of eczema
pairs (n = 27), breast milk samples of eczema pairs
(n = 27), stool samples of healthy pairs (n = 25) and
breast milk samples of healthy pairs (n = 25). For each
pair, 5 ml of breast milk from the mother and 2 g of her
baby’s stool were collected using a sterile bag or box,
temporarily stored at 20°C, and transported at 80°C
before sequencing.
Amplicon sequencing
Samples in dry ice were sent to BGI Co., Ltd (Shen-
zhen, Guangdong Province, China) for 16S rRNA DNA
sequencing. The workﬂow was composed of eight steps,
including
sample
DNA
extraction,
sample
quality
testing, library creation, targeted amplicon enrichment,
end-repair, 30-poly(A) addition, adapter ligation, library
quality control and sequencing. DNA extraction was
based on tissue extraction and column chromatography
performed by BGI Co., Ltd. A Qubit Fluorometer and
a Qubit dsDNA BR Assay Kit were used for DNA con-
centration measurements, and agarose gel electrophore-
sis was used for DNA integrity measurements. The 103
samples were checked, and one sample was discarded,
and 30 ng of each suitable sample was used for library
creation. The V4 region of 16S rRNA was enriched
using PCR (515F: 50-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-30
and 806R: 50-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-30). Library
products were puriﬁed using Agencourt AMPure XP
beads and assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer.
Sequencing was performed using an Illumina Hiseq2000
platform.

OTU analysis
Raw data from the Illumina platform were preprocessed
following Douglas’s method as paired-end reads (Fadrosh
et al. 2014) and were appended to tags using FLASH (ver.
1.2.11) (Magoc and Salzberg 2011). OTUs were clustered
from tags using
USEARCH (ver. 7.0.1090) and annotated
using the Greengene database with a 97% similarity
threshold (Edgar et al. 2011; Edgar 2013).
Alpha diversity analysis
Chao, ACE, Shannon and Simpson indexes were used to
determine the alpha diversities of four groups, including
stool samples from eczema and healthy infants, breast
milk samples from eczema infants’ mothers and healthy
infant’s mothers. This analysis was performed using
MOTHUR (ver. 1.31.2) (Schloss et al. 2009).
PCA analysis
PCA analysis of clustered OTUs was performed using the
ade4 package in R (ver. 3.1.1).
LEFSE analysis
LEfSe analysis was performed using LEFSE software.
Overlapping OTU analysis
The overlapping OTU counts and percentages between pairs
of stool and breast milk from infants, and their mothers were
ﬁltered and manually annotated using Excel software. Venn
diagrams were generated using Venny (Oliveros 2015).
SparCC network analysis
SparCC network simulation was performed using Micro-
biome analysis (Achal et al. 2017). The permutations of
this network = 100, P = 01 and correlation = 04.
Metagenomic predictions
Metagenomic predictions based on the OTU data of four
groups were achieved using Piphillin (Iwai et al. 2016).
We selected 141 pathways at the KO3 level. GraphPad
Prism 8 software was used to evaluate statistical signiﬁ-
cance.
Statistical analysis
GraphPad Prism 8 software was used to perform and dis-
play statistical analyses. An unpaired t test was applied
for conditions with an F test indicating no signiﬁcant dif-
ference, and the Mann–Whitney test was applied to con-
ditions when an F test indicated a signiﬁcant difference.
Results
Study cohort and sequence analysis
The morbidity of eczema among infants younger than
1 year is highest and keeps rising along with the develop-
ment of urbanisation, as 168% of incidence rate of
eczema
was
reportedly
increased
in
Norway
within
5 years (Mohn et al. 2018). Moreover, morbidity is asso-
ciated with environmental factors such as temperature,
climate and season. Winter and spring are the two sea-
sons with a high incidence of eczema in dry and cold cli-
mates (Silverberg et al. 2013). The winter in Northeast
China
is
dry
and
cold,
with
an
average
tempera-
ture = 15°C, making this area an ideal place to identify
factors associated with eczema.
Considering the features above, to explore the interac-
tions among infants’ intestinal ﬂora, breast milk ﬂora and
eczema, 104 samples were collected from hospitals and
communities in Heilongjiang Province, Northeast China
during winter. One hundred-three samples were qualiﬁed
and sequenced later, with one sample discarded because
of low-quality DNA. These samples were divided into
four groups, including groups with 27 stool samples from
eczema infants (abbreviated as ES), 26 breast milk sam-
ples from their mothers (abbreviated as EM), 25 stool
samples from healthy infants (abbreviated as HS) and 25
breast milk samples from mothers of healthy infants (ab-
breviated as HM).
To avoid bias caused by sampling error, our research
considered age, probiotic and antibiotic intake and other
personal characteristics using strict selection criteria and
background inquiries. Relative information is shown in
Fig. 1a and Dataset S1. The age range of recruited infants
was 1–6 months. Recruited infants and their mothers did
not take probiotics within 2 weeks and no antibiotics
within 1 month. No signiﬁcant difference was found
among infants’ ages, delivery mode or sex between
eczema pairs (ES and EM) and healthy pairs (HS and
HM; Fig. 1a).
After sample collection, 103 of 104 samples were quali-
ﬁed and sequenced using paired-end sequencing of the
V4 region of bacterial 16S rRNA using an Illumina Hise-
q2000 platform. For each sample, approximately 100 000
clean reads were clustered into OTUs. The total OTU
number of each group is shown in Fig. 1b. Breast milk
held more OTUs compared with stool samples, and more
OTUs were found in the eczema relative groups than the
healthy groups.

Alpha diversity was chosen to proﬁle the ﬂora’s ecosys-
tem and bacterial community structure according to rich-
ness and evenness. Four indexes were used to assign the
alpha diversities to the four groups. In Fig. 1c, species
richness is represented by Chao1 and ACE indexes. There
was a signiﬁcant distinction between stool samples from
infants with and without eczema, as the species richness
of ES group was much higher than HS group. Moreover,
the species richness of breast milk showed no signiﬁcant
difference, likely because the standard deviation within
breast milk groups were much higher.
However, although the species richness was unequal, no
signiﬁcant differences were found in species diversities
among breast milk and stool samples with or without
infantile eczema, as indicated by the Shannon and Simpson
indexes. These two indexes represent the evenness of each
group, which is inﬂuenced by gut physiology and host diet
(Reese and Dunn 2018). Therefore, we speculated that the
evenness of the gut and breast milk ﬂora is not the major
inﬂuence on eczema. This result is consistent with those of
other studies (Wang et al. 2016; Lee et al. 2018).

Eczema-related microbiota differentiation occurs in
breast milk and intestinal ﬂora
We next investigated the differences between the eczema
and healthy groups in their stool and breast milk ﬂora.
Samples were arranged based on OTU compositions
using a principal components analysis (PCA) coordinate
axis. As shown in Fig. 1d, PC1 was 146%, indicating that
the ﬂora composition of each sample was similar. The
coordinates of each group showed that ﬂora composi-
tions were inﬂuenced more by different anatomical sites
than symptoms. For example, the coordinates of the EM
group overlapped more with HM group, rather than ES
group.
For the same body site, sample coordinates from
healthy and eczema groups were slightly different but did
not completely overlap (HM vs EM, ES vs HS), indicating
that eczema-related changes of the microbiota occurred
in the intestinal ﬂora and the breast milk ﬂora. Interest-
ingly, a larger overlapping region was observed between
EM vs ES than HM vs HS, indicating that ﬂora composi-
tion similarity between breast milk and stool samples in
eczema families was higher than that of healthy families,
indicating a closer connection between eczema families
than healthy families.
Eczema-related microbial development trends and
indicators are different
To identify microbiota features, OTU compositions of
each group were annotated using the Greengene database.
Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes and Proteobac-
teria were the main phyla of the four groups according to
annotated results. As shown in Fig. 2a, in stool samples,
the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes was higher in the
ES group than in the HS group, while the relative abun-
dance of Proteobacteria was higher in the HS group
(P > 005). In samples of breast milk samples, the relative
abundance of Actinobacteria in the EM group was signiﬁ-
cantly higher than that of the HM group (P < 005). The
relative abundance of Firmicutes in the EM group was
higher than the HM group (P > 005), while the relative
abundance
of
Bacteroidetes
and
Proteobacteria
was
higher in the HM group (P < 005).
Next, we arranged all samples by their ages and simu-
lated the trends of the main bacteria on their phyla,
expecting to ﬁnd developmental trends related to infantile
eczema. As shown in Fig. 2b, for stool samples, the per-
centages of Actinobacteria and Bacteroidetes increased
with age in the HS and ES groups, while the trends of
Firmicutes and Proteobacteria were opposite. The per-
centage of Firmicutes increased slowly with age in ES
group and rapidly deceased in the HS group. The trends
of Proteobacteria were different in that the percentage
decreased in the eczema group and increased in healthy
group with age. When we next analysed breast milk sam-
ples, the trends of the main phyla were roughly the same
as in the HM and EM groups, although the percentage of
Firmicutes was twofold higher in the EM group than in
the HM group for all ages.
Evidence indicates that eczema is closely linked to the
human microbiota (Marrs and Flohr 2016). Here, we
used LEfSe analysis to present indicators of different
groups. Figure 2c shows that in the ES group, Bacteroide-
tes and Erysipelotrichi were signiﬁcantly observed on the
phylum level, because Erysipelotrichaceae was enriched
on the family level. Indicators of the HS group were Acti-
nobacteria and Biﬁdobacteriaceae. In the EM group,
Weissella from Leuconostocaceae, Bacillaceae from Bacil-
lales and Flavobacterium were the indicators of micro-
biota. The indicators of HM group were placed farther
from the other three groups in the cladogram and were
more diverse (Verrucomicrobiaceae from Verrucomicro-
biae, Xanthomonadaceae from Xanthomonadeles, Sphin-
gomonadaceae from Alphaproteobacteria, Moraxellaceae
and Akkermansia).
More OTUs were associated with eczema pairs
All recruited families were exclusively breastfed, which
allowed us to explore how the microbiome of breast
milk, the only food source for infants, was delivered to
an infant’s intestine. We compared the overlapping OTU
counts between pairs of samples (stool and breast milk
from the same family). The OTUs were clustered based
on the sequencing results of the microbiota, and we
therefore treated overlapping OTUs within pairs as those
delivered from breast milk to the infant’s intestine. Fig-
ure 3a shows the numbers of overlapping OTUs in
eczema pairs and healthy pairs as well as the percentage
of overlapping OTUs of different pairs.
Eczema pairs represented signiﬁcantly higher overlap-
ping OTUs than healthy pairs, indicating that bacterial
deliveries within eczema infants and their mothers were
signiﬁcantly higher than the deliveries in healthy families.
Figure 3a shows further that the overlapping OTU num-
bers accounted for the total OTU number of stool or
breast milk samples according to groups. Only a slight
difference was found between the stool and breast milk
samples, because the percentage was somewhat higher in
the eczema group than the healthy group. These results
are consistent with the PCA data (Fig. 1d), indicated by
the distance on the coordinate axis, which was closer
between HM and EM than ES and HS. Interestingly, only
about 20% of breast milk OTUs was delivered in stool
samples, which was much lower than our prediction.

Bacteria only enriched in breast milk ﬂora appeared in
the co-networks above, and 20% of breast milk bacteria
were detected in stool samples. We therefore simulated
the human-related metabolic abilities of ﬂora among the

were evaluated using this tool. We collected 141 pathways
that are associated with Cellular Processes, Environmental

organismal systems.
Next, we separately compared the predicted abundance
between the eczema and healthy group of stool samples
and breast milk samples. The results are presented in
Dataset S2. There was no signiﬁcant difference in stool
samples between the eczema and healthy groups. How-

from six KO2 groups of the three KO1 groups are shown

most of the 18 pathways, indicating that the microbiota
of HM group had a higher capability to regulate infant
intestinal homeostasis.
We next simulated the frequencies of antigen process-
ing and presentation, Th17 cell differentiation, IL-17 sig-
nalling and steroid hormone biosynthesis pathways. We
found that they were signiﬁcantly lower in the EM group
compared with the HM group (P < 001). Steroids act as
anti-inﬂammatory

2008). Considering these ﬁndings and our simulated
results, we conclude that the ﬂora in the breast milk of
the eczema group exhibited weaker immune regulation-
related metabolism and contributed to the onset of infan-
tile eczema.
Discussion
Our research here ﬁlls the gap in the features of the EM
ﬂora, contributing more data related to the features of
the ES ﬂora and further describes the linkage among
breast milk ﬂora, intestinal ﬂora and eczema.
We ﬁrst investigated the richness and evenness of bac-
terial ecosystems according to alpha diversity. A series of
studies led us to conclude that decreased alpha diversity
was linked with a dysbacteriosis such as childhood diar-
rhoea, obesity and IBD (Deweerdt 2014; Rouhani et al.
2019; Schirmer et al. 2019). However, during the devel-
opment of infant intestinal ﬂora, high alpha diversity is
not always an effective signal, because one study shows
higher alpha diversity in 1-year-old children with subse-
quently lower cognitive development (Carlson et al.
2018), and both intake foods and anaerobic intestinal
environment
shaping
the
intestinal
ﬂora
after
birth
decreases alpha diversity (Dominguezbello et al. 2019).
Here, we found a signiﬁcantly higher richness in the stool
ﬂora of eczema, although the bacterial evenness of breast

Similar higher richness of alpha diversity was found in
research focused on cow’s milk allergy (Aurelie et al.
2019). A hypothesis based on this appearance argues that
lower selective abilities of breast milk and infants’ imma-
ture intestinal environment, or the weaker colonising
capacities of major functional bacterial such as biﬁdobac-
terial species in eczema families (He et al. 2001), leads to
less prebiotic production in the gut, which further
induces an immature gut ecosystem with a ‘half-packed’
mucosal barrier that introduces harmful bacteria with a
chance to colonise the gut (Hirata and Kunisawa 2017).
This may explain why only richness rather than evenness
was higher in the eczema group. The evenness of alpha
diversity is related with host diet and gut physiologies
(Reese and Dunn 2018). Our research excluded the effects
of antibiotics and probiotics on the results, indicating
that the evenness of alpha diversity is not related with
infantile eczema.

composition can provide more information about inter-

inﬂammation and hypersensitivity, according to previous
studies (Bunyavanich and Berin 2019). In our study, for

In contrast, the indicative bacteria in the ES group
were extensively found in the gut of those with allergic
diseases. For instance, a higher Bacteroidetes abundance
in the intestinal ﬂora of infants residing in Estonia and
Finland triggers their high prevalence of autoimmune dis-

in infants with allergic diseases (Nakayama et al. 2011;
Bunyavanich et al. 2016; Zheng et al. 2016). Here, Erysi-

group, which is the ﬁrst observation of its association
with eczema. However, previous studies show that these
bacteria are associated with an increased inﬂammatory
response (D. et al. 2018).
Bacillales and Akkermansia were breast milk indicators
in the present study. The role of Bacillales is not fully
understood, because some Bacillus species inhibit inﬂam-
mation, while some may be pathogenic (Elshaghabee
et al. 2017). In contrast, Akkermansia is an indicator of
healthy breast milk, which confers beneﬁcial functions as
a promising probiotic (Zhang et al. 2019). The informa-
tion about other indicators in breast milk ﬂora is insufﬁ-
cient. However, we conclude that the breast milk ﬂora
and intestinal ﬂora of the healthy group were more salu-
tary than those in the eczema group.
Numerous studies conﬁrm the important functions of
the breast milk microbiota associated with immunological
activities and their abilities to promote infant intestinal
development (Toscano et al. 2017). Our research signiﬁ-
cantly expands the boundary of the inﬂuence of breast
milk
ﬂora
on
eczema
infants’
intestinal
ﬂora.
A
combination of breast milk ﬂora and infant stool ﬂora
was present in the SparCC network (Fig. 3d). A similar
network among eczema infant stool ﬂora is established
(Wang et al. 2016; Zheng et al. 2016). Notably, our pre-
sent research is the ﬁrst to include the breast milk ﬂora
inside the conetwork.
In our network, strains such as Gemellales and Strepto-
phyta, which mainly exist in breast milk, were shown
here to have relationships between bacteria in stools sam-
ples, which may inﬂuence the appearance of eczema.
Next, considering the ratio and types of delivered bacteria
between a mother’s breast milk and the infant’s gut, bac-
teria from breast milk represent 70–88% of stool ﬂora in
the ﬁrst 3 months after birth (Murphy et al. 2017). Our
research shows that about 50% of stool OTUs are present
in the ﬁrst 6 months after birth (Fig. 3a, left). For the
observed delivered bacteria of each pair, more Bac-
teroidaceae and Bacillaceae were delivered in eczema
pairs, which were indictors of ES ﬂora and EM ﬂora.
Approximately 20% of the OTUs in breast milk colonised
the stool samples (Fig. 3a, right), suggesting that the bac-
teria in breast milk may play their roles without colonisa-
tion.
Infants may intake between 1 9 105 and 1 9 107 bac-
teria per day (Fernandez et al. 2013), and we assume that
breast milk ﬂora inﬂuences infant intestinal development
by colonisation and may modify the system through its
own metabolism during the initial development of the
infant’s gut ecosystem. This assumption is supported by
the metagenomic prediction based on our results (Dataset
S2).
Higher bacterial infection pathway enrichments were
observed in EM ﬂora, while healthy breast milk ﬂora showed
a consistently higher abundance in most of the predicted
selected pathways. Thus, we observed a signiﬁcantly higher
concentration in glycan biosynthesis and metabolism, meta-
bolism of cofactors and vitamins and immune system. The
weaker ﬂora functions of EM contribute to the unbalance of
the gut environment and delayed immune system develop-
ment. Intestinal pathogen infections are associated with coe-
liac disease, food sensitivity and common co-occurrence
with allergic diseases after antibiotic abuse (Caminero et al.
2019; Ortqvist et al. 2019).
Bacterial-associated
nutrition
acquired
from
the
mother is considered important for infant health (Kang
et al. 2020). Riboﬂavin mediates the development of
mucosal-associated invariant T cells and promotes tissue
repair, and lipopolysaccharide is associated with immune
tolerance induction as a cosensitiser with the house dust
mite (Bachus et al. 2019). Moreover, a signiﬁcantly lower
abundance of antigen processing and presentation path-
ways and Th17 cell-related pathways was identiﬁed in the
EM ﬂora. According to the hygiene hypothesis, antigen

An issue that was not addressed in this study was
whether the recruited babies with parental history of
atopy. Parents with eczema is a risk factor for infantile
eczema (Kolokotron et al. 2012). Without this back-
ground, it is hard to ascertain whether genetic factors
inﬂuence the relationships between host-microbe and
infantile eczema. Another potential limitation is that our
research only addressed with babies who were exclusively
breast-fed in urban areas. Whether the results are suitable
for other feeding patterns or rural areas is unknown.
In summary, using amplicon and downstream analy-
ses, here we describe the interactions among breast milk
ﬂora, infant intestinal ﬂora and eczema. Changes were
observed in eczema-associated breast milk ﬂora and
intestinal ﬂora. The bacteria delivered from breast milk
to the eczema-infantile intestinal ﬂora were distinct,
compared with that of the healthy infant gut, with a
higher abundance of eczema-related bacteria. Moreover,
breast milk-typical bacteria were observed in interactive
networks along with infantile intestinal ﬂora, suggesting
that the breast milk ﬂora may independently function in
the development of the infant intestinal ecosystem rather
than only serving as a source for colonisation. This
assumption was supported by subsequent metagenomic
predictions. The eczema-associated breast milk ﬂora had
weaker human-associated metabolic activities, particularly
associated with predicted hypersensitivity-related path-
ways. As a result, our research reveals that the composi-
tion and metabolic activities of breast milk ﬂora are
important factors that contribute to the development of
eczema.
