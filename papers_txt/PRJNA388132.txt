An intricate relationship exists and interactions occur between gut microbiota and colorectal cancer (CRC). Radical surgery
combined with adjuvant chemotherapy (AC) serves as the mainstream therapeutic scheme for most CRC patients. The current
research was conducted to assess the effect of surgery or chemotherapy on gut microbiota. Forty-three CRC patients who
received radical surgery and AC were enrolled. Fecal samples were collected preoperatively, postoperatively, and after the first to
fifth cycles of postoperative chemotherapy. The microbial community of each sample was analyzed using high throughput 16S
rRNA amplicon sequencing. Compared with preoperative samples, fecal samples collected postoperatively exhibited a sig-
nificant decrease of obligate anaerobes, tumor-related bacteria, and butyric acid-producing bacteria. However, a significant
increase of some conditional pathogens was observed. In addition, the AC regimen (CapeOx) was found to alter intestinal
microbiota dramatically. In particular, several changes were observed after chemotherapy including an increase of pathogenic
bacteria, the “rebound effect” of chemotherapy-adapted bacteria, the shift of lactate-utilizing microbiota from Veillonella to
Butyricimonas and Butyricicoccus, as well as the decrease of probiotics. Both radical surgery and CapeOx chemotherapy exert a
non-negligible effect on the gut microbiota of CRC patients. Microbiota-based intervention may be beneficial for patients during
postoperative clinical management.

INTRODUCTION
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer in
both genders worldwide (Siegel et al., 2017a). Radical sur-
gery combined with adjuvant chemotherapy serves as the
main therapeutic scheme for most CRC patients; however, its
actual efficiency varies greatly among individuals and the
overall poor 5-year survival is still lamentable (Siegel et al.,
2017b). In such a scheme, radical surgery, mechanical bowel
preparation, and prophylactic use of antibiotics cause drastic
changes in the post-operative intestinal environment (Lassen
et al., 2009). Moreover, the pathological status of the intes-
tine may also induce gut microbiota dysbiosis, thus resulting
in an increase of harmful microbe populations and subse-
quently contributing to systemic side effects (Li et al., 2018;
van Vliet et al., 2009). Many studies have confirmed the
changes of intestinal microbiota before and after radical re-
section of CRC, including a decrease of obligate anaerobes

as well as an increase of facultative anaerobes and aerobes

Furthermore, the post-operative use of probiotics in CRC
patients can improve the gut microbiota and mucosal barrier,
while decreasing the incidence of infectious complications
(Liu et al., 2011).
Currently, three major chemotherapy regimens are em-
ployed for advanced CRC, including regimens of capecita-
bine plus oxaliplatin (CapeOx), combined 5-fluorouracil
(FU)-leucovorin therapy plus either irinotecan (the FOLFIRI
regimen) or oxaliplatin (the FOLFOX regimen) (Cersosimo,
2013; Zhang et al., 2017). The majority of chemotherapy
drugs have cytotoxic effects and frequently result in side
effects such as mucositis and adverse gastrointestinal tract
symptoms, which can be attributed to the fact that che-
motherapy drugs cause the regeneration of the colonic epi-
thelium, which disturbs gut homeostasis, thereby driving
more dramatic changes in the gut environment (Cunningham
et al., 1985; Yang J. et al., 2013). Studies also indicated that
intestinal microbiota are closely related to the side effects of
postoperative chemotherapy and gut microbiota dysbiosis is
proportional to the severity of chemotherapy responses
(Shen et al., 2017). It is also believed that the intestinal
microbiota may affect the efficacy of chemotherapy through
bacterial translocation, immune regulation, biotransforma-
tion, enzymatic degradation, and reduced gut biodiversity
(Bruneau et al., 2018). Two widely used chemotherapeutic
drugs, i.e., 5-FU and irinotecan, significantly change the
diversity and abundance of the gut microbiota, which may
induce mucositis in CRC chemotherapy patients (André et
al., 2004). In addition, 5-FU was found to directly affect the
composition of luminal gut microbiota in human patients, as
characterized as increased relative abundance of Bacteroides
and decreased abundance of Escherichia/Shigella (Van-
lancker et al., 2017). One study compared the microbial
abundance of fecal samples before and after chemotherapy in
28 non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma patients; fecal samples col-
lected after chemotherapy exhibited decreased Firmicutes
and Actinobacteria but increased Proteobacteria when com-
pared to those collected before chemotherapy (Montassier et
al., 2015). The abundances of Enterococcus spp. and Lac-
tobacillus spp. were reduced in rat colon after a single in-
traperitoneal (IP) dose of 5-FU while the total number of
facultative anaerobes increased, leading to the increased in-
testinal permeability and impaired mucosal barrier function
(Stringer et al., 2009). The increased abundance of Proteus,
Clostridium, and Peptostreptococcus together with the re-
duction of Bacillus and Bifidobacterium species in fecal
samples were also noted after a single IP dose of irinotecan in
rats (Stringer et al., 2008). Unfortunately, in current reports
of the effects of CapeOx treatment on the intestinal micro-
biota, only the disturbance of the latter was addressed, and no
study has been found highlighting the global/specific mi-
crobial changes and the relevance between microbiome
changes and adverse events of chemotherapy (Flórez et al.,
2016).
Radical surgery and various chemotherapeutic agents can
perceptibly change the intestinal microbiota in CRC animal
models and CRC patients, reflecting alterations of the in-
testinal environment; thus, the intestinal microbiota can be a
good predictor of the recovery of intestinal function as well
as prognosis. However, previous studies mainly used animal
models, and the information therefrom might not be as ac-
curate as that from clinical tissues. Moreover, few studies
simultaneously analyzed the changes in intestinal microbiota
caused by both radical resection of CRC tissues and suc-
cessive multiple cycles of CapeOx therapy. Therefore, in the
present study, a full set of fecal samples was collected from
CRC patients receiving radical surgery combined with Ca-
peOx therapy, and these samples were then analyzed using
the high throughput 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing. This
study aims to not only clarify the effects of radical surgery
and anticancer drugs on the composition and function of gut
microbiota in CRC patients. It also demonstrates a short-term
continuous trend of changes in intestinal microbial abun-
dance after surgery and the following multiple cycles of
CapeOx therapy, providing invaluable theoretical evidence
for improving gut microbiota during postoperative patient
management.
RESULTS
General descriptions of sample classification and en-
rolled patients
Seventy-four fecal samples were collected from 43 patients,
including 19 fecal samples from preoperative CRC patients
(Group B). Ten fecal samples were collected after surgery
and before the first postoperative chemotherapy (Group A0).
The remaining 45 fecal samples fell into five groups, i.e., A1
to A5, which consisted of 7, 9, 11, 11, and 7 fecal samples
obtained after the first to fifth round of chemotherapy, re-
spectively. None of the patients received radiotherapy or
chemotherapy before surgery. Clinical characteristics of the
patients are listed in Table 1. All patients experienced gas-
trointestinal (GI) mucositis-related symptoms, such as nau-
sea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhea with varying
degrees of severity. None of the CRC patients were found to
undergo local recurrence or regional/distant metastasis dur-
ing postoperative follow-up.

Microbe network construction and function prediction
To predict microbiota function, we evaluated the gene
abundance of all data that were annotated by the Clusters of
Orthologous Groups of proteins (COG) database. The box
diagram in Figure S3 in Supporting Information shows that
the higher gene abundance was concentrated in the func-
tional classes such as Carbohydrate transport and metabo-
lism, Amino acid transport and metabolism, as well as
Transcription. Based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes
and Genomes databases, we established differentially ex-
pressed functions between preoperative and postoperative
patients with the threshold of a Bonferroni adjusted Q value
<0.01. In the L2 category, genes belonging to Metabolism of
Terpenoids and Polyketides, and Signaling Molecules and
Interaction were more abundant before surgery (Figure 4A).
Different functional pathways before and after chemotherapy
were noted. Only in the L3 category, carbon fixation path-
ways in prokaryotes, replication recombination and repair
proteins, and arginine and proline metabolism were found to
be significantly abundant in post-chemotherapy groups A1,
A4, and A5 (Figure 4B−D, respectively). We then evaluated
the correlation between the top 50 specific bacteria according
to the abundance and variation of each species in the seven
groups, including those with both positive and negative
correlations, and mapped a cytoscape-network diagram with
the screening criteria of R>0.6, P<0.05 (Figure 4E). Strik-
ingly, all differential taxonomic groups were most highly
correlated with each other in each group as follows: Alistipes
and Butyricimonas (correlation coefficient r=0.87) along
with Enterococcus and Butyricimonas (r=−0.68) in Group B;
Candidatus_Stoquefichus and Parasutterella (r=1) along
with Escherichia-Shigella and Alistipes (r=−0.92) in Group
A0; Escherichia-Shigella and Enterococcus (r=1) along with
Lactobacillus and Flavonifractor (r=−0.99) in Group A1;
Holdemanella and Prevotella_7 (r=1) along with Rumino-

DISCUSSION
Microbiome-targeted studies aim to shed light on the com-
position and function of the intestinal microbiome in various
pathological conditions (Fan et al., 2018; Huttenhower et al.,
2014). By studying a homogeneous cohort of CRC patients
and employing the high throughput 16S rRNA amplicon
sequencing, we have revealed for the first time a significant
shift in human fecal microbiota in response to radical surgery
and CapeOx therapy.
First, we found the composition of preoperative and
postoperative intestinal microbiota varied significantly in
CRC patients. Specifically, the ratio of Bacteroidetes to
Firmicutes, which can affect nutrient uptake, decreased sig-
nificantly in postoperative samples. Similarly, obesity also
changed the intestinal microbiota, manifested by a shift of
dominant bacteria from Bacteroides to Firmicutes (Cui et al.,
2013; Schwiertz et al., 2010). For example, patients with
obesity exhibited an increased abundance of Blautia and
Escherichia-Shigella, which may contribute to the intestinal
inflammation (Gao et al., 2018). Likewise, we speculate that
radical surgery may induce intestinal inflammation through a
similar intestinal microbiota change (Ottosson et al., 2018).
We found the abundance of the beneficial bacterial groups
were markedly decreased after CRC surgery, so were the
obligate anaerobes, the most common bacteria in the human
gut and important for maintaining intestinal environmental
stability (Hooper and Gordon, 2001; Neish, 2009). In parti-
cular, the abundance of Bacteroides, Parabacteroides, Fae-
calibacterium, Prevotella_9 and Bifidobacterium decreased
after surgery, which could shift the gut metabolic profile and
increase the risk of gut inflammation and other adverse
events (Hooper and Gordon, 2001; Miquel et al., 2015). It is
noteworthy
that
Enterococcus,
which
promoted
CRC
through activating macrophages to exert a bystander effect,
decreased after surgery (Wang X. et al., 2008). Meanwhile,
the abundance of Fusobacterium, another well-established
CRC related bacteria, also decreased postoperatively (Yu et
al., 2017). A recent study found that Fusobacterium could be
detected in the liver metastasis and blood samples, indicating
that this genus may be a crucial driver in the metastasis of
CRC cells (Bullman et al., 2017; Kwong et al., 2018). In this
regard, we suggest that radical surgery is beneficial because
it removes tumor lesions as well as most tumor-associated
microbes, which effectively reduces the risk of tumor re-
currence. However, an increase was observed in the abun-
dance of potentially pathogenic bacteria such as Escherichia-
Shigella,
Enterobacteriaceae_unclassified,
Veillonella,
Morganella, Streptococcus, and Proteus, while butyrate-
producing bacteria including Bacillus, Bilophila, Barne-
siella, and Enterococcus significantly decreased after radical
surgery. Escherichia-Shigella, Enterobacteriaceae_uncla-
ssified, and Streptococcus are often associated with the de-
velopment of cirrhosis and inflammatory bowel disease
(Lupp et al., 2007; Ponziani et al., 2019; Thorkildsen et al.,
2013). Moreover, butyrate-producing bacteria have anti-in-
flammatory and immunoregulatory functions, enabling them
to become a promising treatment for intestinal inflammation
(Zhang et al., 2016). In this regard, because radical surgery
involves resection of part of the intestinal tissue, some
beneficial bacteria may also be reduced, contributing to un-
favorable outcomes such as inflammatory responses, in-
testinal
function
disorders
and
nutritional
absorption
dysfunction.
CapeOx provides one of the most important chemotherapy
regimens because it effectively prevents postoperative re-
currence of CRC and improves overall survival (Grothey and
Venook, 2018; Nagasaka et al., 2016). However, this regi-
men was also reported to cause adverse treatment-related
events such as neutropenia (12.6%), peripheral sensory
neuropathy (6.3%), diarrhea (4.2%), and thrombocytopenia
(4.2%) (Danno et al., 2017). In our cohort, all the patients
experienced mucositis and gastrointestinal tract symptoms
after CapeOx therapy. Because previous literature has shown
that intestinal microbiota are associated with side effects of
chemotherapy toxicity, we next analyzed the effect of Ca-
peOx regimens on intestinal microbiota by 16S RNA am-
plicon sequencing (Touchefeu et al., 2014). First, we found
CapeOx therapy reduces the abundance of cancer-promoting
bacteria (Enterococcus) and certain pathogenic bacteria
(Escherichia-Shigella, Morganella, Pyramidobacter, and
Proteus) to some extent, although Escherichia-Shigella did
not decrease (Vanlancker et al., 2017). Enterococcus faecium
is a facultative pathogenic bacterium that often causes life-
threatening infections especially in nosocomial settings, and
can also indirectly promote the progression of CRC by
producing reactive oxygen species and exerting a bystander
effect through activated macrophages (Gröbner et al., 2010;
Yang Y. et al., 2013). Morganella was associated with ab-
dominal incision infection and a relatively severe degree of
sepsis, while Proteus induced secondary infections, espe-
cially urinary tract infections, implying that CapeOx therapy
may prevent these complications to some extent (Drze-
wiecka and Lewandowska, 2016; Erlanger et al., 2017).
Moreover, the lactate-utilizing and butyrate-producing bac-
teria Butyricimonas and Butyricicoccus increased while the
conditional pathogens of Veillonella decreased after CapeOx
therapy. These organisms were suggested to compete for
lactate with sulfate-reducing bacteria such as Desulfobacter.
High concentrations of sulfate are toxic for the gut epithe-
lium and may contribute to bowel disease (Marquet et al.,
2010). After chemotherapy, the lactate-utilizing microbiota
shifted from Veillonella to Butyricimonas and Butyr-
icicoccus. This could be a beneficial adaptation, because
lactate could otherwise be used as a co-substrate for sulfate-
reduction. Sulfate-reducing bacteria, however, were not de-

tected in the present study. It should also be noted that the
abundance of Dorea, Ruminococcaceae_UCG-010, Strep-
tococcus, Prevotella_9, Mogibacterium, and Roseburia
fluctuated after one or two cycles of chemotherapy. How-
ever, after five chemotherapeutic cycles, their abundance
recovered, sometimes even showing a ‘‘rebound-effect’’
with numbers recovering to initial levels. It is possible that
when the gut microorganisms adapt to the environmental
challenges, some of them may develop resistance to che-
motherapeutic drugs.
In addition, we also found the ratio of Bacteroidetes to
Firmicutes showed an increase after chemotherapy, which is
contrary to the perioperative trend. This change has also been
found in mice following a broad-spectrum of antibacterial
therapy (Hill et al., 2010). A recent study found Firmicutes
was abundant in obese patients, suggesting this group of
bacteria may be involved in the host energy absorption from
diet (Turnbaugh et al., 2006). Thus, we suggest that che-
motherapy may impair the energy absorption of cancer pa-
tients through decreasing the abundance of Firmicutes. We
also noted that CapeOx therapy led to an increased abun-
dance of many conditionally pathogenic bacteria such as
Collinsella, Anaerostipes, Bilophila, Comamonas, Weissella,
Bacteroides, and Eggerthella. For instance, intestinal Col-
linsella have been shown to exert pro-inflammatory effects
through increasing intestinal permeability and releasing in-
flammatory cytokines (Chen et al., 2016). Eggerthella lenta
was increasingly found in patients with severe comorbidities
especially in immunosuppressed patients, and this was re-
lated to the occurrence of bacteremia; a case of bypass graft
infection and bacteremia caused by Anaerostipes caccae has
also been reported (Woerther et al., 2017; Workneh et al.,
2016). The opportunistic pathogen, Bilophila wadsworthia
caused various intestinal diseases, while probiotic interven-
tion reduced its abundance in irritable bowel syndrome pa-
tients (Veiga et al., 2014). The increased abundance of
Bacteroides was also found in acute leukemia patients un-
dergoing chemotherapy (Nyhlén et al., 2002). Bacteroides
can benefit their host by eliminating potential pathogens in
the gut, but some species such as B. fragilis are opportunistic
human pathogens, causing abdominal infection and appen-
dicitis (Bloom et al., 2011). Shifts of these genera and an
increase of Collinsella have also been found in autistic pa-
tients who often suffer from symptoms of gastrointestinal
disorders (Strati et al., 2017). Additional clues are worth
mentioning. For example, chemotherapeutic drugs have been
shown to reduce the abundance of Lactobacillus. The safety
and efficacy of Lactobacillus in the prevention and man-
agement of inflammatory bowel disease have been proved
(Le and Yang, 2018). Thus, probiotics may be used to
modulate the intestinal microbiota, inducing colonization
resistance against pathogens and modulating host immune
responses (Borchers et al., 2009). The results of our study
support this assumption; Lactobacillus could be adminis-
tered to alleviate the drastic shift in intestinal microbiota.
Prebiotics are not degraded in the upper digestive tract and
can promote the growth of beneficial autochthonous micro-
organisms in the gut. Probiotics, together with prebiotics, are
referred to as synbiotics. The use of synbiotics after surgery
and chemotherapy treatments for cancer patients can im-
prove the intestinal microbial environment and significantly
decrease the incidence of postoperative infection as well as
the severity of chemotherapy-related mucositis (Sugawara et
al., 2006; Wang H. et al., 2013). Lastly, we identified the
microbial function alterations in preoperative, pre-che-
motherapy, and chemotherapy groups. Changes of carbohy-
drate transport and metabolism were revealed by COG
analysis. Metabolic pathway analysis indicated that carbo-
hydrate metabolism was affected by CRC surgery and Ca-
peOx therapy, e.g., the concentration of short-chain fatty
acids decreased after surgery and chemotherapy, possibly
due to the reduction of obligate anaerobes and the increase of
facultative and aerobic bacteria (Ohigashi et al., 2013). Al-
terations in the metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides,
fatty acid biosynthesis, carbon fixation pathways and re-
plication, recombination and repair proteins, revealed by
Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analysis, may
also be important factors involved in an altered microbiota
structure.
Despite our novel findings, some inherent limitations still
merit attention. First, the sample size is not large enough to
comprehensively clarify and validate the effects of surgery or
chemotherapy on gut microbiota. Thus, the following vali-
dations based on sufficient clinical samples, especially
samples from the same patient, are necessary and expected to
be achieved by more multicenter cooperations. Second, re-
lated to methodologically, 16S rRNA sequencing was used to
detect gut microbiota in our study, which can be further
improved and complemented by more accurate techniques
such as metagenomic sequencing. Furthermore, metabo-
lomics detection techniques are also recommended to clarify
the alteration of intestinal metabolites so that the integrated
microecosystem network affected by surgery and che-
motherapy can be better understood. Third, since none of the
patients have undergone tumor recurrence during our follow-
up, we still do not know if postoperative microbiota changes
are correlated with tumor recurrence or chemotherapy effi-
cacy; this should be given attention to in our follow-up
studies. Finally, this study focused on the CapeOx scheme
and failed to investigate the effects of other chemotherapy
schemes (e.g., FOLFOX or FOLFIRI) or targeted drugs
(such as Cetuximab) on gut microbiota. This limitation is
expected to be solved by more clinical samples and well-
designed follow-up schemes.
Our study found that both radical surgery and CapeOx
chemotherapy scheme exert a non-negligible effect on the

approaches effectively eliminate some CRC-related bacteria
to help prevent the recurrence of CRC, they may increase the

(such as probiotics or prebiotics) may be of great benefit for
improving microbiota dysbiosis in CRC patients receiving
radical surgery combined with use of the CapeOx scheme.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Study participants and fecal sample collection
Participants diagnosed with CRC were recruited between
February 2016 and January 2017 in Shanghai Tenth People’s
Hospital, China. We have access to information that could
identify individual participants during or after data collec-
tion. All the patients had received radical surgery that was
performed by the same surgeons (stage IV patients received
R0 resection), followed by the standardized CapeOx therapy
scheme (day 1, oxaliplatin, 130 mg, intravenous injection;
capecitabine, 1.0 g, oral, twice daily, from day 1 to day 14).
Ornidazole (0.5 g) and cephalosporin (2 g) was used for
preoperative gastrointestinal preparation (twice for one day)
and postoperative infection prevention (twice for three days).
Fecal samples were collected at hospital registration (Group
B), three weeks after surgery (Group A0), as well as the end
of the first to fifth cycles of chemotherapy (groups A1–5),
respectively. Each fecal sample was collected in a sterile tube
and then stored at −80°C prior to microbial analysis.
The informed consent was provided by each patient fol-
lowing the protocol approved by the Institutional Review
Board of Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital. The study pro-
tocols were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee
of Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital.
Microbial DNA extraction and PCR amplification of 16S
rRNA
Genomic DNAwas extracted from 74 fecal samples using an
E.Z.N.A.
® soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA,
USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The DNA
concentrations and purity were determined using a Nano-
Drop 2000 UV-vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific,
Wilmington, MA, USA), and the DNA quality was examined
by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The V4–V5 hypervari-
able regions of the bacteria 16S rRNA gene were amplified
with primers 515F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGG-3′) and
907R (5′-CCGTCAATTCMTTTRAGTTT-3′) on a thermo-
cycler PCR system (GeneAmp 9700, ABI, Thermo Scien-
tific). The PCR program was as follows: 95°C of 3 min, 27
cycles of 30 s at 95°C, 30 s at 55°C, and 45 s at 72°C, and a
final extension at 72°C for 10 min. The PCR reactions were
performed in triplicate, and a 20 μL mixture contained 4 μL
5× FastPfu Buffer, 2 μL 2.5 mmol L
–1 dNTPs, 0.8 μL each
primer of 5 μmol L
–1, 0.4 μL FastPfu polymerase, and a
10 ng template DNA. The PCR products were extracted from
a 2% agarose gel and then purified using a AxyPrep DNA
Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA,
USA) and quantified using a QuantiFluor™-ST fluorimeter
(Promega US, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manu-
facturer’s protocol.
Illumina MiSeq sequencing
Purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar solutions and
underwent paired-end sequencing (2× 300 nt) on an Illumina
MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according
to the standard protocols in Majorbio Co., Ltd. (Shanghai,
China). The raw reads were deposited into the National
Center for Biotechnology Information Sequence Read Ar-
chive database (Accession No.: SRP108098).
Bioinformatic analysis
Raw FASTQ files were de-multiplexed, quality-filtered by
Trimmomatic-0.30,
and
merged
by
FLASH
1.2.7.
USEARCH software 7.1 was used to analyze the 16S rRNA
raw sequences (Edgar, 2013). Original sequences were fil-
tered to obtain the optimized ones, which were assigned to
operational taxonomic units (OTUs) based on OTUs having
≥97% similarity. OTU cluster analysis was performed after
the chimeric sequences were removed. The composition of
each sample community was counted at each taxonomic le-
vel, i.e., domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family,
genus, and species, by comparing our data with the following
databases: 16S bacteria and archaea ribosomes Silva (Re-
lease 123, http://www.arb-silva.de) (Quast et al., 2013); RDP
(Release 11.3, http://rdp.cme.msu.edu/) (Cole et al., 2009);
Greengene (Release 13.5, http://greengenes.secondgenome.
com/) (DeSantis et al., 2006); ITS fungus Unite (Release 7.0,
http://unite.ut.ee/index.php) (Kõljalg et al., 2013); functional
genes FGR (Release 7.3, http://fungene.cme.msu.edu/) (Fish
et al., 2013). Mothur 1.30.1 was used to assess the diversity
indices of gut microbiota.
Statistical analysis
All experimental data were analyzed using SPSS 17.0 and
GraphPad prism 7.0 software. Measurement data (height,
weight, body mass index, and age of the preoperative,
postoperative, and chemotherapy groups) were expressed as
mean±standard deviation. We used a Student’s t and Pear-
son’s Chi-square tests to analyze and compare the continuous
and categorical variables, respectively. The differences be-
