Abstract  
27 
Escherichia coli is a leading contributor to infectious diarrhea and child mortality worldwide but it 
28 
remains unknown how alterations in the gut microbiome vary for distinct E. coli pathotypes and 
29 
whether these signatures can be used for diagnostic purposes. Further, the majority of enteric 
30 
diarrheal infections are not diagnosed with respect to their etiological agent(s) due to technical 
31 
challenges. Towards addressing these issues, we devised a novel approach that combined 
32 
traditional, isolate-based and molecular biology techniques with metagenomics analysis of stool 
33 
samples and epidemiological data. Application of this pipeline to children enrolled in a case-
34 
control study of diarrhea in Ecuador showed that, in about half of the cases where an E. coli 
35 
pathotype was detected by culture and PCR, E. coli was likely not the causative agent based on 
36 
metagenomic-derived low relative abundance, level of clonality and/or virulence gene content. 
37 
Our results also showed that diffuse adherent E. coli (DAEC), a pathotype that is generally 
38 
underrepresented in previous studies of diarrhea and thus, thought to not be highly virulent, 
39 
caused several small-scale diarrheal outbreaks across a rural to urban gradient in Ecuador. 
40 
DAEC infections were uniquely accompanied by co-elution of high amounts of human DNA and 
41 
conferred significant shifts in the gut microbiome composition relative to controls or infections 
42 
caused by other E. coli pathotypes. Our study shows that diarrheal infections can be efficiently 
43 
diagnosed for their etiological agent and categorized based on their effects on the gut 
44 
microbiome using metagenomic tools, which opens new possibilities for diagnostics and 
45 
treatment. 
46

Introduction 
62 
Diarrheal diseases remain a major public health issue worldwide, especially in developing 
63 
countries where poor sanitary conditions and limited access to clean water exacerbate the 
64 
burden [1]. Although most diarrheal cases self-resolve relatively quickly [2, 3] and hence the 
65 
causative agent(s) is never identified, there are instances where acute diarrheal infections lead 
66 
to death, making detailed investigations of the causative agents and their signatures necessary. 
67 
Such cases include not only acute child diarrhea in the developing world but also foodborne 
68 
outbreaks linked to contaminated food and other causes in developed countries [2-4].  
69 
 
70 
Diagnostic testing for enteric pathogens has historically relied on culture-based techniques or 
71 
culture-independent molecular assays such as PCR that are based on cultured material. 
72 
Accordingly, reference (cultured) isolates are the foundation for public health surveillance [5-7]. 
73 
It is important to realize, however, that culture-based techniques are typically time consuming, 
74 
costly and require significant expertise, while they frequently miss moderately or distantly 
75 
related relatives of the reference isolates, as many bacterial microorganisms are difficult to 
76 
growth or are even non-cultivable, although still viable. For example, some pathogens, including 
77 
Escherichia coli, can quickly lose the ability to be cultured upon exposure to laboratory 
78

http://aem.asm.org/
Downloaded from 
 
4
conditions [8]. In addition, culture-based techniques may detect transient pathogens that are not 
79 
related to the current diarrheal episode. Several of these limitations also apply to (culture-
80 
derived) culture-independent methods: in cases where PCR detection is applied for example, 
81 
there is still a high risk of detecting traces of asymptomatic carriage or residual DNA from 
82 
previous infections or missing divergent (target) sequence of close or distant relatives to the 
83 
reference isolate [9, 10]. Further, and perhaps more importantly, culturing does not provide 
84 
quantitative estimates of the pathogen abundance, its virulence potential and diversity, and 
85 
does not allow the characterization of the gut microbiome response, which could be important 
86 
for diagnosis.  
87 
 
88 
In the United States, a total of 38.4 million cases of foodborne illness per year cannot be 
89 
attributed to specific causes, and the proportion caused by yet-to-be-described microbial agents 
90 
is unknown [9, 11, 12]. In developing country settings, even using highly sensitive methods such 
91 
as qPCR to detect a broad array of pathogens, >10% of moderate-to-severe diarrhea cases can 
92 
not be traced to an etiologic agent and the fraction of false positive signal for cases with 
93 
detected pathogens, due to transient pathogens for instance, remains essentially speculative 
94 
[12]. Culture-independent metagenomic approaches can be employed to robustly assess 
95 
diarrheal infections. 
96 
 
97 
E. coli is a gut commensal of vertebrates, including humans [13]; it can nevertheless cause a 
98 
broad range of diseases, including intestinal and extra-intestinal infections, through the 
99 
acquisition of accessory genes. The relevance of this species in diarrheal disease has been 
100 
extensively illustrated. For example, the Global Enteric Multicenter Study (GEMS) demonstrated 
101 
that Shigella (a distinct lineage within the E. coli phylogenetic clade) and Enterotoxigenic E. 
102 
coli (ETEC) producing heat stable toxin (ST), either alone or in combination with heat labile toxin 
103 
(LT), are among the most important pathogens associated with moderate-to-severe diarrhea 
104 
 on October 7, 2019 at UCSF LIBRARY
http://aem.asm.org/
Downloaded from 
 
5
(MSD) in children younger than five years old in developing countries [11, 12]. ETEC, 
105 
enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), and Shigella were responsible for an estimated 18% of 
106 
diarrhea deaths in children <5 years old globally [12]. Despite the relevance of E. coli as a 
107 
diarrheagenic pathogen, little is known about the effect(s) of pathogenic E. coli infections on the 
108 
gut microbiome, such as how the structure and diversity of the community changes during 
109 
active infections. 
110 
 
111 
While commensal E. coli is typically a relatively minor component of the colonic gut microbiome 
112 
in humans, where it represents less than 0.1% of the total bacterial cells (estimated at ~108 
113 
cells/g) [13, 14], during enteric infections with invading pathogenic E. coli strains or another 
114 
enteric pathogen, the overall signal of E. coli in the gut microbiome can increase substantially 
115 
[15, 16], allowing the detection and recovery of genomes based on culture-independent 
116 
metagenomic techniques that could accurately represent the invading population. In addition, 
117 
quantifiable shifts in the proportion and diversity of the entire gut microbial community in 
118 
response to the enteric infection disturbance have been observed [17-20]. However, the number 
119 
of samples analyzed in these previous studies were limited and the methodological approaches 
120 
employed were based almost exclusively on taxonomic marker genes such as 16S rRNA that 
121 
have limited resolution. Furthermore, and perhaps more importantly, in all previous studies the 
122 
etiological agent of infection was inferred by DNA sequencing and was not validated by an 
123 
independent approach. Accordingly, it remains unclear whether or not different pathogens such 
124 
as different E. coli pathotypes produce distinct alterations in the indigenous microbiome due to 
125 
their characteristic mechanism of infection and virulence factors they excrete and employ during 
126 
infection. Alternations of the gut microbiome composition upon infection have been shown to 
127 
take place quickly, within 1-2 days or a few hours in some cases [16, 20]. Thus, metagenomic 
128 
sampling following the onset of diarrhea, even in a cross-sectional sampling strategy, could 
129 
potentially provide insights into pathotype-specific signatures and quick results for diagnostics.

During the interaction with the intestinal epithelial cells, in the phase of attachment and 
131 
colonization, distinctive machineries of pathogenicity are known to be used by each of the six 
132 
known E. coli pathotypes, depending on whether the pathogen invades the cell, produces 
133 
biofilms or secretes toxins [21]. For example, ETEC is thought to adhere to the small bowel 
134 
mucosa and delivers secretory enterotoxins [22]. Enterohemorrhagic E. coli (EHEC) adheres to 
135 
the colonic mucosa and transduces a signal, resulting in secretory diarrhea. Concurrently, the 
136 
organism releases Shiga toxin, resulting in local and systemic effects [21]. Enteroaggregative E. 
137 
coli (EAEC) adheres to intestinal epithelial cells and produces a thick mucous gel (biofilm) and 
138 
causes intestinal secretion and damage [23]. Diffusely adherent E. coli (DAEC) has been shown 
139 
to elicit elongation of microvilli in vitro, although this effect has not been demonstrated in vivo, 
140 
and has been considered not as virulent as other pathotypes [24]. EPEC elicits the attaching 
141 
and effacing lesion in the small bowel, resulting in intestinal secretion [25] and enteroinvasive E. 
142 
coli (EIEC) invades the colonic mucosa, giving rise to inflammatory enteritis [26]. Among the six 
143 
pathotypes, EPEC and ETEC have received special attention in developing countries because 
144 
they are believed to be major pathogens causing diarrhea in children under five [1, 14]. It 
145 
remains to be elucidated whether these biological differences in infectious mechanisms disturb 
146 
the gut microbial community in different, distinguishable ways.  
147 
 
148 
We devised a novel bioinformatic approach that combined traditional, isolate-based and PCR 
149 
techniques with metagenomic and epidemiological data to identify diarrheal cases where E. coli 
150 
was most likely the causative agent and evaluate whether pathotype-specific signatures in the 
151 
disease-state gut microbiome exist that distinguish among different E. coli infections. For this 
152 
purpose, we took advantage of a large epidemiological, case-control study of diarrhea that was 
153 
carried out over a period of 18 months in Northern coastal Ecuador (named EcoZUR for “E. coli 
154 
en Zonas Urbanas y Rurales”). The study was uniquely suited to address these questions as it 
155 
included data on diarrheal disease outcome, a large collection of pathogenic E. coli isolates 
156

from diarrheal and non-diarrheal (control) samples, and other socio-demographic and clinical 
157 
data from study subjects. Most genetic studies of pathotypes have been carried out using 
158 
collections of unrelated (in time and space) isolates. Therefore, our study circumvents these 
159 
previous limitations and allowed us to observe E. coli strain relatedness in addition.   
160 
 
161 
We previously reported on risk factors for diarrhea and pathotype distribution [27] in the 
162 
EcoZUR study. Here, we report on a subset of the EcoZUR samples that comprised cases of 
163 
infected children with three major E. coli pathotypes, (DAEC, ETEC, and EPEC), and their age-
164 
matched controls (no diarrhea). Our study addressed three main objectives: 1) to describe and 
165 
compare the overall gut microbiome diversity between cases of diarrhea and controls using both 
166 
16S rRNA marker genes and whole shotgun metagenomic data; 2) to identify cases of diarrhea 
167 
where E. coli was presumably the etiological agent based on a combination of metagenomics, 
168 
isolate genome sequencing and epidemiological data; and, 3) to determine whether pathogen-
169 
specific signatures in the disease gut microbiome exist that distinguish between DAEC, EPEC 
170 
and ETEC infections.

Results 
173 
Study design and demographics of the study population. We analyzed samples collected in 
174 
the EcoZUR study, a case-control study of diarrhea carried out in four sites across a rural-urban 
175 
gradient in northern Ecuador between April 2014-September 2015. Participants were recruited 
176 
from Ecuadorian Ministry of Health hospitals and/or clinics in: Quito, Ecuador’s capital with 
177 
approximately 1.6 million inhabitants; Esmeraldas, a coastal city in the northwest of Ecuador 
178 
with ~160,000 inhabitants; Borbón, a town in Esmeraldas Province with ~7,000 inhabitants; and 
179 
rural villages located in the Borbón region, with <800 inhabitants each. Cases comprised 
180 
individuals who presented with diarrhea, defined as three or more loose stools in a 24-hour

182 
no record of diarrhea or vomiting in the previous seven days. Both cases and controls were 
183 
excluded if they reported antibiotic usage in the prior week, or if they had not lived in the study 
184 
location for at least six months. Data on demographics, medical history, water, sanitation, 
185

186

187 
Cases and controls were similar on key demographic variables (e.g., age, race), indicating that 
188 
the case-control matching was robust [27]. Sociodemographic and WASH conditions varied as 
189 
expected across the rural-urban gradient, with urban sites reporting higher levels of improved 
190 
sanitation and improved drinking water, and rural sites reporting higher proportion of individuals 
191 
receiving government assistance, and a lower proportion of individuals with fixed employment 
192 
and higher education levels. Additional information and previous results from the EcoZUR study 
193

Briefly, we found variability by site in enteropathogen prevalence, and outcomes of infection. 
195 
DAEC was the most common diarrheagenic E. coli (DEC) pathotype, and was more common, 
196 
and more associated with disease, in urban areas. EPEC and ETEC were more common in 
197 
rural areas. Travel to urban destinations was associated with higher risk of diarrhea and 
198

High frequency of co-eluting human DNA in diarrhea samples. Analysis of the composition 
216 
of metagenomic reads showed that specimens collected in this study differed in the proportional 
217 
amount of microbial and human DNA sequenced. The average percent of human reads 
218 
detected in the diarrhea group was 17.8% while in control group it was only 0.07%. Samples 
219

220

221

223

279

284

285

286

287

291 
 
292 
(1) Metagenomic abundance. We estimated the in situ metagenomic abundance of the E. coli 
293 
pathotype isolate (or the MAG that originated from the same sample as the isolate) and a 
294 
reference commensal E. coli strain HS (NC_009800.1), using the diarrhea metagenomic dataset

296

297

298

299

300

306

307

339 
Taking these five lines of evidence together, our expectation was that the putative E. coli 
340 
pathogen (isolate and/or MAG) was likely the causative agent of disease in the sample that it 
341 
was recovered from when (1) the E. coli population presented a higher estimated metagenomic 
342 
abundance compared to the commensal E. coli strain HS, (2) encoded more virulence genes 
343 
than its counterparts from control samples, including the detection of key enterotoxins and 
344 
pathotype-specific marker genes, (3) the corresponding diarrheal metagenome harbored a more 
345 
clonal population of E. coli compared to control metagenomes, (4) the isolate were assigned to 
346 
a clonal complex in the core genome phylogenetic tree that was enriched in isolates from other 
347 
disease (as opposed to control) samples and (5) we were able to recover E. coli MAGs 
348 
harboring the typical pathotype-specific virulence genes. We tested these expectations for the 
349 
three pathotype groups with the highest number of diarrhea cases in our dataset: DAEC, ETEC 
350 
and EPEC.

Diffusely Adherent E. coli (DAEC) as causative agent of diarrhea. Our results indicated that 
353 
approximately 50% of the samples from which DAEC isolates were obtained from (i.e., eight 
354

Discussion  
460 
Diagnostic testing for diarrheal pathogens has relied for decades on culture-based techniques 
461 
that do not provide a quantitative estimate of the pathogen or the response of the gut 
462 
 on October 7, 2019 at UCSF LIBRARY
http://aem.asm.org/
Downloaded from 
 
19
microbiome to the infection. Consequently, a significant fraction of diarrhea episodes remains 
463 
undiagnosed, and others are spuriously associated with infections of putative pathogens, due to 
464 
the challenges associated with the accurate detection of the etiological agent using traditional 
465 
techniques. The signatures of pathogen-specific disturbances in the ecology of the healthy gut 
466 
microbiome remain also uncharacterized. Here, we provide a novel metagenome-based 
467 
methodology that employs pathogen population in-situ abundance, level of intra-population 
468 
diversity and virulence gene content (e.g., Figure 2) to study diarrheal cases and to provide 
469 
diagnostic resolution that is usually not attainable by traditional methods.  
470 
 
471 
Application of our methodology to child diarrheal samples and age-matched controls collected in 
472 
Northern Ecuador showed that DAEC was likely the causative agent of several diarrheal cases, 
473 
and the DAEC isolates recovered from these samples were assigned to eleven distinct clonal 
474 
complexes. These complexes were apparently associated with small-scale diarrheal outbreaks 
475 
given that the great majority isolates in the complexes (>75% of total) were recovered from 
476 
disease as opposed to control samples (despite the similar number of case and control samples 
477 
and isolates in our dataset), and the subjects that provided the disease samples were from both 
478 
rural and urban settings.  
479 
 
480 
Even though DAEC is not thought to be a highly virulent E. coli pathotype [24, 34], DAEC 
481 
infections were found to be accompanied by co-elution of high amounts of human DNA and 
482 
conferred small (in terms of number of taxa affected) but significant shifts in the composition of 
483 
the gut microbiome relative to the control or infections caused by other pathotypes (e.g., ETEC) 
484 
according to our study. These findings echoed the findings by Huang et al., (2018) [16] who 
485 
applied shotgun metagenomic to stool samples collected from two geographically isolated 
486 
foodborne outbreaks in the US, where the etiologic agent was identified by culture-dependent 
487 
methods as distinct strains of Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Heidelberg. Similar to 
488 
 on October 7, 2019 at UCSF LIBRARY
http://aem.asm.org/
Downloaded from 
 
20
our study, the acute Salmonella infections described by the Huang et al. study were 
489 
accompanied by a high-frequency of co-eluting human DNA sequences, significant shifts in the 
490 
gut microbiome composition and diversity relative to healthy control samples, signatures of high-
491 
abundance of the pathogen in the metagenomic diarrheal sample and reduced intra-population 
492 
diversity. Hence, it appeared that the DAEC infections identified by our metagenomic analysis 
493 
were likely caused by a DAEC strain.  
494 
 
495 
While the number of samples analyzed here was limited, and thus, the compositional shifts 
496 
identified should be clearly considered as preliminary results only, our results do indicate that 
497 
there may be consistent signatures in the gut microbiome that could provide reliable additional 
498 
diagnostic features to distinguish different etiologic agents of diarrhea. The negative correlation 
499 
in abundance between E. coli and P. copri was a notable such signature that should be 
500 
explored in future work in order to elucidate the underlying mechanism(s) for the observed anti-
501 
correlation pattern.  Of particular interest also was the observation that, in general, ETEC 
502 
samples presented lower average metagenomic abundance of the pathogen (5.1%) than DAEC 
503 
samples (27.8%) by ~5 fold, on average, for the cases with clear evidence of E. coli pathotype 
504 
infection. This observation suggests that ETEC infection may require a lower pathogen load in 
505 
order to elicit disease than DAEC, providing potentially an additional diagnostic metagenomic 
506 
feature of ETEC versus DAEC infections. Consistent with the latter results, ETEC pathogens 
507 
are thought to cause infection by the production of enterotoxins [22, 35] that alter the 
508 
concentration of important cellular messengers such as cyclic AMP, cyclic GMP and Ca2+, while 
509 
DAEC is strongly attached to the cell surface, where it induces a cytopathic effect characterized 
510 
by the development of long cellular extensions [24]. These differences in mechanisms of 
511 
pathogenicity might explain, at least in part, the differences in pathogen abundance and 
512 
associated changes in the gut microbiome (e.g., Figure 5) that were observed in ETEC- and 
513 
DAEC-dominated metagenomes.

A critical question in diagnostic testing for enteric pathogens is how often conventional culture-
515 
dependent techniques, such as those implemented for isolating E. coli from stool samples, 
516 
readily capture the targeted pathogen as opposed to transient or dormant/inactive populations. 
517 
Our metagenomic and/or epidemiological data showed that from the total set of diarrheal DAEC 
518 
(n=16) samples analyzed, the isolate was highly similar to the recovered MAG in only ~30% of 
519 
the cases (n=5). The same results for ETEC (+) and EPEC (+) samples (by isolation and PCR) 
520 
where 70% and 0%, for a total average of roughly about 50%. Thus, it appeared that in about 
521 
half of the diarrheal samples, the isolate likely was not the causative agent. This may help 
522 
explain results of many studies that have observed high rates of enteropathogen infection in 
523 
individuals without diarrhea symptoms [36].  
524 
 
525 
Beyond pathogen detection, our metagenomic study also generated complete or nearly 
526 
complete pathogen MAGs directly from the samples, which allowed a more comprehensive 
527 
characterization of the virulence and diversity of the infectious E. coli population. Besides 
528 
application in diagnostic testing, population genome binning can also be used to recover 
529 
pathogenic genotypes and, when coupled to epidemiological information, to identify person-to-
530 
person transmission events and outbreak dynamics, which represent important tasks in public 
531 
health investigations.  
532 
 
533 
Although a small number of samples were excluded from the pathogen-specific signatures of 
534 
the infection based on relatively low coverage observed in comparison with other positive 
535 
samples or with controls, it is important to highlight that finding a 0.05-0.1X coverage, i.e., the 
536 
minimum threshold required for robust detection of a target genome in a complex gut 
537 
metagenome [37], still translates to a relative large number of cells in situ. The average E. coli 
538 
genome size is 5Mbp and our metagenome libraries were, on average, ~1.78Gbp in size after 
539 
removing contaminant host DNA and low quality reads. Finding 0.05X coverage for an E. coli 
540

http://aem.asm.org/
Downloaded from

541 
or ~0.014% of the total metagenome size. Our extracted DNA came from an average of 
542 
1.26x106 cells in total based on the normalization of the average extracted DNA concentrations 
543 
(6.45 ng/µl) with the estimated molecular weight of an E. coli bacterium 5.14x10-6 ng [38-40]. 
544 
Therefore, 0.014% of 1.26x106 would be 1.77x102 in total (i.e. >100 cells), which is still a large 
545

546

547

552

553

554

556

557

558

was determined by the number of reads recruited by the VF genes (≥1X) and the length of the 
684

685 
gene absence). E. coli intra-population diversity was estimated by calculating the average 
686 
nucleotide identity of the metagenomic reads (defined as ANIr) mapping to the reference 
687 
genome (pathotype isolate or reference commensal) with percent identity between 90-100%, 
688 
i.e., reads representing the total E. coli population in a sample, based on competitive mapping.

690 
used as described in (https://github.com/lmrodriguezr/enveomics/tree/master/enveomics.R).
