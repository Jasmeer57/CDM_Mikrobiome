Abstract
Background: Very-low-birth-weight (VLBW; born weighing <1500 g) infant feeding with mother’s own milk (mother’s
milk) is associated with numerous beneficial health outcomes. Several interventions, including the prophylactic use of
probiotics, are being adopted to promote a gastrointestinal microbiota favorable to the gut health of VLBW infants. An
improved understanding of the microbiota that results from mother’s milk feeding would therefore facilitate progress in
this field.
Objective: A preplanned primary objective of this research was to characterize the development of the gut microbiota
in exclusively mother’s milk–fed VLBW infants and describe the reference taxonomic profile that results from mother’s
milk feeding.
Methods: In this prospective longitudinal cohort study, we collected weekly stool samples from exclusively mother’s
milk–fed VLBW infants admitted to Mount Sinai Hospital and profiled their gastrointestinal microbiota development from
birth (primary outcome of stool collection). In total, we profiled 231 stools from 54 exclusively mother’s milk–fed VLBW
infants with the use of V6-16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing.
Results: Bacterial evenness, but not bacterial richness, increased over time in VLBW infants (P < 0.001). Bifidobacterium
relative abundances were consistently low in all microbiotas at all time points (<0.5% in 97% of samples). VLBW infant
microbiotas did not cluster by birth mode, gestational age, or weeks after birth and instead clustered as a function of
patient identity (R2 = 0.51, P < 0.001).
Conclusions: Exclusively mother’s milk–fed VLBW infants rapidly develop personalized gut microbiotas that show in-
creasing evenness and are seemingly unaffected by birth mode or gestational age at birth. The benefits from mother’s
milk feeding are likely modulated through microbes or pathways that are not dependent on Bifidobacterium because
these microbes are present at low levels in VLBW infants. These results help define a reference VLBW infant microbiota
profile derived from mother’s milk, the optimal source of nutrition for these infants. This trial was registered at ISRCTN
(http://www.isrctn.com/) as ISRCTN35317141.

Introduction
Among very-low-birth-weight (VLBW; born weighing <1500 g)
infants, human-milk feeding is associated with improved feed-
ing tolerance and a reduction in severe infections, chronic lung
disease, and severe bowel disease, such as necrotizing ente-
rocolitis (NEC) (1–3). Many mechanisms are thought to be
responsible for these associations, including differences in bacte-
rial colonization patterns in the gastrointestinal tract of breast-
fed compared with formula-fed infants. More than a century
ago, Tissier (4) reported that breastfed infants, unlike formula-
fed infants, had a microbiota dominated by Bifidobacteria
species and that diarrhea caused by an imbalance of intestinal

There are many reasons why there may be different colo-
nization patterns between mother’s own milk (mother’s milk)
and formula-fed infants. For example, mother’s milk con-
tains its own rich microbiome and is also an abundant
source of indigestible human-milk oligosaccharides, which to-
gether serve as a natural source of pro- and prebiotics to
inoculate and stimulate the growth of beneficial bacteria
(12, 13).
The gastrointestinal tract of VLBW infants is frequently per-
turbed by factors associated with preterm birth, including ce-
sarean delivery, routine antibiotic use, living in a hospital envi-
ronment with an enriched pathogen load, and lack of exposure
to mother’s skin and breast milk (14). Several strategies have
been identified to improve feeding tolerance and gut health of
VLBW infants, with the biological premise that their mecha-
nism of action is an alteration of the microbial composition of
the gastrointestinal tract. Interventions include the use of probi-
otics, prebiotics, skin-to-skin care, and the use of fresh instead
of frozen mother’s and donor human milk (3, 15–18). Although
the reported impact of some of these strategies on health out-
comes is impressive, implementation into practice has been slow,
in part because of a limited understanding of their short- and
long-term effect on the infant gut microbiome. A more thor-
ough understanding of the microbiome of VLBW infants fed
mother’s milk will facilitate progress in this field. The aim of the
current study, therefore, was to characterize the development of
the gut microbiota from serially collected stool samples from
exclusively mother’s milk–fed infants.
Methods
Study participants and specimen collection. In this prospec-
tive longitudinal cohort study, VLBW infants were enrolled between
February 2012 and April 2014 from Mount Sinai Hospital, a large ter-
tiary care neonatal unit located in Toronto, Ontario, Canada. Infants
were eligible for inclusion if they were born weighing <1500 g, enteral
feedings were likely to commence in the first postnatal week, and writ-
ten informed consent was secured from a guardian within 96 h of birth.
At study initiation, infants were concurrently enrolled in a double-blind
randomized clinical trial (ISRCTN35317141) and received by random
assignment either pasteurized donor human milk or preterm formula as
a supplement when mother’s milk was unavailable (19). Mother’s milk
was always provided first, and nutrient fortification was achieved with
powdered, intact, bovine-based multinutrient fortifiers [Similac Human

Only exclusively mother’s milk–fed infants were included in the
present study. Briefly, thawed mother’s milk was provided by feeding
tube as soon as available (typically ≤3 d after birth) and advanced
10–25 mL  kg−1  d−1 by using weight-based criteria up to 160 mL
 kg−1  d−1. Nutrient fortification of mother’s milk commenced at
≥120 mL  kg−1  d−1; first at 22 kcal/ounce (0.74 kcal/mL) and then
at 24 kcal/ounce (0.81 kcal/mL). Feedings were further concentrated if
growth was suboptimal (<15 g  kg−1  d−1) with the use of formula
powder and nutrient modular. Once an infant received a supplement,
future stool samples were excluded from the present study. Mothers
pumped their milk at home or at the hospital where double electric
breast pumps were available. Milk was stored at −20°C and used to
feed infants in order of collection (e.g., first in the freezer and first out).
Human Research Ethics Boards at Mount Sinai Hospital and The Hos-
pital for Sick Children approved the study protocol.
Baseline demographic data on study participants, including their
birth weight, size for gestational age at birth (e.g., small for gestational
age or appropriate for gestational age), 5-min Apgar score, and mode
of delivery were extracted from the medical record. Maternal age, edu-
cation, and ethnicity were determined by a member of the study team
who interviewed each mother with the use of a standardized question-
naire. At enrollment and each Monday for 7 wk thereafter or transfer
to a unit other than The Hospital for Sick Children, stool collection kits
were posted at each infant’s bedside for weekly stool collection. With
the use of a sterile spoon provided with the kit, nurses collected a pea-
sized stool sample from the infant’s diaper and placed it in the provided
container, which was then stored in a −20°C freezer. Frozen stool sam-
ples were then transported to The Hospital for Sick Children daily and
stored at −80°C until transported on dry ice to the University of Ottawa
for DNA extraction and sequencing library construction.
Information on what type of enteral feeding (e.g., mother’s milk,
donor milk, or preterm formula) each infant was provided each day and
whether or not human milk was nutrient enriched during the study was
prospectively collected from records maintained in the milk-preparation
rooms and in each child’s medical record. Similarly, the names of all
antibiotics used daily were collected. At the time of this study, probiotics
were not used at either participating hospital for VLBW infants during
initial hospitalization, and skin-to-skin care was routine.
DNA extraction and 16S ribosomal RNA gene library con-
struction and sequencing. Metagenomic DNA was extracted
from infant stools by using the FastDNA Spin Kit (catalog no.
116540600; MP Biomedicals) according to the manufacturer’s proto-
col. Briefly, samples were taken directly from −80°C and allowed to
thaw on ice and between 2 and 300 mg stool was resuspended in 1 mL
cell lysing solution TC. Resuspended stools were subjected to 2 rounds
of mechanical lysis (40 s at speed 6.0 m/s) in a FastPrep24 homogenizer
(MP Biomedicals), the columns were washed as detailed in the Spin Kit
protocol, and DNA was eluted in water.
V6 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene library construction was per-
formed by using 2 successive rounds of PCR as described previously
(21). Briefly, the extracted metagenomic DNA was subjected to 2 PCR
reactions to first amplify the V6 16S rRNA gene hypervariable region
and to subsequently introduce Illumina HiSeq sequencing adapter se-
quences (Supplemental Table 1). Final PCR products were purified,
and equimolar combinations of each sample were combined for each
sequencing library. Sequencing libraries were sent to the Center for

To confirm that microbial composition findings, and in particular
the potential detection of Bifidobacterium spp., were not unduly influ-
enced by primer bias, a subset of the samples were also subjected to mi-
crobiota profiling targeting the V4 hypervariable region, as previously
described (21) with minor modifications described below. The V4-16S
rRNA gene amplicons were first visualized on an agarose gel and quan-
tified, and equal masses of the barcoded libraries were pooled together
for subsequent PCR purification and size selection by using Agencourt
AMPure XP DNA beads to remove primer dimers and PCR artifacts.
Final libraries were analyzed by using Agilent Bioanalyzer. The libraries
were templated and sequenced by using an in-house Life Technologies
Ion Chef/Ion Torrent Personal Genome Machine on a 314 chip accord-
ing to the manufacturer’s protocol.
Processing and analysis of reads and determination of
microbial composition. The raw paired-end V6-16S rRNA gene
amplicons were processed as we previously described (21). Briefly, the
paired-end reads were merged into a single sequence to reconstruct the
V6-16S rRNA region with the use of FLASh (22) and low-quality reads
were removed (reads were required to have a minimum quality score
of 20 across 90% of the bases) and demultiplexed based on the bar-
codes embedded within the sequence with the use of FastqMultX (23).
These sequences have been deposited in the National Center for Biotech-
nology Information Sequence Read Archive archive under the accession
number PRJNA328987 (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra). The V6-16S
amplicons were converted from fastq to fasta format and inputted into
Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology (QIIME version 1.8.0)
(24) for clustering into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at the 97%
level by using a closed reference OTU picking strategy against the Green-
genes database (version 13.5) using the default settings. OTUs that were
only present 1–2 times (singletons or doubletons) were removed for
each individual sequencing library, and samples with <100,000 reads
remaining after filtering were removed from the analysis.
The raw V4 sequencing reads were assigned to OTUs as previously
described (21). Briefly, the unaligned BAM file generated by the Ion Tor-
rent Personal Genome Machine was downloaded, converted to fastq
format, and reads <140 or >200 bp were discarded from further anal-
ysis with the use of Bioawk (25). Potential chimeras (PCR amplification
artifacts) were identified and removed with UCHIME (16) by using the
default settings against the Greengenes database (version 13.5) at 97%
sequence identity. The reads were then converted from fastq to fasta and
fed to QIIME (version 1.8.0) for clustering OTUs at the 97% level by
using a closed reference OTU picking strategy against the Greengenes
database (version 13.5) with the use of the default settings. OTUs that
were only present 1–2 times (singleton or doubletons) were removed and
samples with <1000 reads after filtering were removed from the analy-
sis. The demultiplexed and filtered reads were deposited to the National
Center for Biotechnology Information Sequence Read Archive database
under the accession number PRJNA328987.

participant baseline demographic and clinical data were computed. For
the microbiota compositional analysis, the resulting V6-16S OTU table

tween α diversities and clinical metadata were assessed by using linear
mixed-effects models (lme4) (28) and by using patient as a random-
effect term. Significant differences between the models were assessed by
using the ANOVA function in R. β Diversities and principal coordinate
analysis (PCoA) were also determined in phyloseq, and significant dif-
ferences based on clinical metadata were assessed by using the adonis

Adonis estimates the amount of variation explained by each clinical
data grouping [e.g., mode of delivery (vaginal or cesarean delivery) or
gestational age at birth (<27 or ≥27 wk of gestation)] and whether these
groupings explain why different samples are more or less similar to each
other with regard to their microbial composition. In contrast, anosim re-
ports an estimate of whether samples show group-specific clustering of
microbial composition, with “0” indicating completely random group-
ing and values approaching 1 indicating nonrandom group-specific clus-
tering. Patients with >4 microbiota samples were used to calculate the
median intrapatient and interpatient β diversity distance, and signifi-
cance was determined by using a Mann-Whitney test.
The relative abundances of bacterial taxa at different levels of tax-
onomy (i.e., phylum, class, etc.) were calculated by using phyloseq, and
corresponding locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOWESS) re-
gressions were plotted by using ggplot2 (30). We also performed a qual-
itative analysis of the microbiota in infants with low or high antibiotic
exposure. For the purposes of this analysis, we considered patients in
the bottom 15% of total antibiotic use to be “low exposure,” whereas
those in the top 15% of total antibiotic use were considered “high ex-
posure.” The V4-16S OTU table was also inputted into R, and the rela-
tive abundance of the bacterial taxa determined by using phyloseq. The
taxa relative abundances were summarized at the class level for both V4
and V6 microbiota profiling results and compared to ensure that the re-
sults were similar with the use of both microbiota profiling approaches.
For all statistical analyses, P values <0.05 were considered significant
and, where appropriate, P values were corrected for multiple hypothe-
sis testing with the use of the Benjamin-Hochberg approach with a false
discovery rate cutoff of <0.05.

A total of 231 stool samples were available from the ex-
clusively mother’s milk–fed infants. Within this cohort of pa-
tients, 1 infant died, 3 developed a confirmed case of NEC
(stage 2), and 6 infants had a laboratory-confirmed case of
sepsis during the sampling period. In total, 14 infants had a
laboratory-confirmed case of sepsis after stool collection was
completed. We were able to sample a median of 20 stool sam-
ples/wk in the study, with the sample number naturally de-
creasing as infants became ineligible for the study (i.e., supple-
ment to mother’s milk required, hospital transfer, or discharge)
(Supplemental Figure 1).
Overall, the bacterial richness (i.e., total number of OTUs)
in the VLBW infant microbiota remained relatively constant
over the first 3 mo after birth (Figure 1A), although individual
infants showed great variation in their bacterial richness over
time (Supplemental Figure 2). In contrast, there was an increase
in bacterial evenness (how equally abundant each OTU is rela-
tive to others) over time (days-after-birth β of 0.065, P < 0.001)
(Figure 2B). There was a trend for infants born at <27 wk of
gestation to gain less bacterial evenness over time than infants
born at ≥27 wk of gestation (P = 0.09). There was no apparent
difference in VLBW infants born by cesarean compared with
vaginal delivery (P = 0.31) (Supplemental Figure 3). As in the
bacterial richness results, individual infants showed great varia-
tion in their levels of bacterial evenness over time (Supplemental
Figure 4).
To further explore clustering of microbial composition by
birth mode, gestational age at birth, and postnatal age, the β
diversity of the VLBW infants was subsequently assessed by
PCoA by using the Bray-Curtis index of dissimilarity. There
was no obvious separation by birth mode and gestational age
(Figure 2A). Instead, samples from the same infant clustered
together on the PCoA plot (Figure 2B). Furthermore, ANOVA
via Adonis confirmed this visual observation, with the patient
identity explaining the majority of the variation (R2 = 0.51,
P < 0.001) with birth mode, gestational age, and postnatal age
making minor, but significant, contributions in explaining sam-
ple clustering (Table 2). In support of this finding, microbiota
compositions from samples originating from the same patient
were significantly more similar to each other compared with
samples obtained from other patients (Figure 3). In addition, the
analysis of similarity approach implemented in Anosim iden-
tified differences between samples on the basis of patient ID,
but not their clinical data (Supplemental Table 2). Similar re-
sults were also found by using the weighted and unweighted
Unifrac distances that take the phylogenetic taxonomy of the
identified OTUs into account (Table 2, Supplemental Table 2,
Supplemental Figure 5).
Analysis of the different taxa that made up the VLBW mi-
crobiota showed 3 bacterial classes (Bacilli, Clostridia, and
Gammaproteobacteria), which comprised >90% of the bac-
terial relative abundance across all time points. Each of these
classes had a different profile in relative abundance over time
as evidenced by their LOWESS regressions (Figure 4A). Bacilli
showed a “bump” in their LOWESS regressions at early time
points, peaking at approximately the 21st day postnatally be-
fore receding to basal levels. In contrast, Clostridia relative
abundances were initially low in VLBW infant microbiotas
and showed a steady increase in their relative abundance over
time. It should be noted that we did not identify any OTUs
that matched to Clostridium difficile in this study. Gammapro-
teobacteria remained at fairly high levels throughout the first
90 d in VLBW infant microbiotas and did not appear to be al-
tered in relative abundance over time.
We conducted a subanalysis to ascertain the impact of nutri-
ent fortification of mother’s milk with a bovine protein–based
fortifier on microbiota profiles (Supplemental Figure 6). There
were no clear changes in microbiota composition upon com-
mencing nutrient fortification of mother’s milk.
Intriguingly, the “bump” in Bacilli relative abundance at
early time points is primarily due to increased levels of Bacilli
in infants born via cesarean delivery, with vaginally born in-
fants showing no trend for increased Bacilli during the first
3 wk after birth (Figure 4B). It should be noted that the Bacilli
class was dominated by taxa typically associated with skin mi-
crobiotas. Specifically Staphylococcaceae and Streptococcaceae
families represented >65% of all Bacilli reads, whereas Lacto-
bacillaceae were almost completely absent (<0.1% of all Bacilli

Discussion
Although the predominant taxa changed in the gastrointesti-
nal microbiotas of mother’s milk–fed VLBW infants in the
present study over hospitalization, they did not show an over-
all trend toward increasing species richness as time progressed
(Figure 1A). This result suggests that the gut environment in
these infants is in a constant state of flux and as new bacte-
rial taxa colonize the VLBW infant gut, other bacterial taxa
are being lost. However, we note that individual VLBW in-
fants showed striking changes in their species richness profile
over time (Supplemental Figure 2). One factor that could ex-
plain these shifts would be the use of different antibiotics for
each infant. A recent study showed that antibiotics have a dra-
matic impact on species richness in VLBW infants (32). Inter-
estingly, this latter study reported that particular antibiotics
decreased species richness, whereas others could result in ei-
ther increased or decreased species richness and also found that
breast-milk feeding was associated with increased species rich-
ness (32). Given that all of the infants in the present study
were exclusively mother’s milk fed, this suggests that breast milk
serves as an important factor in allowing new taxa to properly
colonize the gastrointestinal tract of VLBW infants. In contrast
with species richness, VLBW infant microbiotas showed a sig-
nificant increase in evenness over time (Figure 1B), a hallmark
of normal gut microbiota development (33). Increasing species
evenness has also been found to be characteristic of mother’s
milk–fed VLBW infant microbiotas in another recent study (34).
As in the species richness results, individual VLBW infants have
unique species evenness profiles (Supplemental Figure 4).
Birth mode, gestational age, or weeks after birth made mi-
nor contributions to the variations observed among VLBW in-
fant microbiotas (Table 2, Figure 2). Instead, the major factor
in VLBW microbiota clustering was patient identity, suggest-
ing that VLBW infants have highly personalized microbiotas,
as reported by others (32, 35). There are several factors that
could lead to personalized microbiotas in VLBW infants, such
as genetics (36) and repeated antibiotic exposures (37). Another
factor that would result in personalized microbiotas would be
feeding regime. At first glance, it would be easy to dismiss feed-
ing as an important factor leading to personalized microbiotas,
given that all the infants in this study were exclusively mother’s
milk fed. However, the composition of human milk is variable
between women (13), with one of the major variations being the
expression and presence of human-milk oligosaccharides (total
content and specific form). These oligosaccharides cannot be di-
gested by the infants themselves and instead perform a variety
of different functions (13). In particular, human-milk oligosac-
charides are believed to promote the growth of specific bacteria
(13). Because bacteria vary in their innate ability to digest spe-
cific human-milk oligosaccharides (38, 39), this would naturally
lead to the milk from different mothers promoting a unique mi-
crobiota in their breastfed infants.
Whether a full-term infant is born vaginally or via cesarean
delivery has been shown in some studies to have an impact on
the composition and development of the human gut microbiota
(40). In contrast, and in agreement with previous reports (32,
41–43), we found that VLBW microbiotas do not vary signifi-
cantly between those born vaginally and those born by cesarean
delivery. Indeed, the main difference by mode of delivery ap-
pears to be a transient colonization of cesarean-delivered in-
fants with Bacilli taxa during the first several weeks after birth
(Figure 4B). It should be noted that others have found that mode
of delivery affects VLBW microbiota profiles when analyzing
hundreds of samples from multicenter studies (44, 45). One of
the main differences identified in these studies was enrichment
in Bacilli taxa in VLBW infants born via cesarean delivery, as
reflected in our results (44). Overall, it is likely that the micro-
biota differences imparted by birth mode among VLBW infants
are subtle in the short term and difficult to distinguish from the
variety of other variables that would affect microbiota devel-
opment in children born very preterm. Moreover, with regard
to our specific cohort, it is also possible that exclusive mother’s
milk feeding would minimize the differences observed between
VLBW infants with different birth modes, as others have re-
cently suggested (34).
VLBW infants are typically exposed to a variety of different
antibiotics and often receive multiple courses of antibiotic treat-
ment. Early exposure to antibiotics has been associated with in-
creased risk of several diseases, such as obesity and metabolic
syndrome (14, 33, 46–49). We thus performed a qualitative
analysis with the use of 4 infants who represented the high-
est and lowest antibiotic-exposed patients in our cohort for
which we had multiple samples across different time points.
The differences between the high- and low-antibiotic–exposed
infants are striking (Figure 5). Low-antibiotic–exposed infants
display a tendency toward increasing Clostridia as they age,
whereas high antibiotic exposure appears to favor high levels of
Gammaproteobacteria. Interestingly, increases in Gammapro-
teobacteria and repeated antibiotic exposure have been shown
to be associated with increased NEC risk (44, 50, 51). It should
be noted that heavy antibiotic use also correlates with periods
in which infants were not feeding orally, and thus not receiving
mother’s milk. Part of the differences observed between these in-
fants may therefore be attributable to a lack of an energy source
(e.g., human-milk oligosaccharides) and nutrients for the devel-
oping gut microbiota.
Bifidobacterium species are seen as key microbes involved in
colonizing the gastrointestinal tract of healthy full-term infants
and have been reported to be the primary microbial beneficia-
ries of breast milk (13, 33). In contrast, we were unable to de-
tect significant levels of Bifidobacterium spp. in the majority of
microbiotas profiled in this study, despite these infants being ex-
clusively mother’s milk fed. To ensure that our use of the V6-16S
rRNA gene-sequencing primers region did not obscure the pres-
ence of Bifidobacterium spp. in our samples, we profiled a ran-
dom subset of our samples (Supplemental Figure 7) with the use
of V4-16S rRNA gene primers that have been shown to amplify
these taxa (31). These results show that the lack of Bifidobac-
terium spp. in our samples is not due to a primer bias and instead
suggests a true absence of Bifidobacterium spp. in VLBW infant
gut microbiotas. These findings echo the results from other stud-
ies that have profiled VLBW gut microbiotas and reported very
low levels of Bifidobacterium spp. in these infants (32, 35, 41,
42, 44, 45, 52–54). These aforementioned studies used differ-
ent primer sets that target different 16S hypervariable regions,
such as the V1–V3 (42), V3–V5 (44, 45), V4 (32, 41), V4–
V5 (34), V4–V6 (52), or V6 (53, 54) regions or used shotgun
metagenomic sequencing (35). In addition, qPCR was used in
several of these studies to confirm the lack of Bifidobacterium
in the samples (41, 42, 44). It is therefore increasingly apparent
that Bifidobacterium is rarely present in VLBW infant microbio-
tas, even when these infants are exclusively fed mother’s milk.
Thus, the benefits that VLBW infants receive when fed mother’s
milk are likely to be modulated through microbes or path-
ways that are not dependent on the presence of Bifidobacterium
spp.

Although the microbiota of individual VLBW infants fed
mother’s milk in the present study is highly personalized
(Figures 2 and 3), there is an overall trend for increas-
ing Clostridia taxa over time, with little apparent change in
Gammaproteobacteria (Figure 4). Our findings are consistent
with the recent report of Gregory et al. (34), who noted an in-
crease in Clostridiales and a trend for decreased Enterobacte-
riales over time in VLBW infants; these are orders within the
Clostridia and Gammaproteobacteria classes, respectively. Un-
like in our study, Gregory et al. did not include infants who
received antibiotics within 7 d of stool collection in their anal-
ysis. As previously discussed, we noted that infants with high
amounts of antibiotic use showed decreases in Clostridia and in-
creases in Gammaproteobacteria. Together, these 2 studies sug-
gest that increases in Clostridia (and, by extension, Clostridiales
as well) are characteristic of exclusively breastfed infants but
can be disrupted by antibiotic treatment.
In conclusion, these results define a reference VLBW in-
fant taxonomic profile derived from mother’s milk, the optimal
source of nutrition for these infants. In particular, our results
suggest that high levels of Bifidobacterium are not required for
the benefits that VLBW infants derive from mother’s milk. This
reference profile will be useful in assessing the impact of alter-
nate strategies to improve the health of VLBW infants mediated
through the microbial composition of the gastrointestinal tract,
such as use of probiotics, prebiotics, and the use of fresh instead
of frozen mother’s milk and donor human milk.
