Growth-promoting effect of alginate on Faecalibacterium prausnitzii through

A B S T R A C T   
Faecalibacterium prausnitzii is a commensal gut bacterium that is thought to provide protection against inflam­
matory diseases. However, this bacterium is extremely oxygen sensitive, which limits its industrial application as 
a probiotic. The use of prebiotics to increase the abundance of this bacterium in the gut is an alternative strategy 
to achieve its possible health-promoting effect. We evaluated nine substances as candidate prebiotics for 
F. prausnitzii using a pH-controlled single-batch fermenter as a human gut microbiota model. Of them, alginate 
markedly increased the relative abundance of F. prausnitzii, as determined by the significant increase in the 
number of 16S rRNA sequences corresponding to this bacterial taxon in the fecal fermentation samples detected 
by real-time PCR. However, F. prausnitzii strains were incapable of utilizing alginate in monoculture, implying 
that an interaction with another gut microbe was required. There was a positive correlation between the relative 
abundance of F. prausnitzii and that of Bacteroides when cultured in medium containing alginate as the sole 
carbon source, indicative of cross-feeding between these bacteria. Interestingly, the ratio of acetic acid, a known 
substrate for F. prausnitzii, produced by Bacteroides was significantly higher in the alginate-containing medium 
than in media containing other prebiotic candidates. Bacterially degraded alginate oligosaccharides (AOS) 
remained in the medium after Bacteroides monoculture, and an isolate of F. prausnitzii was able to utilize a portion 
of them. Genomic sequencing revealed that the strain that consumed the AOS contained an ATP-binding cassette 
transporter, an alginate lyase, and AlgQ1/2 homologs encoding solute-binding proteins. Furthermore, in real- 
time PCR analyses, AlgQ1/2 homologs were detected in fecal samples collected from 309 of 452 (68.4%) Jap­
anese subjects. Thus, the products of alginate assimilation by Bacteroides may promote the growth of 
F. prausnitzii.   
1. Introduction 
Gut microbiota exert various effects on host health and disease states 
through their metabolites. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are some of 
the most abundant metabolites in the gut and are key mediators of 
beneficial effects elicited by gut microbiota on gut barrier functions, 
glucose homeostasis, immunomodulation, and obesity (Chambers, 
Preston, Frost, & Morrison, 2018). Butyrate is the main SCFA in the 
colon, and it provides approximately 70% of the energy used by colo­
nocytes, which play critical roles in colon homeostasis (Li et al., 2018; 
Martin-Gallausiaux, Marinelli, Blotti`ere, Larraufie, & Lapaque, 2020) 
One of the dominant butyrate-producing bacteria in the healthy 
human gut is Faecalibacterium prausnitzii. This Gram-negative amor­
phous bacterium accounts for about 5% of the total bacteria in an adult 
human colon (Frost et al., 2021; Martín, Bermúdez-Humar´an, & 
Langella, 2018). The depletion of F. prausnitzii has been reported in 
patients with bowel disease, including those with inflammatory bowel 
disease, Crohn’s disease, and colorectal cancer (Leylabadlo et al., 2020; 
Touchefeu et al., 2021). Therefore, this species has a potentially 
important role in promoting gut health (Lopez-Siles, Duncan, Garcia-Gil, 
& Martinez-Medina, 2017). Indeed, some beneficial effects have been 
suggested by both in vitro and in vivo assays. Experiments using an in vitro 
device that is capable of co-culturing intestinal epithelium and anaer­
obic bacteria showed that F. prausnitzii has an anti-inflammatory effect 
on colon epithelial cells (Zhang et al., 2021). Alameddine et al. (2019) 
reported that F. prausnitzii skewed human dendritic cells to form IL-10- 
producing T-cells, resulting in increased expression of IL-10 and IL-27 
and decreased expression of IL-6 and TNFα. Several studies using ani­
mal models also showed that that F. prausnitzii treatments can upregu­
late regulatory T cells and anti-inflammatory cytokines (Geuking,

K¨oller, Rupp, & McCoy, 2014; Qiu, Zhang, & Yang, 2013) and reduce 
indicators of inflammation, such as gut permeability, colonic serotonin, 
and cytokines (Martín et al., 2015; Xu et al., 2020), leading to improved 
hepatic health and reduced adipose tissue inflammation (Munukka 
et al., 2017). In a clinical study, Estevinho et al. (2020) detected an 
increased abundance of Clostridium, including F. prausnitzii, in the feces 
of patients with inflammatory bowel disease who responded to treat­
ment with biopharmaceuticals. Overall, F. prausnitzii has gained the 
status of a next-generation probiotic with new health-promoting effect 
(s) compared with traditional probiotics such as lactic acid bacteria and 
Bifidobacterium (Langella, Guarner, & Martín, 2019; Lin et al., 2019). 
However, because F. prausnitzii is extremely oxygen sensitive (Khan 
et al., 2012), it is difficult to culture on an industrial scale. In addition, it 
is unknown whether it is safe to introduce F. prausnitzii to the gut via 
consumption, even though it is a dominant species of gut microbiota. 
Because it is a Gram-negative bacterium, its supplementation by an 
exogenous route, whether in the form of a biotherapeutic product or 
functional food, must be carefully evaluated from a viewpoint of safety. 
For these reasons it has been difficult to apply in the food industry. As an 
alternative, the use of prebiotics, which have been defined as “a sub­
strate that is selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring a 
health benefit” (Gibson et al., 2017), is an attractive strategy to increase 
the abundance of this bacterium in the gut to achieve tangible health 
benefits. Some previous studies have evaluated the ability of a range of 
compounds to promote the growth of F. prausnitzii. In in vitro mono­
cultures, F. prausnitzii grew well in a medium supplemented with inulin 
(Duncan et al., 2014). Cherbuy et al. (2019) found that administration of 
resistant starch increased the abundance of Faecalibacterium in mice 
harboring human microbiota. In a clinical study, the oral administration 
of kestose was shown to increase the F. prausnitzii count in 0- to 1-year-­
old infants but not in older subjects (Koga et al., 2016). Because of the 
limitations of the human microbiota-associated mice model (Park & Im, 
2020), the effects of these ingredients on adult gut microbiota remain 
controversial. The aim of this study was to identify an appropriate 
prebiotic for F. prausnitzii in the adult human gut. We compared the 
following nine prebiotic candidates: alginate (polysaccharide extracted 
from brown alga); acacia gum (water-soluble dietary fiber extracted 
from acacia trees); resistant corn starch (type II resistant starch derived 
from corn); indigestible dextrin (indigestible glucose polymer obtained 
by hydrolyzing starch); isomaltodextrin (indigestible glucose polymer 
obtained by enzymatically decomposing starch); inulin (a fructan pro­
duced by an enzymatic method); lactulose (isomerized lactose); guar 
gum decomposition products (PHGG, dietary fiber obtained by decom­
posing guar beans (Cyamopsis tetragonoloba); and 2′-fucosyl lactose (a 
type of human milk oligosaccharide). The effects of these substrates on 
fecal microbiota were determined using a pH-controlled fermenter as a 
human gut microbiota model (Takagi et al., 2016). Then, the activity as 
well as the underlying mechanisms of alginate as a compound to pro­
mote the growth of F. prausnitzii were investigated. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Fecal samples 
Fecal samples were collected from seven healthy Japanese volun­
teers without bowel disease or recent antibiotic treatment (within the 
last 3 months). The volunteers were five men and two women, desig­
nated as subjects A to G, and were between 28 and 45 years of age. 
Samples from subject F whose cell number of F. prausnitzii was under the 
detection limit (106 per wet feces) were removed from the study. Written 
informed consent was obtained from all the subjects. The fresh fecal 
samples were transferred by the subjects into tubes, and then immedi­
ately enclosed in plastic bags containing AnaeroPouches (Mitsubishi Gas 
Chemical, Tokyo, Japan) to create an anaerobic environment. The bags 
were stored at 4 ◦C for<3 h. The fecal samples were diluted 10 × with 
saline, vortexed, and stored at −
80 ◦C until the day of analysis. 
2.2. Fecal fermentation 
Fecal samples were cultured using a pH-controlled multi-channel jar 
fermenter (Bio Jr.8; ABLE, Tokyo, Japan) (Takagi et al., 2016). Each 
vessel, containing 100 mL yeast extract, casitone, and fatty acid (YCFA) 
medium (Supplementary Table 1) supplemented with 1% (w/v) of a 
carbon source, was maintained at 37 ◦C at pH > 6.0 with constant 
stirring at 80 rpm under anaerobic conditions (100% CO2) during 
fermentation. Each vessel was inoculated with 100 μL of the 10 ×
-diluted fecal suspension to initiate fermentation, the pH was adjusted to 
pH 6.5 ± 0.2, and then the culture was fermented for 24 h. The sup­
plemental carbon sources were alginate (ULV-L3; KMICA Corp., Tokyo, 
Japan), acacia gum (Nexria, Rouen, France), resistant corn starch 
(HIMAIZE; Ingredion Incorporated, Westchester, IL, USA), indigestible 
dextrin (Pinefiber W; Matsutani Chemical Industry Co., Ltd, Hyogo, 
Japan), isomaltodextrin (Fibryxa; HAYASHIBARA Co., Ltd., Okayama, 
Japan), inulin (Fuji FF; Fuji Nihon Seito Corp., Tokyo, Japan), lactulose 
(MLC-97; Morinaga Milk Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), PHGG 
(Sunfiber R, Taiyo Kagaku, Japan), and 2′-fucocyl lactose (Funakoshi 
Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Samples of each culture were collected at 0 and 
24 h after fecal inoculation. Fecal fermentations supplemented with 
each carbon source were conducted as single assays, except for fer­
mentations with inulin (duplicate) and alginate (triplicate or more). 
Fig. 1a shows the representative data. 
2.3. DNA extraction 
We extracted DNA using the bead-beating method with some mod­
ifications (Hosomi et al., 2017). Briefly, each culture was centrifuged to 
obtain a cell pellet, which was then mixed and vigorously vortexed with 
0.3 mL lysis buffer (No. 10, Kurabo Industries Ltd., Osaka, Japan) and 
0.3 g 0.1-mm glass beads. The mixture was mechanically disrupted by 
bead beating using a Fast prep-24 5G cell disruptor (Funakoshi Co., Ltd., 
Tokyo, Japan) at speed 5.0 for 60 s at room temperature and then 
incubated on ice for 5 min. After repeating this process five times, the 
homogenized sample was centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 5 min at 4 ◦C, 
and 0.2 mL supernatant was collected. Then, DNA was extracted using a 
Gene Prep Star PI-480 automated DNA extraction machine (Kurabo In­
dustries Ltd., Osaka, Japan). The DNA concentration was checked using 
a NanoDrop 1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, 
USA). A DNA concentration higher than 10 ng/µL was sufficient for 
further analyses. 
2.4. Microbiota analysis 
The V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified 
using a TaKaRa Ex Taq HS Kit (TaKaRa Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) and 
sequenced using the paired-end method on an Illumina MiSeq instru­
ment with the MiSeq v3 Reagent Kit (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) 
as previously described (Odamaki et al., 2016). After removing se­
quences matching with data from the Genome Reference Consortium 
human build 38 (GRCh38) and phiX reads from the raw Illumina paired- 
end reads, the sequences were analyzed using the QIIME2 software 
package version 2017.10 (Bolyen et al., 2019). Potential chimeric se­
quences were removed using DADA219, and then 30 bp and 90 bp of the 
3′ region were trimmed from the forward and reverse reads, respec­
tively. Taxonomical classifications were performed using the Naive 
Bayes classifier trained on the manually curated Greengenes 13.8 
database with a 99% threshold of operational taxonomic unit full-length 
sequences. Hierarchical clustering analyses of species-level composi­
tions were performed using distances based on Pearson’s correlations 
(Fig. 1).

2.6. Growth assay 
Bacteria pre-cultured at 37 ◦C for 16 h under anaerobic conditions in 
YCFA were used for the growth assays. To evaluate their ability to utilize 
alginate, 96-well plates (Becton, Dickinson and Company, NJ, USA) 
containing 100 μL YCFA, YCFA-GMC, or YC medium (Supplementary 
Table 1) supplemented with 1% (w/v) alginate were incubated at 37 ◦C 
for 24 h under anaerobic conditions (N2:H2:CO2, 10:10:80). Each well 
was inoculated with 1 μL pre-cultured bacteria to initiate the culture. 
To evaluate the existence of symbiotic relationships, co-cultures of 
F. prausnitzii and Bacteroides were established using the same method as 
that used for the fecal fermentations described in Section 2.2. Each 
vessel was inoculated with 100 μL pre-culture (OD600 (optical densi­
ty600nm) = 1.0) to initiate the culture, and the culture was grown for 24 
h. 
To evaluate the ability to utilize alginate oligosaccharides (AOS), 
each F. prausnitzii strain was cultured in 2 mL YCFA supplemented with 
1% (w/v) commercially available AOS (Hokkaido Mitsui Chemicals, 
Inc., Hokkaido, Japan) at 37 ◦C for 24 h under anaerobic conditions. 
The OD600 values of all the cultures were measured using a SH- 
9000Lab multi-micro plate reader (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). All the as­
says were performed in either triplicate or three independently repeated 
experiments. 
2.7. Labeled main SCFAs analysis 
The carboxyl group of SCFAs and organic acids (e.g. lactic acid, 
acetic acid, propionic acid, crotonic acid, isobutyric acid, n-butyric acid, 
3-methyl crotonic acid, isovaleric acid, n-valeric acid) in each medium 
was labeled with 2- nitrophenyl hydrazide using a Short- and Long- 
Chain Fatty Acid Analysis Kit (YMC Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan) with the 
improved kit protocol. Briefly, 40 µL supernatant of a 24-h culture in 
YCFA medium was mixed with 20 µL 50 mM caproic acid (FUJIFILM 
Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan) as an internal standard, 40 µL 
20-mM 2-nitrophenylhydrazin (in water), and 40 µL 0.25 M N-(3- 
dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride HCl [in 
ethanol, with an equal volume of 3% pyridine in ethanol (v:v)]. The 
mixture was heated at 60 ◦C for 20 min. Then, 40 µL 15% (w/v) po­
tassium hydroxide was added, and the mixture was incubated for 
another 15 min at 60 ◦C. The reaction mixture was added to 400 µL of a 
3.8:0.4 (v:v) mixture of 0.03 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.4):0.5 M hy­
drochloric acid and then 500 µL hexane was added. The components 
were mixed to remove the long- and middle-chain fatty acids. The 
hexane layer was removed, and hexane extraction was performed again. 
After removing the long- and middle-chain fatty acids, the SCFA de­
rivatives were extracted with 600 µL diethyl ether. The diethyl ether 
layer was evaporated to dryness under a nitrogen stream at room tem­
perature. The residue was dissolved in 200 µL methanol, and 10 µL of 
each sample was analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography 
(HPLC) to quantify the main SCFAs (acetic, propionic, and butyric 
acids). 
Chromatographic separations were performed using the YMC-Pack 
FA method with some modifications (Miwa, Hiyama, & Yamamoto, 
1985) using an Ultimate 3000 LC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., 
Waltham, MA, USA). A YMC-Pack FA HPLC column (YMC Co., Ltd., 
Kyoto, Japan) was used. The solvent system for elution from the YMC- 
Pack FA column consisted of 5:16:79 (v:v:v) acetonitrile (Merck, 
Darmstadt, Germany): methyl alcohol (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical 
Corporation): ultrapure water. The elution program consisted of a linear 
gradient from 9% to 40% acetonitrile over 25 min, followed by a step­
wise increase to 80% acetonitrile over 5 min, and finally, a stepwise 
decrease to 22% acetonitrile to re-equilibrate the column for 2 min. The 
proportion of methyl alcohol was kept constant at 16%. Solvents were 
adjusted with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid. The flow rate was 1.2 mL/min. 
The column temperature was kept constant at 50 ◦C. The eluents were 
monitored at 400 nm to detect derivatized 2-nitrophenylhydrazin. 
2.8. Thin layer chromatography analysis 
Degraded alginate and its derivatives were evaluated using thin layer 
chromatography (TLC) with pre-coated silica gel-60 TLC aluminum 
plates (Merck) as previously described (Bai et al., 2017). Samples were 
spotted onto plates, and then the plates were developed with a solvent 
system consisting of 4:6:1 (v: v: v) 1-butanol (NACALAI TESQUE, INC.): 
formic acid (NACALAI TESQUE, INC.):water or 1:2:1 (v: v: v) 1-butanol: 
acetic acid:water. The plates were then stained by spraying with 
diphenylamine–aniline–phosphoric acid reagent [100 mL acetone 
(FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp.), 1 g diphenylamine (Nacalai 
Tesque Inc.), 1 mL aniline (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp.), and 
10 mL phosphoric acid (Hayashi Pure Chemical Industry, Ltd., Osaka, 
Japan)] and visualized by heating in a toaster oven. 
2.9. Genome sequencing and data assembly 
Genomic DNA of F. prausnitzii MCC2041 was used to construct a li­
brary for genome sequencing as previously described (Odamaki et al., 
2018). Paired-end sequencing was performed using an Illumina MiSeq 
instrument with the MiSeq v3 Reagent Kit (Illumina Inc.). Quality 
trimming and de novo assembly were performed using the CLC Genomics 
Workbench version 8.0 software package (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) 
with default settings, except that the minimum contig length was 500 
bp. 
2.10. Metagenomics analysis 
Metagenomic analysis was performed on DNA extracted from bac­
terial cells in the feces fermentation medium supplemented with algi­
nate. Sequence libraries were generated using a ThruPLEX® DNA-Seq 
Kit (Takara Bio Inc.), and shotgun sequencing was carried out on the 
Novaseq 6000 platform (Illumina). The obtained sequences were 
quality-filtered with custom Perl and Python scripts and publicly 
available software, as follows. After removing sequences containing 
degenerate base ‘N’, Bowtie2 (version 2.3.4.1) was used to remove reads 
mapped to the phiX 174 sequence and the Genome Reference Con­
sortium human build 38 (GRCh38). Then, we trimmed adapter se­
quences and bases with quality values of 17 or less consecutively at the 
3′ region with cutadapt (version 2.9). Finally, we removed trimmed 
reads<25 bp in length with an average quality score of<25 and those 
lacking paired reads. 
The high-quality reads from each sample were assembled using 
megahit (v1.2.9) with default parameters. Contigs longer than 5 kbp 
were annotated by BLASTn searches against the NCBI reference genome 
(cut-off: e-value 〈10 −5). Of them, contigs assigned to Faecalibacterium 
were subjected to further analyses. We predicted open reading frames 
using prodigal (v2.6.3) and identified AlgQ1/2 homologs in the meta­
genome by BLASTn-based searches (cut-offs: identity > 70 and coverage 
> 60). The abundance of AlgQ1/2 homologs was indicated as a score,

2.11. Real-time PCR 
Real-time PCR was performed using an ABI PRISM 7500 fast real- 
time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with TB Green® Premix 
Ex Taq™ Tli RNaseH Plus (TaKaRa Bio Inc.). The primers used to 
amplify the 16 s rRNA sequences from total bacteria were F: 5′- 
CCTACGGGRSGCAGCAG-3′ and R: 5′-ATTACCGCGGCTGCT-3′, as pre­
viously described (Milani et al., 2013); those used to amplify the 16 s 
rRNA gene of F. prausnitzii were F: 5′-′GAGGAGCTTGCTTTCTCAAG-3′
and R: 5′- GGCCGTTACCTCACCAACTA-3′; and those used to amplify 
AlgQ1/2 homologs were designed in this study; F: 5′-ACATCGCCCT­
GAAAATCAAC-3′
and R: 5′-TCACATTCAGCACCTGCTTC-3′. The 
amplification program was as follows: initial hold at 95 ◦C for 20 s, 
followed by 40 cycles at 94 ◦C for 3 s and 60 ◦C for 30 s. Fluorescent 
products were detected at the last step of each cycle. Melting curves 
were obtained by heating from 60 ◦C to 95 ◦C in 0.2 ◦C/s increments, 
with continuous fluorescence collection. All samples were assessed in 
duplicate. The detection limit was 106 copies/mL. 
2.12. Statistical analyses 
Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient analyses were performed 
using SPSS IBM version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Other 
statistical analyses were performed using EZR software version 3. 4. 1 
(Kanda, 2013). All data are presented as means ± standard deviations. P- 
values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

All of the sequence data reported in this paper are available in DNA

3. Results 
The main workflow of this study is shown as a flowchart (Fig. S1). 
3.1. Simulation of prebiotic effects on the human gut microbiota 
First, we evaluated whether nine different potential prebiotics 
affected the composition of gut microbiota using a pH-controlled single- 
batch fermenter as a human gut microbiota model. The compositions of 
the six fecal microbiota samples used in the present study and the pH 
changes that occurred during fecal fermentation are shown in Supple­
mentary Figs. 2 and 3a–j, respectively. As expected, lactulose and inulin, 
which are both well-known prebiotics for bifidobacteria, resulted in 
significant increases in Bifidobacterium abundance (Fig. 1a). Notably, the 
proportion of F. prausnitzii out of total bacteria was markedly increased 
in fecal fermentation culture containing alginate (Fig. 1a). Real-time 
PCR analyses confirmed a significant increase in the cell number of 
F. prausnitzii in the fecal fermentation cultures containing alginate 
(Fig. 1b). An additional time-course fermentation experiment showed 
that the relative abundance of F. prausnitzii gradually increased and 
became stable after 24 h of fecal fermentation with alginate in the me­
dium (Fig. S4). At 24 h of culture, the relative abundance of F. prausnitzii 
was significantly positively correlated with the butyrate concentration 
in all fermentation mixtures (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary 
Fig. S5). 
3.2. Cross-feeding between F. Prausnitzii and Bacteroides strains 
Next, we evaluated the growth capabilities of two F. prausnitzii 
strains, an isolate (MCC2041) from a fecal sample and a type strain 
(ATCC27768), in medium with alginate as the sole carbon source. Un­
expectedly, neither of the strains grew well (Fig. 2a, b), indicating that 
F. prausnitzii could not grow without other gut microbes in the medium. 
Based on the finding that, among the bacterial taxa with relatively high 
abundance, Bacteroides showed positive correlations with F. prausnitzii 
in the alginate-supplemented culture (Fig. 3), we independently co- 
cultured F. prausnitzii with three Bacteroides strains confirmed to uti­
lize alginate. The F. prausnitzii strains grew in the medium in the pres­
ence of alginate-assimilating strains of Bacteroides (Fig. 4a, b). The 
concentration of acetic acid produced by the Bacteroides was higher in 
medium containing alginate than in media containing other prebiotics 
(Fig. S4a–c). The ratio of acetic acid to the main SCFAs showed the same 
trend. The amount of butyric acid produced by three Bacteroides strains 
was below the detection limit (Fig. 5a–c). 
3.3. Mechanism of uptake of AOS in F. Prausnitzii 
We found that bacterially produced AOS remained in the medium 
after a Bacteroides monoculture, and F. prausnitzii strain MCC2041 was 
able to utilize a portion of them (Fig. 6). Therefore, we evaluated the 
growth capabilities of the two F. prausnitzii strains in a medium con­
taining commercially available AOS composed of mainly two to six units 
of β-D-mannuronate and/or α-L-guluronate. Consistent with the co- 
culture results with Bacteroides, F. prausnitzii strain MCC2041, but not 
strain ATCC27768, was able to grow in the medium by utilizing sugars 
shorter than hexa-oligosaccharides (Fig. 7a, c). Our genome sequencing

4. Discussion 
Alginates are natural macromolecules composed of β-D-man­
nuronate and α-L-guluronate units linked by 1–4 glycosidic bonds, and 
they are the main component of cell walls of brown algae. Like other 
dietary fibers, alginate reaches the colon without being broken down by 
digestive enzymes and is then decomposed by gut bacteria (Bai et al., 
2017; Li et al., 2016; Shang et al., 2018). There are some reports that 
alginate may modulate the composition of gut microbiota (Cherry et al., 
2019; Gupta, Lokesh, Abdelhafiz, & Siriyappagouder, 2019; Wang, Han, 
Hu, Li, & Yu, 2006); however, it is unknown whether alginate specif­
ically affects the proportion of F. prausnitzii in adult gut microbiota. In 
this study, we found that alginate elevated the relative abundance of 
F. prausnitzii during fecal fermentation in a pH-controlled single-batch 
system. 
One advantage of this study was the use of the pH-controlled culture 
system that mimics gut environmental conditions, including bacterial 
interactions (Takagi et al., 2016). Our findings are from a fermentation 
system that functions as a human gut microbiota model, not from 
monocultures, which are commonly used to evaluate carbohydrate uti­
lization by bacteria. Thus, our initial results of alginate degradation by 
gut bacteria were obtained using a system that allows complex in­
teractions among microbiota to occur. Inulin (Rios-Covian, Gueimonde, 
Duncan, Flint, & De Los Reyes-Gavilan, 2015), resistant starch (Cherbuy 
et al., 2019), and 1-kestose (Tochio, Kadota, Tanaka, & Koga, 2018) are 
candidate prebiotics for F. prausnitzii growth. However, we observed 
that the cell number of F. prausnitzii significantly increased in the me­
dium supplemented with alginate rather than these prebiotic candidates 
(Fig. 1b). This may be because gut microbes other than F. prausnitzii 
preferentially utilized those carbohydrates. For example, F. prausnitzii 
assimilates inulin in monocultures (Rios-Covian et al., 2015), but bifi­
dobacteria may more rapidly incorporate inulin than F. prausnitzii, 
resulting in a greater increase in bifidobacteria abundance compared 
with F. prausnitzii abundance in a medium containing inulin (Fig. 1). Our

A correlation analysis of gut microbiota composition in the alginate-

acetic acid is an energy source for F. prausnitzii (Duncan, Barcenilla,

symbiosis between F. prausnitzii and Bacteroides.

key factors influencing cross-feeding between these bacterial taxa.

pectin-derived uronic acid. Alginate is composed of mannuronic acid 
and guluronic acid, both of which are uronic acids. Thus, F. prausnitzii 
might be able to utilize these uronic acid-related substances.

AlgM1M2SS in Sphingomonas sp. A1 is the only ABC transporter that 
has been experimentally identified, and it may play a role in the incor­
poration of alginate (Momma et al., 2000). This transporter includes two 
periplasmic alginate solute-binding proteins, AlgQ1 and AlgQ2 
(Momma et al., 2005) and two alginate lyases; a polylyase that degrades 
alginate polymers into oligosaccharides (Hashimoto, Miyake, Momma, 
Kawai, & Murata, 2000), and an oligo-alginate lyase that degrades oli­
gosaccharides into monosaccharides (Hashimoto, Miyake, Ochiai, & 
Murata, 2005). Our sequencing analyses revealed a gene cluster that 
encodes homologs of AlgQ1/2 and an oligo-alginate lyase, but not a 
polylyase, in the genome of F. prausnitzii MCC2041. In the culturing 
experiments, F. prausnitzii MCC2041 was able to assimilate both the 
bacterially produced AOS and commercially available AOS (Figs. 6–8). 
These findings, coupled with the significantly increased copy number of 
AlgQ1/2 after fermentation in the alginate culture, led us to hypothesize 
that this gene cluster encodes proteins involved in the utilization of AOS. 
We detected AlgQ1/2 homologs in 68.4% (309/452) of tested Japanese 
subjects by real-time PCR. Interestingly, AlgQ1/2 homologs were pre­
sent at a relatively higher level in elderly subjects than in other adults. 
According to the National Health and Nutrition Survey of 2018, elderly 
Japanese individuals eat much more seaweed than do their younger 
counterparts (National Health and Nutrition, 2018). Therefore, the 
enrichment of AlgQ1/2 may be advantageous for F. prausnitzii strains in 
terms of enhanced adaptation and increased competitiveness in the 
elderly human gut environment because it allows them to efficiently 
incorporate AOS. These results indicate that AOS have potential appli­
cations as prebiotics for F. prausnitzii. 
While using a pH-controlled fermenter as a human gut microbiota 
model has many advantages, it is still only an in vitro assay (Takagi et al., 
2016). Further in vivo and clinical studies are needed to evaluate the 
effects of alginate on the composition of human gut microbiota. 
5. Conclusion 
In this study, we determined that the presence of alginate indirectly 
increased the abundance of F. prausnitzii under fecal fermentation 
mimicking human gut conditions. The effect of alginate to enhance 
growth may rely on cross-feeding with Bacteroides strains that assimilate 
alginate and produce acetic acid and AOS. Furthermore, the strain that 
utilized AOS harbored homologs of genes encoding components of the 
ABC transporter involved in alginate metabolism. Given the high prev­
alence of the AlgQ1/2 homologs in Japanese individuals detected in this 
study, our results suggest that alginate may have a growth-promoting 
effect on F. prausnitzii through alginate assimilation and degradation 
by Bacteroides species.
