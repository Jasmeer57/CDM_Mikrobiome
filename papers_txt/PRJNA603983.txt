ABSTRACT 
13 
Whole grains are generally low in non-digestible carbohydrates that are available for 
14 
fermentation by the gut microbiota, or microbiota accessible carbohydrates (MAC). However, 
15 
there is potential to increase MAC in whole grains through food processing. Five processing 
16 
methods: boiling, extrusion, sourdough bread, unleavened bread, and yeast bread, were applied 
17 
to whole wheat flour and then subjected to in vitro digestion followed by fermentation using fecal 
18 
microbiomes from 10 subjects. The microbiomes separated into 2 groups: those that showed 
19 
high carbohydrate-utilization (CU) and those that exhibited low CU, the former of which 
20 
exhibited not only enhanced CU but also increased butyrate production (MAC: 31.1±1.1% 
21 
versus 19.3±1.2%, p<0.001; butyrate: 5.26±0.26 mM versus 3.17±0.27 mM, p<0.001). Only the 
22 
microbiomes in the high CU group showed significant differences among processing methods: 
23 
extrusion and sourdough bread led to dichotomous results for MAC and short chain fatty acid 
24 
production, where extrusion resulted in high MAC but low butyrate production while sourdough 
25 
bread resulted in low MAC but high butyrate production. Extrusion led to a noticeable decrease 
26 
in α-diversity and some members of the Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae families, with 
27 
increases in Acinetobacter, Enterococcus, and Staphylococcaceae. This study demonstrated 
28 
that only microbiomes that exhibited high CU responded to the effects of processing by showing 
29 
significant differences among processing methods. In these microbiomes, extrusion was able to 
30 
increase accessibility of the cell wall polysaccharides, but did not increase butyrate production. 
31 
In contrast, sourdough bread led to high butyrate production by supporting important butyrate-
32 
producers in the Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae families. 
33 
IMPORTANCE 
34 
Dietary non-digestible carbohydrates, or dietary fiber, have long been recognized for 
35 
their beneficial health effects. However, recent studies have revealed that fermentation of non-
36 
digestible carbohydrates by gut bacteria is critical in mediating many of the health-promoting 
37 
properties of dietary fibers. Whole grains are excellent candidates to supply the microbiome with

INTRODUCTION 
47 
Dietary, non-digestible carbohydrates, mainly from dietary fiber, are the major energy 
48 
sources for the microbiome (1). Fermentation of these carbohydrates by the microbiota results 
49 
in numerous metabolites, including short chain fatty acids (SCFA), that are absorbed by the host 
50 
and have pleiotropic local effects on intestinal function as well as systemic roles in insulin 
51 
secretion, lipid metabolism, and inflammation, among others (2–4). 
52 
Unfortunately, a large proportion of non-digestible carbohydrates in whole grains are not 
53 
metabolically available to gut microbes for fermentation (5–7). Human feeding trials have 
54 
suggested that only about one-third of the non-digestible carbohydrates in grains are accessible 
55 
for fermentation by the microbiota compared with 75-90% for fruit and vegetable fibers (6). 
56 
Therefore, although whole grains are rich sources of non-digestible carbohydrates, they may not 
57 
be good sources of microbiota accessible carbohydrates (MAC) (8).  
58 
Processing has been proposed as a way to manipulate carbohydrate utilization (CU) by 
59 
the microbiome (9). For example, boiling is a minimal form of grain processing that involves 
60 
moderate temperatures (100 °C), atmospheric pressure, minimal shear, and an abundance of 
61 
water. During boiling, water swells the starch granules resulting in gelatinization, but the 
62 
structure of the principal non-digestible carbohydrates, β-glucan and arabinoxylan, appeared 
63 
unaffected (10). In contrast, extrusion is a severe form of processing that uses high heat (>120 
64 
°C), pressure, and mechanical shear at low moisture contents to process grains. This process 
65 
results in not only gelatinization but also partial depolymerization of the starch and non-
66 
digestible carbohydrates (10). Furthermore, extrusion enables the conversion of some insoluble 
67 
fibers to soluble fibers; the latter of which exhibit increased fermentability (11). Accordingly, 
68 
extrusion has been shown to increase the metabolism of dietary fibers from grains by gut 
69 
bacteria both in vitro using human fecal microbiota (12) and in vivo in rat feeding studies (13).  
70 
Another form of grain processing, bread making, is the most common processing 
71 
technique for wheat (14). The breadmaking process has been shown to increase soluble dietary

fiber at the expense of insoluble fiber (15). Spontaneous fermentation of wheat bran increased 
73 
solubility of arabinoxylan with a lower average degree of arabinose substitution than native 
74 
bran, which are thought to be more easily degradable by intestinal microbiota (16). Bread 
75 
making techniques were compared in whole grain rye bread in yeast-fermented crispbread, 
76 
unfermented crispbread, and sourdough bread (10). In all breads, β-glucan and arabinoxylan 
77 
were distributed as small fragments throughout the starch matrix. The unfermented crispbread 
78 
had larger pieces of bran and intact cell structures compared with the other breads. For 
79 
sourdough, starch granules were less swollen and surrounded by more leached amylose, which 
80 
was proposed to act as a protective layer against starch hydrolysis during digestion. 
81 
Furthermore, pre-fermentation of wheat bran with yeast and enzymes in bread making resulted 
82 
in increased SCFA production during in vitro fermentation compared with no pre-fermentation 
83 
(17).  
84 
Clearly, the variety of processing methods of grains affect the chemical and physical 
85 
structures of non-digestible carbohydrates in differential ways. While most of these chemical 
86 
and physical changes would likely increase MAC, no studies have compared processing 
87 
methods to determine which method might result in the greatest improvement in CU by the 
88 
microbiome. Thus, the purpose of this study was to evaluate how different common food 
89 
processing techniques change MAC in whole grains and shift the microbiota during in vitro 
90 
fermentation. (This research was conducted by author Caroline Smith in partial fulfillment of the 
91 
requirements for a Master of Science degree from the University of Nebraska-Lincoln (18).)

Whole wheat processing and digestion 
94 
Because grain processing is known to affect the physical and, in some cases, the 
95 
chemical composition of the non-digestible carbohydrates in whole grains, whole wheat flour 
96 
was processed using five common grain processing methods—boiling, extrusion, sourdough 
97 
bread, unleavened bread, and yeast bread—to determine how these processes may affect MAC

Microbiota accessible carbohydrates in processed whole wheat samples 
108 
 
Grain processing method resulted in significant differences in MAC in processed whole 
109 
wheat samples during in vitro fermentation using microbiomes collected from 10 subjects (Fig 
110 
2A). Extrusion resulted in higher MAC compared to boiled wheat and sourdough bread.  
111 
Examining the results by microbiome, it was evident that CU across 5 microbiomes (1, 3, 
112 
4, 9, 10) was higher than that of the other 5 microbiomes (2, 5, 6, 7, 8) (Fig 2B). Indeed, the 
113 
microbiomes exhibiting high CU fermented 31.1±1.1% of the total carbohydrates during 
114 
fermentation compared with only 19.3±1.2% for the low CU microbiomes (p<0.001). Thus, 
115 
microbiomes 1, 3, 4, 9, and 10 were termed “high CU” and 2, 5, 6, 7, and 8 were termed “low 
116 
CU” microbiomes in some subsequent analyses. 
117 
An analysis of the proportion of carbohydrates fermented by CU group revealed that the 
118 
significant differences among processing methods were driven by the high CU microbiomes; the 
119 
microbiomes with low CU showed no significant differences among processed samples (Fig 
120 
2C). Notably, however, the differently processed samples ranked the same across CU groups 
121 
(i.e., extrud > yeast > unleav > boil > sour). Therefore, although both CU groups responded 
122 
similarly to the processed samples, the high CU microbiomes responded to a greater magnitude 
123 
than the low CU microbiomes did.

DISCUSSION 
241 
Due to the effect of processing on the physicochemical composition of the non-digestible 
242 
carbohydrates in whole grains, five common processing methods for whole wheat were 
243 
evaluated for their influence on MAC. Across the 10 microbiomes, extrusion resulted in the 
244 
highest MAC and sourdough bread the lowest. However, microbiomes differed significantly in 
245 
the magnitude of CU, where high CU microbiomes exhibited, on average, 37.5% more 
246 
carbohydrate fermentation than those in the low CU group. It was only these high CU 
247 
microbiomes that were able to distinguish (significantly) among the differently processed 
248 
samples in terms of both MAC and butyrate production; the low CU microbiomes showed no 
249 
significant differences between processed samples. Although there were no significant 
250 
differences in MAC among processed samples when fermented with the low CU microbiomes, 
251

the numeric ranking of the samples was the same as for the high CU microbiomes (i.e., 
252 
extruded > yeast-leavened bread > unleavened bread > boiled wheat > sourdough bread). 
253 
Therefore, the processing methods induced similar but diminished responses in the low CU 
254 
microbiomes compared to the high CU microbiomes.  
255 
These results are similar to a previous study performed in our laboratory (using different 
256 
fecal donors) where only microbiomes in the high CU group showed significant differences in 
257 
MAC and butyrate production among different types of grain (19). This provides some possible 
258 
insight into why some individuals “respond” in terms of improvements in host health, while 
259 
others are “non-responders” (20). Since fermentation by the microbiome is considered critical 
260 
for bringing about the health benefits of many dietary fibers (21), diminished fiber fermentation 
261 
may attenuate these effects. Others have noted that food science and food processing will play 
262 
a central role in the development of foods that target the microbiome (22). However, from the 
263 
present results it may be that only people that harbor microbiomes that are capable of high CU 
264 
will be able to realize the benefits of such foods derived from whole wheat. 
265 
The microbiomes in the high CU group exhibited some important differences compared 
266 
to the low CU microbiomes in the fecal samples that help explain why the high CU microbiomes 
267 
were able to ferment more carbohydrates. First, the high CU microbiomes were significantly 
268 
more diverse in terms of observed ASVs, but not Shannon diversity, than the low MAC 
269 
microbiomes. Since observed ASVs takes into account only presence or absence of ASVs 
270 
without considering abundance, while Shannon diversity takes into account the abundance 
271 
(evenness), this suggests that the high CU microbiomes had more low abundance ASVs than 
272 
low CU microbiomes. Indeed, when differentially abundant ASVs were analyzed between the 
273 
two CU groups, many more ASVs were significantly associated with the high CU microbiomes 
274 
compared with the low CU microbiomes. In general, having a diverse microbial community 
275 
results in a broader range of carbohydrate fermenters that are able to hydrolyze a wider range 
276 
of glycosidic bonds and carbohydrate configurations (23). Upon examination of the differentially 
277

abundant ASVs between CU groups, it was immediately evident that the composition within the 
278 
Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae families in particular was very different (not in terms of 
279 
overall relative abundance, but in types of ASVs present). This was especially relevant because 
280 
members of these families have been identified as important complex fiber degraders (24).  
281 
Although we did not collect dietary data from the subjects that donated fecal samples for 
282 
this study, the differences in microbiota composition between the high and low CU microbiomes 
283 
suggests that the high CU microbiomes may have come from fecal donors with higher diet 
284 
quality, including higher fiber intakes. Several previous studies have shown associations 
285 
between host dietary fiber intake and abundance of Prevotella and certain fiber-degrading 
286 
members of the Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae families (25–27), which were notable 
287 
distinguishing differences between the high and low CU microbiomes in this study. Furthermore, 
288 
in a previous study we did differentiate microbiomes on the basis of fiber intake and found that 
289 
only the microbiomes from donors with high fiber intake showed significant differences between 
290 
different types of grain, which is analogous to different processing methods in the present study 
291 
(19). 
292 
Although important differences in microbiome diversity and composition between the 
293 
high and low CU microbiomes were noted in fecal samples, the changes that occurred during 
294 
fermentation exaggerated these differences. For instance, in the fecal samples Shannon 
295 
diversity across the entire microbiota was not different between the high and low CU 
296 
microbiomes, nor were any of the measures of α-diversity within the Lachnospiraceae family. 
297 
However, after fermentation all of these metrics were significantly different. The high CU 
298 
microbiomes not only were more diverse before fermentation, but they maintained that diversity 
299 
even during fermentation. In contrast, the low CU microbiomes started with less diversity and 
300 
ended with even less diversity. This was evident in the β-diversity results, where the high CU 
301 
microbiomes exhibited a smaller “shift” during fermentation than the low CU microbiomes.  
302

The smaller shift could be framed as microbiota “resilience.” Previous research has 
303 
shown that when the microbiome receives a severe perturbation, such as an antibiotic regimen, 
304 
a fiber-rich diet increases resilience, i.e., it reduces loss of taxa and speeds the time until the 
305 
microbiota recovers (28). Using a stool sample in an in vitro fermentation experiment could 
306 
certainly be classified as a severe perturbation to the microbiota, where it is taken from its 
307 
normal environment and moved to a test tube.   
308 
Within the high CU group, some important significant differences were exhibited 
309 
between processing methods. At the outset of the study we hypothesized that extrusion would 
310 
result in the highest MAC, since previous researchers have shown that extrusion leads to 
311 
modifications in the carbohydrates, such as increased porosity and fineness of cell wall 
312 
structures and the conversion of a portion of the insoluble fiber to soluble fiber, which is 
313 
generally considered more fermentable (23). This is indeed what we found. Surprisingly, 
314 
however, this was not accompanied by higher butyrate production. This may be explained by 
315 
the decreases in α-diversity and carbohydrate-degrading bacteria from the Ruminococcaceae 
316 
and Lachnospiraceae families, which are important butyrate producers in the gut (29). Instead, 
317 
extrusion resulted in increases in Acinetobacter, Enterococcus, and Staphylococcaceae, some 
318 
members of which grow rapidly and prolifically in fermentation media, but are not butyrate 
319 
producers (30).  
320 
Therefore, the disruption of the cell wall matrix by extrusion may change the competition 
321 
dynamics among bacteria such that the microbiota is less reliant on specialized complex 
322 
carbohydrate-degrading microorganisms to initiate cell wall metabolism, i.e., cell wall breakdown 
323 
is already initiated by the extrusion process. This would mean that fast-growing microbes could 
324 
outcompete other bacteria for the newly available MAC leading to decreased diversity and 
325 
decreases in butyrate producers (Fig 7).  
326 
The unexpected dichotomy between high carbohydrate fermentation and low butyrate 
327 
production introduces the importance of “microbiota accessible carbohydrate quality” rather than 
328

http://aem.asm.org/
Downloaded from 
 
15 
 
just “quantity.” There are several papers and reviews that discuss the importance of fiber 
329 
structure on fermentation properties (31). In these papers, authors argue that the structure of 
330 
the fiber, rather than simply the quantity, should be considered when selecting a dietary fiber to 
331 
promote gut health. Here, extrusion was effective in increasing fermentable fiber, but that fiber 
332 
that was released was not associated with positive outcomes. Therefore, food processing 
333 
operations that are designed to increase carbohydrate fermentation should increase 
334 
accessibility of “quality” carbohydrate, i.e., those carbohydrates that will support beneficial 
335 
microbes (such as butyrate producers) in the gut, rather than just increasing carbohydrate 
336 
fermentation in general. 
337 
  
The high MAC and low butyrate production during fermentation of the extruded wheat is 
338 
different from a previous study conducted in our laboratory using wheat bran instead of whole 
339 
grain (12). In our previous study, extrusion led to increased MAC, but also showed increases in 
340 
butyrate production compared to boiled wheat bran. The contrasting results could be due to the 
341 
different substrates (whole wheat versus wheat bran). Wheat bran, which primarily consists of 
342 
non-starch polysaccharides, has an increased concentration of cell wall components. Thus, the 
343 
effect of extrusion on MAC and SCFA production could have been diminished in the present 
344 
study due to the dilution of the cell wall components in whole grains compared to what bran. 
345 
Indeed, in the previous study, the extruded bran was reconstituted to whole wheat flour to make 
346 
whole grain breads and there were no significant differences in MAC or SCFA production 
347 
between the whole grain breads after fermentation.  
348 
Importantly, the extrusion conditions selected in this study were very severe. These were 
349 
chosen based on our previous study in wheat bran to maximize the effects of the processing 
350 
method (12). However, the combination of very low moisture and high temperature and screw 
351 
speed would be unlikely to be used in a typical food product. Therefore, the effect of extrusion 
352 
on gut microbial fermentation in real food products may not be as pronounced as shown in this 
353 
study.

In contrast to extrusion, the sourdough processed samples resulted in lower MAC, yet 
355 
resulted in higher butyrate production in the high CU microbiomes. Sourdough bread, along with 
356 
the other breads, exhibited increased abundance of beneficial microbes from the 
357 
Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae families that ferment complex carbohydrates into 
358 
SCFA (24). Sourdough-like fermentation of wheat bran with enzyme mixtures and yeast has 
359 
been previously shown to promote SCFA production in vitro compared to native wheat bran 
360 
(17). 
361 
The especially high butyrate production from fermentation of the sourdough samples 
362 
could be a result of the changes in the starch that occur during the sourdough process. 
363 
Sourdough has been shown to increase the leaching of amylose during breadmaking, which is 
364 
rapidly retrograded during cooling and is associated with resistant starch (10, 32). Indeed, 
365 
together with the boiled wheat, the sourdough processed samples had the most resistant starch 
366 
among all of the processing methods. 
367 
Although resistant starch may explain some of the positive effects noted during 
368 
fermentation of the sourdough samples, it cannot explain everything. As mentioned, the boiled 
369 
samples contained similar concentrations of resistant starch to the sourdough samples, yet 
370 
resulted in only moderate butyrate production, maintenance of microbial diversity, and increases 
371 
in Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae. Therefore, the sourdough process must induce 
372 
some additional effects, likely on the cell wall polysaccharides (10), that help maintain microbial 
373 
diversity and enable high butyrate production (Fig 7). 
374 
In summary, it is hypothesized that severe extrusion changes the microstructure of 
375 
carbohydrates in whole grains by breaking down the cell wall components. These changes in 
376 
structure result in easier access to the cell wall polysaccharides by microbiota with high capacity 
377 
for CU. This changes the competition dynamics among the microbes such that the fastest 
378 
growing taxa can outcompete specialized cell wall degrading bacteria that typically break down 
379 
cell wall polysaccharides but may grow slower. This leads to decreased diversity and production 
380 
 on August 30, 2020 at Edward G. Miner Library, UNIV OF ROCHESTER
http://aem.asm.org/

17 
 
of beneficial metabolites such as butyrate. In contrast, during sourdough bread making, 
381 
increased resistant starch together with minor modifications to the cell wall matrix creates higher 
382 
quality MAC both in terms of variety and complexity. These phenomena lead to a more diverse 
383 
microbiome that has increased capacity to produce SCFA, especially butyrate, in microbiomes 
384 
with high CU. Similar trends can be seen in microbiomes with low CU, but the differences are 
385 
diminished. This information can be used to better understand how to use food processing to 
386 
create microbiota directed foods that promote a healthy microbiome.

MATERIALS AND METHODS  
388 
Whole wheat processing 
389 
Hard red wheat was obtained from Bay State Milling (Quincy, MA). Wheat kernels were 
390 
dried at 40°C for 16 h before milling as described (33). All milled fractions obtained from the mill 
391 
were mixed together to obtain whole wheat flour. 
392 
For boiling, water (530 g) was brought to a boil on a gas range. Once boiling, the heat 
393 
was reduced to a simmer and 1 g of salt and 100 g whole wheat flour were added under rapid 
394 
manual stirring for 5 min. The boiled wheat porridge was then cooled to room temperature 
395 
before being frozen at -80°C and then freeze dried. This procedure was performed in triplicate. 
396 
For extrusion, whole wheat flour (1 kg) was mixed for 10 min with 1% salt (w/w) and 
397 
water to adjust to 20% moisture content (dry weight basis) in a stand mixer (c-100, Hobart, Troy, 
398 
OH). The mixtures were equilibrated in closed containers at 4 °C overnight. The moisture-
399 
adjusted whole wheat flours were then extruded using a benchtop-scale twin-screw extruder 
400 
equipped with a single stage mixing zone and a 3 mm outlet die at 250 rpm, 3:1 compression 
401 
ratio, and a 20:1 L/D ratio (CW Brabender Instruments, NJ, USA). The extruder was operated 
402 
by a direct current drive unit (Intelli-Torque, Pastic Corder Lab-station, C.W. Brabender) with a 
403 
5.6 kW motor. The flour was fed into the extruder using a volumetric feeder (FW 40 Plus, C. W. 
404 
Brabender) set at a constant flow rate of ~50 g/min. Barrel temperatures were set at 60 °C 
405 
(zone 1; inlet), 70 °C (zone 2),  120 °C (zone 3) , and 120 °C (zone 4; die assembly). Samples 
406

407

408 
the product contained very low moisture at this stage (~3%), the dried extrudates were subject 
409 
to freeze drying (3600, Freeze Dry Co., Pine River, MN) because the other processed wheat 
410 
products were freeze dried following processing. The whole wheat flour was extruded in 
411 
triplicate. 
412 
For bread making, this study employed an in-house method for bread making that 
413 
involved production of flatbreads. Flatbread-type breads were selected because they are made 
414 
using any of the three selected fermentation procedures: unleavened, baker’s yeast 
415 
fermentation, or sourdough fermentation. The procedures were planned to unify the essential 
416 
bread making procedures and only varying the steps that were unique to each fermentation 
417 
procedure. For the baker’s yeast fermentation treatment, instant dry yeast was used (Saf Instant 
418 
Red, Lesaffre, Milwaukee, WI). For the sourdough fermentation, a type1 spontaneous 
419 
sourdough starter culture was used. To prepare the culture, 50 g of whole wheat flour and 50 g 
420 
of water (100% absorption) were incubated at 30°C for 24 h, whereupon 50 g of that mixture 
421 
was mixed with 50 g of fresh whole wheat flour and 50 g of water and then allowed to ferment 
422 
for another 24 h at 30°C. After 5 d, the starter culture was sampled for yeast and lactic acid 
423 
bacteria using standard methods (34, 35). At this point, the culture contained 107 CFU/g of lactic 
424 
acid bacteria and 106 CFU/g yeast, measured using standard methods. During the analysis, 
425 
daily feeding of the starter culture was continued. Once sufficient numbers of yeast and lactic 
426 
acid bacteria were confirmed, the starter culture was used to make breads, which was on day 9.  
427 
All breads were prepared on the same day in triplicate 500-g batches with 70% 
428 
absorption (70 g water/100 g whole wheat flour on a 14% moisture basis). The doughs were 
429 
prepared by kneading for 10 min in an electric stand mixer with a dough hook at speed 2 
430 
(KitchenAid, Whirlpool, Benton Harbor, MI USA). The dough was then rounded by hand, 
431

In vitro digestion 
440 
Samples were digested following established procedures (36). Briefly, freeze-dried 
441 
processed whole wheat products were broken into small pieces by hand. Twenty-five grams of 
442 
sample were then stomached with 300 mL of water for 1 min. Then, the pH was adjusted to 2.5 
443 
with 1 M HCl followed by the addition of 10 mL of 10% (w/v) pepsin (P7000, Sigma, St Louis, 
444 
MO) in 50 mM HCl. The slurry was then incubated for 30 min at 37 °C with orbital shaking at 
445 
150 rpm. Following pepsinolysis, 50 mL of 0.1 M sodium maleate buffer (pH 6, containing 1 mM 
446 
CaCl2) was added and the pH was adjusted to 6.9 with 1 M NaHCO3. Next, 50 mL of 12.5% 
447 
(w/v) pancreatin (P7545, Sigma) in sodium maleate buffer was added followed by 2 mL of 
448 
amyloglucosidase (3260 U/mL, Megazyme, Bray, Ireland). The slurry was then incubated for 6 h 
449 
37 °C with orbital shaking at 150 rpm. Following digestion, the material was transferred to 
450 
dialysis tubing (molecular weight cutoff, 12,000-14,000) and dialyzed against distilled water for 4 
451 
d at 4 °C with a water change at least every 12 h. The retentate from dialysis was collected and 
452 
freeze dried.

490

504 
weight as it occurs in a polysaccharide (0.88*weight for pentose sugars and 0.9*weight for 
505

506

Microbiota composition 
512 
Changes in microbial population were observed using 16S rRNA sequencing. A kit from 
513 
Biovet (BioSprint 96 One-For-All Vet Kit, Quebec Canada) was used for bacterial DNA 
514 
extraction. Microbiome characterization was performed by amplicon sequencing of the V4 
515

522

523 
analyses. Diversity metrics of the fecal and fermented samples were calculated using QIIME2.

525

528

529

530 
inner diameter × 0.25 μm film thickness, Supelco, Bellefonte, PA) and flame ionization detector. 
531 
SCFA were quantified by calculating response factors for each SCFA relative to 2-ethylbutyric 
532 
acid using injections of pure standards.
