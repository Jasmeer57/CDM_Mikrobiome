A B S T R A C T   
Probiotics and vitamins can impact immune responses and modulate gut microbiome. We evaluated the effects of 
consuming a yogurt fortified with vitamins and probiotic Lacticaseibacillus casei and rhamnosus on upper respi­
ratory tract infections frequency and gut microbiome during a 3-month intervention. The study included 2 case 
groups (consuming different flavours) and a placebo group of healthy adults (n = 158–160 in each group). The 
effects on URTI-related parameters in both case groups were gender-specific. The female subjects had lower URTI 
incidence and frequency, while for the males no significant differences were found. The URTI duration was 
shorter in one of the probiotic groups for females and in both such groups - for males. The observed changes in 
microbiome composition, blood and stool parameters were not different from those observed in the placebo 
group. Consumption of fortified fermented dairy foods is promising for improving immunity status within the 
general population.   
1. Introduction 
Probiotics have been defined as the “live microorganisms that, when 
administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host” 
(Gibson et al., 2017). Dairy products are the most common category of 
functional foods to be fortified with probiotics. Although the underlying 
full set of mechanisms is still unclear, the application of probiotics shows 
some promising results and trends with respect to immunity modula­
tions. Limited evidence from systematic reviews shows that probiotics 
are effective means for treating infectious diarrhea (Niel, Cornelius, 
Feudtner, Garrison, & Christakis, 2002), preventing antibiotic- 
associated diarrhea (Kale-Pradhan, Jassal, & Wilhelm, 2010) and 
certain other disorders. The mechanisms by which probiotics may confer 
health benefits include the immunomodulation of local immunity (by 
maintaining gut wall integrity) and systemic immunity (by enhancing 
non-specific and specific arms of the immune system). While the 
probiotics can be consumed in a stand-alone way, one of the convenient 
ways to deliver them is supplementing functional food products with 
them, possibly along with the other components like dietary fibers, 
minerals and vitamins. Intake of vitamin supplements can be effective in 
improving immunity, particularly, related to respiratory tract infections 
(Martineau et al., 2017). 
“Immune health” is an umbrella term to describe how the body de­
fends against infection and harmful substances. In simple consumer 
understanding, the frequency of seasonal flu and colds (which can be 
called in common respiratory tract infections) can be an indicator of 
immune health resistance state useful for evaluating the health benefits 
of fortified products. Acute upper respiratory tract infections (URTI) 
including the common cold, acute sinusitis, acute pharyngitis, acute 
laryngotracheobronchitis (croup), acute epiglottitis (supraglottitis), 
acute rhinosinusitis and acute otitis media are a major cause of 
morbidity, especially in children and the elderly (Ozen, Kocabas Sandal,

& Dinleyici, 2015). They are caused by a large variety of viruses and 
bacteria. Acute URTIs are usually mild, viral infections with symptoms 
subsiding after a few days. However, they are associated with enormous 
economic burden (Shann et al., 1999) and high antibiotic use: in 
developing and transitional countries, the proportion of primary care 
upper URTI cases in which antibiotics have been prescribed increased 
from 43% to 71% during the 1982 to 2006 period (“WHO | Medicines 
Use in Primary Care in Developing and Transitional Countries” 2015). 
The first experimental evidence that probiotic strains could prevent 
respiratory tract infections was demonstrated by protection of mice 
against influenza by the administration of Bifidobacterium breve strain 
YIT4064 augmented anti-influenza IgG (Yasui, Kiyoshima, Hori, & 
Shida, 1999). In one of the first interventional trials to investigate that 
hypothesis, the children in day care centres were given milk fortified 
with the probiotic Lacticaseibacillus (previously Lactobacillus) rhamnosus 
strain GG resulting in fewer days of absence from day care because of 
illness (Hatakka et al., 2001). Later studies showed this strain was effi­
cient for URTI prevention in adults, too (Capurso, 2019). Interventions 
with L. casei strains suggest that they can reduce various URTI incidence 
and/or symptoms and improve blood and immune parameters, although 
with a strain-specific efficacy (Shida et al., 2017; Jespersen et al., 2015; 
Guillemard, Tanguy, & Ann ’laure Flavigny, Stephan de la Motte, & 
Schrezenmeir, 2010). 
According to a recent Cochrane Collaboration review including 
pooled data from 12 RCTs involving 3720 participants, probiotics were 
found to be superior to placebo in reducing the number of participants 
experiencing episodes of acute URTI by about 47% and the duration of 
an episode of acute URTI - by about 1.89 days (Hao, Dong, & Wu, 2015). 
Probiotics may slightly reduce antibiotic use and cold-related school/ 
work absence, while their health risks in non-immunocompromised 
subjects are minor, with gastrointestinal symptoms being the most 
common (Sotoudegan, Daniali, Hassani, Nikfar, & Abdollahi, 2019). 
Support of immunity based on probiotic-fortified foods is of high rele­
vance especially now, during the global pandemic of COVID-19 (Olai­
mat et al., 2020). 
Gut microbiome plays an important role in human well-being by 
being involved in regulating many of its functions, including the innate 
and adaptive immunity. During the last years, surveys based on high- 
throughput sequencing allowed to outline the landscape of human 
microbiome composition in health and disease. Observative and inter­
ventional studies helped to establish associations between its specific 
features and factors including diet, lifestyle and medications intake 
(Falony et al., 2016; Klimenko et al., 2018; Olekhnovich et al., 2019). 
During the oral intake of probiotics (as a supplement or included in a 
functional food product), these microorganisms may transiently become 
a part of the gut microbial community and affect its ecology. Interest­
ingly, the persistence of a probiotic in the gut and the response 
(measured as microbiome change or physiological outcome) has strong 
interindividual variability that can be to a certain extent predicted from 
the baseline microbiome composition (Ma et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 
2016; Volokh, Klimenko, Berezhnaya, Tyakht, Nesterova, Popenko, & 
Alexeev, 2019). The probiotic effects on host physiology can be 
accompanied or not accompanied with the changes in gut microbiome. 
Most interventional studies assessing the impact of probiotics or 
probiotic-fortified foods on the microbiome have been conducted on 
cohorts with specific disorders, e.g. constipation or post-antibiotic 
treatment. This contrasts with the fact that most consumers of func­
tional foods with probiotics are generally healthy (Khalesi et al., 2019). 
In the present study, we assessed how the intake of a fermented dairy 
product supplemented with vitamins and probiotics affects the URTI 
frequency and other parameters, gastrointestinal health and cellular 
immunity in a large healthy cohort in comparison with the placebo- 
consuming group. The intervention was conducted during the spring 
epidemiological season of URTI. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Study design 
This study was conducted as prospective randomized double-blinded 
placebo-controlled trial in parallel groups to assess the effects of long- 
term consumption of a fortified fermented dairy product on URTI pa­
rameters, gastrointestinal health and cellular immunity parameters in 
healthy adults’ population (18–50 years of age) during the period of 
increased incidence of disease (respiratory tract infections – cold, 
influenza). The primary endpoint was URTI incidence; the hypothesis 
was that it would be reduced in the group consuming the fortified 
product versus the placebo group. 
The study was approved by the local ethics committee of each 
participating clinical center. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in 
the Supplementary Methods. Prior to any study-related procedures or 
examinations, each volunteer signed the informed consent form. The 
participants were enrolled in Moscow, Russia in 3 clinical centers. 
The required number of complete cases in each group was 205. 
Assuming a 20% withdrawal rate, it was intended to enroll 246 subjects 
in each of three groups resulting in 738 subjects in total. The groups 
were: control IP1 group received placebo product (same formulation as 
the fortified product but without the probiotics and vitamin fortifica­
tion; blueberry premix), case IP2 group (fortified product with straw­
berry premix) and case IP3 (fortified product with multi fruit premix). 
The planned duration of the study was 98 days (84 ± 2 days of con­
sumption followed by a 14-days follow-up period), during which 5 on- 
site visits and 1 phone contact were carried out. It was planned to 
enroll the cohort within 14 days, so that all subjects consumed the 
product during the same time period (the period of regionally increased 
incidence of URTI including common cold and influenza - according to 
the epidemiologic data of Russian Ministry of Health). The product 
intake was twice daily (2 portions per day, portion size 100 ml), iden­
tically for the control (placebo) and case (fortified product) groups. The 
subjects were recommended to withhold Pevzner #15 general healthy 
balanced diet during the intervention (Pevzner, 1949). Consuming any 
products or medications (including biologically active supplements) 
fortified with probiotics/prebiotics and/or vitamin-mineral complexes 
were not allowed during the study. 
2.2. Product description 
The investigated product was the Imunele ® (PepsiCo, Russia) 
drinkable yogurt with fruit premix fortified with two bacterial strains 
declared by the manufacturer as probiotics (Lacticaseibacillus casei and 
L. rhamnosus) and a vitamin complex. The concentration of lactic acid 
microorganisms (including the starter culture and probiotic bacteria) 
was at least 1⋅107 CFU/g. The concentration of the probiotic cultures 
was at least 1⋅106 CFU/g; the analysis of the probiotics’ levels in the 
product during the shelf-life using cultivation-dependent and indepen­
dent methods as well as of its total microbial community has been 
described before (Berezhnaya et al., 2021). The vitamin complex 
included the vitamins, per 100 ml of product: A – 105 μg, D3 – 1.5 μg, E – 
1.5 mg, B6 – 0.22 mg, B9 – 30 μg and B12 – 0.3 μg. As each participant in 
the case groups consumed 200 ml of the fortified product daily, it 
amounted for the following proportion of the reference daily intake 
(RDI) of each vitamin: A – 26%, D3 – 60%, E – 30%, B6 – 22%, B9 – 30% 
and B12 – 60%.

(2) Date, duration, symptoms and symptoms’ severity of gastroin­

The severity was measured as a score from 0 (no) to 3 (severe) for the 
symptoms listed in the Supplementary methods. This information was 
collected for all subjects enrolled in the study. 
Collection of stool and blood samples along with the completion of 
the food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was performed before and after 
the dietary intervention. Blood samples were used for the whole blood 
test (WBT) and cellular immune parameters analysis (14 parameters 
describing the concentration of various immune cells types in counts/μL 
and %, see Supplementary methods). One replicate of each stool sample 
was used for the macroscopic and microscopic examination and con­
sistency assessment (an equivalent of the Bristol stool scale; see Sup­
plementary Methods), while the second replicate was frozen and 
transported to the sequencing laboratory where the DNA extraction and 
16S rRNA gene sequencing were performed as described previously 
(Klimenko et al., 2018). Also, two replicates for each investigated 
product (IP1, IP2 and IP3) were collected to identify the sequences 
related to the probiotics and starter culture (the preparation and pre­
processing steps are the same as for microbiome data). 
2.4. Preprocessing of microbiome data 
The 16S rRNA gene sequencing data were preprocessed using the 
Knomics-Biota platform (Efimova et al., 2018). Briefly, the reads were 
denoised using the Deblur algorithm (Amir, McDonald, Navas-Molina, 
Kopylova, Morton, Zhenjiang Zech, & Kightley, 2017) implemented in 
QIIME 2 (Bolyen et al., 2018). The taxonomy was assigned with a naive- 
bayes classifier trained on the GreenGenes database v.13.5, 97% OTU 
similarity (DeSantis et al., 2006). The reads were deposited in the 
Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under PRJNA678221 accession number 
(to be released; reviewer link: https://dataview.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov 
/object/PRJNA678221?reviewer=pprbouoa3uc4tdm8vfm5mb6maj). 
Non-relevant species detected in the negative control samples as well as 
the species reported as likely laboratory contaminants (Park et al., 2019; 
Salter et al., 2014; Weyrich et al., 2019) were removed from the anal­
ysis. The read counts of microbial species and genera were calculated as 
the sum of reads assigned to the amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) 
included in the respective taxon. 
Evaluation of a microbial community richness (alpha diversity) for 
each sample was performed using the Shannon diversity metric on the 
ASV level after rarefaction to 3000 counts. There were 5 iterations of 
rarefaction and the average diversity value was calculated. 
2.5. Statistical analysis 
The statistical analysis was performed in R statistical programming 
language, version 3.4.0 (R Core Team, 2020). All analysed factors can be 
arbitrarily divided into two groups: unpaired (information was recorded 
throughout the study) and paired (information was recorded twice - at 
the beginning and at the end of the study). Normality of the residuals 
was tested with Shapiro-Wilk test if needed. The adjustment for multiple 
comparison was conducted separately for each method and factor 
category using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, the adjusted p values 
are denoted as “q” throughout the text. 
2.5.1. Unpaired clinical data analysis 
The factors registered throughout the whole study were denoted as 
unpaired factors. They included the data related to URTI and GID. Their 
incidence was compared between the groups using the chi-squared test. 
The following group comparisons were performed: IP1 vs IP2, IP1 vs 
IP3. Additionally, as the formulations of fortified products consumed in 
IP2 and IP3 groups were identical but for the fruit premix, IP1 was also 
compared with the union of IP2 and IP3. 
Additionally, the between-group differences in URTI and GID 
duration were analyzed for the subjects that had at least one disease 
episode using the one-sided Mann-Whitney test. An alternative hy­
pothesis was that the incidence or duration mean value was higher in the 
IP1 group than in the other group. Similar comparison was performed 
for the duration and severity of symptoms. 
2.5.2. Paired clinical data analysis 
The factors that had been registered before and after the intervention 
were denoted as paired (WBT, cellular immune and stool microscopy 
parameter categories). These factors were subject to additional filtering 
and transformations (see Supplementary methods). For each paired 
factor, its change during the intervention was analyzed using 2 methods. 
Firstly, it was tested which factors significantly changed their values 
between the two time points in each group separately using a mixed- 
effect linear model with the subject ID as a random variable. Sec­
ondly, to assess the between-group changes, the covariance analysis 
(ANCOVA) was used to find the between-group differences of the 
changes for each factor. The analysis includes construction of a linear 
model, where a group number is used as explanatory variable, the value 
of the factor at the 2nd time point - as a response variable and its value at 
the 1st time point - as a covariate. 
2.5.3. Analysis of microbiome composition changes 
The microbiome composition features included alpha diversity, beta 
diversity and relative abundance of microbial genera. 
The subjects with absolute value of alpha diversity change > 2 or 
baseline value of alpha diversity < 3 were considered as outliers and 
excluded from the analysis of alpha diversity (in total 20 subjects). 
Within-group dynamics of alpha diversity and difference between the 
groups was modeled using ANCOVA (see Supplementary Methods for 
details). Mean change in all subjects was calculated without exclusion of 
outliers. 
In line with the compositionality-aware approach, the beta diversity 
was measured using Aitchison distance at the species level. Total shift of 
microbiome community structure was evaluated with PERMANOVA in 
each group separately (adonis2 function from vegan library with the 
subject ID as a “strata” parameter (Oksanen, Blanchet, & Kindt, 2011)). 
To compare the changes between the groups, the median dissimilarity 
between the paired samples collected before and after the intervention 
was compared using the Mann-Whitney test. 
For the analysis of microbiome composition changes at the genus 
level, the selbal method was used (Rivera-Pinto, Egozcue, Pawlowsky- 
Glahn, Paredes, Noguera-Julian, & Calle, 2018). To compare the 
microbiome dynamics between the groups, the original function selbal. 
cv() was modified to use the difference of the balance values between 
the two samples of a subject instead of the balance value itself (see 
Supplementary Methods for details). Comparison of the obtained bal­
ances between the time points and IP groups was performed using the 
Wilcoxon test for the paired samples and Mann-Whitney test for inde­
pendent samples. Only taxa detected in < 30% of baseline samples or/ 
and samples collected after the intervention were included in this 
analysis. 
Clusters of co-occurring bacteria were obtained with SPIEC-EASI 
package version 1.0.6 (Kurtz et al., 2015) using the read counts at the 
baseline aggregated on the species level. The procedure was conducted 
as described before (Klimenko et al., 2020) with minor changes: the 
species detected in < 10 samples were excluded; the minimum lambda 
ratio was set to 0.2; the clusters were detected using the Louvain algo­
rithm. The defined clusters of taxa were named cooperatives. 
The sets of the taxa clusters at the baseline were used to form the 
balances by calculating a log ratio of taxa geometric means for each 
combination of clusters (for example, geometric mean of cluster 1 taxa 
abundances in numerator and geometric mean of cluster 2 taxa abun­
dances in denominator of the balance). These balances are denoted 
below as cooperative balances. The dynamics of balance values were 
assessed using paired t-test with Benjamini-Hochberg multiple testing

2.5.4. Analysis of associations between various factors and microbiome 
composition 
The preprocessed paired factors were compared to the baseline 
microbiome composition. The set of factors was preprocessed as it was 
described in the section “Paired clinical data analysis”. 
Baseline associations of alpha diversity with each factor were iden­
tified by fitting a linear regression. Besides age as a factor, gender and 
clinical center were included in each model as covariates. The Box-Cox 
transformation was used to transform the data to a normal distribution; 
the transformation parameter values from −5.0 to 5.0 with step 0.1 were 
tested. 
Analysis of variance was performed in a similar way, but using the 
adonis2 function. Gender and center were included in the model as 
“strata” parameters and age - as a covariate. 
During the selbal analysis of the genus-level taxonomic composition, 
the gender, age and clinical center were used as the covariates. The taxa 
detected in <30% of baseline samples were excluded from this analysis. 
For the identified associations, a linear regression was fitted to estimate 
the proportion of balance’ variance explained by each factor. The factor, 
age, gender and clinical center were the predictors in the model. 
3. Results 
3.1. Groups description 
The total number of subjects for whom the data were collected was 
730. The data collected from two sites were discarded as these centers 
did not comply with the Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines. 
Ultimately, there were 477 subjects for whom the data on URTI was 
available: 159 subjects in the IP1, 160 - IP2 and 158 - in the IP3 group. 
The subjects from the three groups were equally distributed across the 
sites (chi-squared test, p = 0.99); for each subject, both samples were 
collected at the site. The groups included 325 females and 152 males. 
The gender ratio was similar in the IP2 (112 females and 46 males) and 
IP3 (116 females and 43 males) groups, however, the IP1 group included 
a lower proportion of females than each of the IP2 and IP3 (97 females 
and 63 males) (chi-squared test, p < 0.05). Neither the age (28.8 ± 8.4 
years in IP1, 28.7 ± 9.2 in IP2 and 28.9 ± 9.4 in IP3) nor the BMI (23.6 
± 3.7 in IP1, 23.4 ± 4.9 in IP2 and 23.3 ± 3.9 in IP3) were significantly 
different between the three groups (ANOVA test, p > 0.05). 
3.2. Effect of the product intake on the URTI parameters 
Overall, out of 477 subjects for whom the data on URTI was avail­
able, 144 (30.2%) subjects had URTI at least once during the study, 
among them 29 (6%) had URTI more than one time. The temporal dy­
namics of URTI episode frequency are shown in Fig. 1A and Table 1. 
We tested if the URTI incidence (number of subjects who had an 
URTI episode at least once) or the URTI frequency (number of URTI 
episodes per subject) were different between the IP1 and each of the IP2 
and IP3 groups. For both incidence and frequency significantly higher 
rates in the control group were observed only in IP1 vs IP2 comparison 
(Table 1). However, as we mentioned above, imbalance in the genders’ 
distribution between groups could influence the observations. Therefore 
more reliable results would be obtained with gender stratification. 
Among the female subjects, there was a significantly lower URTI inci­
dence as well as URTI frequency during the study (Fig. 1, Table 1) in IP2 
and IP3 groups (separately and combined) compared to the control 
group IP1 (for incidence comparison between IP1 and IP3 the associa­
tion was at the edge of significance). For the male subjects, there were no 
significant differences in any of these comparisons.

During the analysis of the reported end points, we observed gender 
specificity of the URTI incidence: 52.6% of females had URTI at least 
once compared to 26.7% of males (chi-squared test, p = 0.0042). 
However, when comparing gender distribution in experimental groups 
separately, the difference was observed only in the placebo group (p =
0.0003 for IP1, 0.5988 for IP2 and 0.6210 for IP3). 
Additionally, the analysis of URTI duration was performed for the 
volunteers that had URTI at least once (n = 178 is the total number of 
disease episodes for which the duration was provided, S = 144 is the 
number of subjects). The results are shown in the Supplementary Table 1 
and Fig. 1 C. The URTI duration was significantly longer for the subjects 
from IP1 group compared to ones from IP2 group - for each of the male 
and female subgroups and also without gender stratification (p < 0.05). 
More to that, for the male subjects, the same association but on the edge 
of significance (p = 0.0651) was observed for IP1 vs IP3 groups 
comparison. 
Duration and severity of each symptom in the IP1 control group were 
compared with the IP2 and IP3 groups for the volunteers that had a 
symptom at least once using a one-sided Mann-Whitney test (for each of 
the factors, the alternative hypothesis was that its values were higher in 
the IP1 group). There were no significant differences for the symptoms 
severity neither for males nor for females in either of the IP1 vs IP2 and 
IP1 vs IP3 comparisons. However, the throat pain and weakness epi­
sodes appeared to be shorter in the males from IP3 compared to the IP1 
group (q = 0.0422 and 0.0489 respectively, Supplementary Fig. 1). The 
weakness episodes were also significantly shorter for the females, but 
only in IP1 vs IP2 comparison (q = 0.0474). 
3.3. Effects on the gastrointestinal symptoms 
Out of 477 subjects for whom the data on GID episodes frequency 
was available, 130 reported at least one GID episode during the study. 
The GID incidence as well as the GID frequency in the IP1 group were 
compared to the ones in each of the IP2 and IP3 groups with gender 
stratification (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 2). The only 
significant difference was a lower GID frequency among the females 
from the IP3 group compared to the females from the IP1 group. 
Overall, neither the duration of episodes (Supplementary Table 3) 
nor the severity and duration of symptoms were different between the 
IP1 vs IP2 or IP1 vs IP3 groups (q > 0.05). 
3.4. Dynamics of the blood, cellular immunity and stool parameters 
The number of the data points for each feature after the filtering (see 
Methods) is provided in the Supplementary Table 4. The changes of 
features after the intervention were evaluated separately for the male 
and female subjects. For the dynamics of blood and stool parameters, we 
identified moderate within-group changes (Supplementary Table 5), but 
no between-group differences. Significant differences in cellular im­
mune response were observed only for 2 factors among the males: the 
placebo-consuming IP1 group had a more pronounced decrease in B- 
lymphocytes (CD19 + CD3-) and lymphocytes (white blood cell differ­
ential) counts compared to the IP3 (ANCOVA, q = 0.0306 for each 
parameter; Supplementary Fig. 3). Noteworthy, the median values of the 
parameters before and after the intervention were within the reference 
interval corresponding to a healthy state (Stiehm, 2004). The baseline 
values of the two parameters were not significantly different between 
these two subgroups (ANOVA, p > 0.05).

intervention using the following descriptors: alpha diversity, beta di­
versity, balances of microbial genera and cooperatives. The analysis 
within each study group was followed by the comparison between the 
groups. 
3.6. Within-group dynamics 
Interestingly, alpha diversity decreased in each group: the Shannon 
index decreased by 0.24, 0.13 and 0.22 in the IP1, IP2 and IP3, 
respectively (p < 0.025). The mean decrease was 0.24 ± 0.93. Beta di­
versity analysis showed that there was a significant shift in overall 
community structure in each group, too (PERMANOVA, p < 0.05; 
Supplementary Table 6). 
For more detailed analysis, the selbal method was used to identify the 
alterations in the microbiome at the genus level. Briefly, the method is 
designed to identify the minimal set of taxa explaining an association of 
a microbiome composition with a given factor. The set is derived as two 
subsets of taxa - positively and negatively associated with the factor; the 
proportion between the two (numerator and denominator) is called a 
balance (see Supplementary Methods for more rigorous explanation). 
Here we considered the time point as the factor and identified the 
respective set for each study group (Fig. 2). As a common feature, each 
of three sets included the Blautia and Dorea genera (Supplementary 
Table 7) with the same direction of association: the ratio of Blautia to 
Dorea decreased after the intervention, as confirmed via the additional 
Wilcoxon signed-rank test (p 〈1 0 −6). 
We extended the methodology of the balances from the genera to the 
bacterial cooperatives (co-occurrence clusters; see Methods) and applied 
it to investigate the microbiome dynamics composition. However, no 
significant changes were found for any of the groups (q > 0.1). 
3.7. Between-group comparison 
Despite the profound microbiome alterations in each group, we 
observed no differences between the placebo- and each of the fortified 
product-consuming groups. The size of alpha diversity decrease was not 
different between the groups (p = 0.17 for IP1 vs IP2, p = 0.827 for IP1 
vs IP3 comparison). Stratification by gender did not produce any dif­
ferences in its change either. 
Total change of microbiome composition (Aitchitson distance be­
tween the samples collected before and after the intervention) was also 
independent of the product type (Supplementary Table 6). 
Finally, there were no significant between-group differences in the 
dynamics of microbiome composition neither at the genus level (Sup­
plementary Table 8) nor at the level of microbial cooperatives. Neither 
were found during the gender-stratified analysis.

parameters.

4. Discussion 
To date, this is one of the largest placebo-controlled interventional 
studies of a probiotic-fortified food product to assess its effect not just on 
human health but also on the gut microbiome. Previously it has been 
demonstrated that intake of food with probiotics can lead to the reduced 
frequency of respiratory tract infections, their symptoms severity and 
duration in generally healthy cohorts (Khalesi et al., 2019; Berggren, 
Ahr´en, Larsson, & ¨Onning, 2011; de Vrese et al., 2005; Guillemard, 
Tondu, Lacoin, & Schrezenmeir, 2010). The supposed mechanisms un­
derlying these observations include changes induced by modulation of 
the intestinal gut community, direct stimulation of immune cells and 
changes of intestinal environment characteristics during probiotics 
passage. In our study, in addition to the probiotics, the fortification also 
included the vitamins consumption of which can prevent URTI (Marti­
neau et al., 2017); the vitamin dosage was quite high - up to 60% of RDI 
for some of them (D3 and B12). To investigate the effect of the product, 
we performed the assessment of upper URTI-related and gastrointestinal 
parameters, stool, blood and cellular immunity parameters along with 
gut microbiome composition before and after the intervention. 
Resonating to previous reports on probiotics intake (Lehtoranta, 
Latvala, & Lehtinen, 2020), we observed a lower incidence of URTI 
events among the subjects who consumed the fortified product 
compared to the placebo-consuming control group - however, only

URTI frequency was much higher among the females than among the 
males. Similar phenomena - slightly higher incidence rates of URTI in 
females - have been reported for a large Dutch cohort (Hak, Rovers, 
Kuyvenhoven, Schellevis, & Verheij, 2006). Several possible explana­
tions for this observation can be suggested. Firstly, in the families with 
small children, it is the children who get infection at kindergarten or 
school and introduce it to their family - where the mothers are at higher 
risk than fathers as their contacts with children are more intense. Sec­
ondly, there are social-economic reasons: in Russia, women are more 
likely to choose the social carriers like medical workers, educators and 
salespeople. Thirdly, the females might be more compliant, particularly, 
more pedantic in reporting end points. Finally, the men who volunteered 
to participate in such a long study were likely health-seekers and could 
be more conscious about their health than the women in the study. 
Both male and female subjects had lower URTI events duration (for 
the females, the significant difference was observed only for the IP2 vs 
IP1 comparison) (Fig. 1). At the same time there were no significant 
differences in symptoms’ severity both for males and females. Reduction 
of URTI duration but not symptoms’ severity has been reported before 
(Guillemard, Tondu, Lacoin, & Schrezenmeir, 2010). The effect of the 
fortified product on gastrointestinal health was less pronounced: a trend 
for the GID frequency decrease was identified for one of the two pro­
biotic groups - and only among the females. 
Interestingly, we did not observe significant differences in dynamics 
of almost any of the measured additional parameters (blood and stool, 
cellular immunity tests) between the control and any of the case groups. 
The whole blood and stool test parameters behave similarly in all three 
groups. The majority of changes for WBT were observed only in the IP1 
group: MCV and PDW increase along with the lymphocytes and baso­
phils decrease. Cellular immunity response analysis revealed significant 
differences only between the IP1 and IP3 groups and only among the 
males: in the control group, the B-lymphocytes and lymphocytes in 
general decreased while in the case group none of them changed. The 
effect of Lacticaseibacillus probiotic intake on B-lymphocytes level has 
been reported before (Berggren et al., 2011), however, the direction of 
the association was opposite (increase). One of the possible reasons we 
observed fewer changes in cellular immunity parameters than earlier 
studies (Berggren et al., 2011; de Vrese et al., 2005; Hao et al., 2015; 
Harbige, Pinto, Allgrove, & Thomas, 2016) could be a longer time be­
tween the immunity analysis (3 months vs. 2 to 4 weeks). The cellular 
immunity parameters could have returned to the baseline within a 
month after an infection. Noteworthy, the study population was quite 
young de facto (28.8. ± 9.0 years), so it is no surprise that no compli­
cations were observed and the cellular immunity parameters remained 
quite within the reference ranges. We suggest that further studies 
including subjects of older age (and hence lower immunity) are more 
likely to show the effects of fortified product intake. 
Overall, while the fortification with probiotics and vitamins led to 
the decrease of several URTI-related indicators in a gender-dependent 
manner, its effects on gastrointestinal health and most physiological 
parameters were not significant. 
The selected study design does not allow to conclude whether the 
observed immunity improvement was mostly due to the intake of pro­
biotics or vitamins (one would have to include 2 additional groups 
consuming products fortified with probiotics only and with vitamins 
only). It is likely that the contribution of vitamins was higher - consid­
ering that the daily product dose for the IP1 group contained as high as 
60% of RDA for some vitamins, while the study population is deficient in 
vitamins: for example, previous study of 1226 residents from North- 
Western Russia showed that as high as 45.7% of them had vitamin D 
deficiency (Karonova et al., 2016). A vitamins deficiency test conducted 
before and after the intervention would be helpful here. Furthermore, 
the daily dose of probiotics was on the order of 108 CFU - a value 1–2 
orders lower than commonly targeted in most trials investigating the 
effects of probiotics on URTI (Hao et al., 2015) - thus possibly limiting its 
effects. As health-promoting properties of probiotic bacteria are often 
strain-specific, our results do not preclude that fortification based on 
other strains of Lacticaseibacillus or other species might be more efficient 
in improving immunity. A more detailed investigation of the interindi­
vidual variability can be conducted by evaluating the persistence and 
viability of the probiotic strains in the gut. The sequencing data showed

Our results showed that addition of probiotics and vitamins did not 
affect the microbiome response to the yogurt intake despite the long 
intervention period. Although we observed significant changes of mi­
crobial composition features during the intervention in each group, they 
were similar between the groups. Firstly, the alpha diversity decreased 
in each group - this could be linked to dietary restrictions stemming from 
the recommendations given to the participants or to seasonal changes of 
diet. The extent of decrease (the decrease of Shannon index of 0.24 ±
0.93) was comparable to the effect of a 2-week uncontrolled diet change 
(mean decrease of Shannon index 0.22) (Klimenko et al., 2018). Sec­
ondly, there were similar specific changes of community structure be­
tween the groups. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use the 
balance approach to evaluate the dynamics in gut microbiome compo­
sition linked to a dietary intervention. Although less intuitive than the 
relative abundance of bacterial taxa, the concept of balances provides 
many advantages. It allows to reveal the ecological competition between 
groups of microorganisms under the influence of changing conditions 
and provides a more rigorous and precise analysis (Gloor, Macklaim, 
Pawlowsky-Glahn, & Egozcue, 2017). 
In our study, it showed that the decrease of Blautia to Dorea ratio was 
a significant pattern common between the groups (after the adjustment 
for gender, age and clinical center performed). Interestingly, these two 
genera were strongly co-occurring, being the members of the same 
bacterial cooperative (Supplementary Fig. 4), as also observed in pre­
vious studies (Volokh et al. 2019; Klimenko et al., 2018). At the baseline, 
the cooperative included links between the major acetate- (Blautia) or 
lactate-producing taxa (Lactococcus, Lactobacillus) with the ones able to 
produce butyrate from lactate (Roseburia, Faecalibacterium) and acetate 
(Coprococcus) (Louis & Flint, 2009). The total dietary intake of lactose 
increased in each of the three groups during the intervention, as sug­
gested from the analysis of FFQ data (data not shown). This may have 
shifted the proportions within the cooperative metabolism in the favor 
of lactate preference over the acetate, reflected in the diminished growth 
of the acetate-producing Blautia. This suggestion is additionally sup­
ported by the observed acetate-utilizing Coprococcus decrease in two 
groups (IP2 and IP3) and lactate-utilizing Faecalibacterium increase - in 
one group (IP3). 
While the main objective of the study implies the comparison of 
multiple data before and after the intervention, the large cohort size also 
provided an opportunity to explore the associations between gut 
microbiota composition and various factors. Some of those have been 
investigated in previous large studies, however, without accounting for 
the compositional nature of the microbiome data - thus possibly having 
brought bias to the results. We for the first time conducted such asso­
ciative analysis based on the proportions between microbial taxa rather 
than their relative abundance, to reveal several interesting findings. 
The list of the factors we identified as those linked to the microbiome 
composition (measured via beta diversity) - age, BMI and gender - was 
strongly overlapping with the top effect size associations from earlier 
population-level surveys on Western European cohorts - LLDeep and, to 
a lower degree, FFGP (Falony et al., 2016). Hence, the associations were 
confirmed not just on another population but also using a more robust 
statistical approach. Extending the previous surveys, our list of factors 
included the analysis of macroscopic and microscopic stool sample 
features. The novel associations of them to microbiome features likely 
reflect alterations of gastrointestinal balance. Among them, presence of 
digestible dietary fiber, intracellular amylum and unstriated muscle fi­
bers in stool are commonly considered a sign of digestion disorders. We 
suggest that further studies on subjects with gut disorders reveal more 
specific links of stool test results to the proportions of specific taxa. 
Another identified association was between the presence of soaps in 
stool and the balance of unclassified Alcaligenaceae to Anaerostipes. Stool 
soaps are the product of undigested fat and calcium and magnesium 
salts, and its presence in stool may reflect disorders in bile and digestive 
ferments production. Reduced production of bile acids important for fat 
digestion is observed in liver cirrhosis (Kakiyama et al., 2013). On the 
other hand, the Alcaligenaceae are known to be overrepresented in stool 
of patients with cirrhosis (G´omez-Hurtado, Such, Sanz, & Franc´es, 
2014). 
Among the gender, age and BMI, the associations with proportions of 
specific taxa were identified only for gender: females had higher levels 
of Bacteroides to Roseburia balance. Influence of gender on the taxa

enterotype and at the same time is more typical for females (Vandeputte

5. Conclusions 
Long-term intake of a yogurt fortified with vitamins and probiotic 
Lacticaseibacillus led to improvement of several URTI-related indicators 
in a gender-dependent manner compared to the placebo. The positive 
effects were more pronounced in females who demonstrated lower URTI 
frequency and incidence in both probiotic-consuming groups and lower 
URTI duration in one probiotic-consuming group. For males, the only 
significant URTI-related improvement was decreased duration in both 
probiotic-consuming groups. Comparison with the placebo-consuming 
group did not show significant impact of the fortification neither on 
blood or stool parameters nor on gut microbiome composition and di­
versity. Further extended studies are required to elucidate the variability 
of the effects between the genders. Functional dairy foods with vitamins 
and probiotics are a promising tool for strengthening immunity of the 
general population. 
Compositionality-aware approach allows a more robust investigation 
of the microbiome dynamics as well as baseline associations with a va­
riety of factors. The comparison of microbiome composition with 
macroscopic and microscopic stool analysis results suggested links to 
alterations of digestion and nutrients absorption.
