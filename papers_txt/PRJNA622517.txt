2 
Abstract 
50 
 
51 
The gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in the conversion of dietary flavonoids, which can affect their 
52 
bioavailability and bioactivity and thereby their health-promoting properties. The ability of 
53 
flavonoids to metabolically-activate the microbiota has, however, not been systematically evaluated. 
54 
In the present study, we used a fluorescence-based single-cell activity measure [biorthogonal non-
55 
canonical ammino acid-tagging (BONCAT)] combined with fluorescence-activated cell-sorting 
56 
(FACS) to determine which microorganisms are metabolically-active after amendment of the 
57 
flavonoid rutin. We performed anaerobic incubations of human fecal microbiota amended with rutin 
58 
and in the presence of the cellular activity marker L-Azidohomoalanine (AHA) to detect 
59 
metabolically-active cells. We found that 7.3% of cells in the gut microbiota were active after a 6 h 
60 
incubation and 26.9% after 24 h. We then sorted BONCAT-positive cells and observed an enrichment 
61 
of Lachnospiraceae (Lachnoclostridium, and Eisenbergiella), Enterobacteriaceae, Tannerellaceae 
62 
and Erysipelotrichaceae species in the rutin-responsive fraction of the microbiota. There was marked 
63 
inter-individual variability in the appearance of rutin conversion products after incubation with rutin. 
64 
Consistent with this, there was substantial variability in the abundance of rutin-responsive microbiota 
65 
among different individuals. Specifically, we observed that Enterobacteriaceae were associated with 
66 
conversion of rutin into quercetin-3-glucoside and Lachnospiraceae were associated with quercetin 
67 
production. This suggests that individual microbiotas differ in their ability to metabolize rutin and 
68 
utilize different conversion pathways.

Introduction 
71 
Flavonoids are a group of bioactive polyphenolic compounds present in a wide variety of plant-based 
72 
foodstuffs. Rutin (quercetin-3-O-rutinoside) is a flavonol glycoside composed of quercetin and 
73 
rutinose, a disaccharide of rhamnose and glucose, and represents the most consumed flavonol (3.75 
74 
% in Europe and in the UK) (Zamora-Ros et al., 2016). Dietary sources of rutin include tea, green 
75 
asparagus, onions, buckwheat, wine, eucalyptus, apples, as well as berries (de Araujo et al., 2013; 
76 
Kumar and Pandey, 2013; Amaretti et al., 2015). Rutin has been shown to have anti-oxidant 
77 
properties 
(Ghorbani, 
2017) 
and 
to 
exert 
anti-aging 
effects 
on human dermal 
78 
fibroblasts and human skin (Choi et al., 2016). It also has anti-neurodegenerative properties 
79 
(Enogieru et al., 2018) and exhibits protective effects against hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, liver 
80 
damage, and cardiovascular disorders (Ghorbani, 2017).  Additionally, the rutin degradation products 
81 
quercetin-3-glucoside and quercetin have been found to have anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidant and 
82 
anti-mutagenic properties (Gibellini et al., 2011; Kumar and Pandey, 2013; Hobbs et al., 2018). 
83 
Quercetin-3-glucoside also possesses  anti-hypertensive, hypolipidemic effects (Gibellini et al., 2011; 
84 
Kumar and Pandey, 2013; Hobbs et al., 2018) and quercetin has been reported to ameliorate 
85 
atherosclerosis and dyslipidemia (Salvamani et al., 2014). 
86 
 
87 
The bioavailability of these dietary flavonoids depends on intestinal absorption, which is determined 
88 
by their chemical composition and, in particular, by the nature of glycosylation (Matsumoto et al., 
89 
2004). The glyco-conjugates of quercetin are poorly absorbed in the upper intestinal tract and 
90 
accumulate in the large intestine. In the colon, members of the gut microbiota can hydrolyze rutin or 
91 
other glyco-conjugates, removing the sugar moiety and permitting the absorption of the aglycone 
92 
(Cardona et al., 2013; Amaretti et al., 2015). Therefore, the colonic microbiota is responsible for the 
93 
extensive breakdown of the original flavonoid structures into low-molecular-weight phenolic 
94 
metabolites (Cardona et al., 2013). Currently, it is estimated that 500–1000 different microbial species 
95 
inhabit the gastrointestinal tract, reaching the highest concentration in the colon (up to 1012 cells per 
96 
gram of faeces) (Thursby and Juge, 2017). Bacteria that metabolize rutin possess α-rhamnosidases 
97 
that transform rutin into quercetin-3-glucoside and/or β-glucosidases that either convert quercetin-3-
98 
glucoside into quercetin (Braune and Blaut, 2016) or convert rutin directly into quercetin (Olthof et 
99 
al., 2003). A limited number of bacteria have so-far been shown to have rutin-metabolizing 
100 
Provisional
 
3 
capabilities in pure culture. α-rhamnosidases involved in deglycosylation of flavonoids have been 
101 
characterized in Lactobacillus acidophilus, Lactobacillus plantarum (Beekwilder et al., 2009) and  
102 
Bifidobacterium dentium (Bang et al., 2015).The capability to degrade rutin into quercetin was 
103 
reported for Bacteroides uniformis, Bacteroides ovatus (Bokkenheuser et al., 1987), and 
104 
Enterococcus avium (Shin et al., 2016). Parabacteroides distasonis was shown produce both 
105 
quercetin-3-glucoside and quercetin via α-rhamnosidase and β-glucosidase activity (Bokkenheuser et 
106 
al., 1987), and Eubacterium ramulus and Enterococcus casseliflavus are able to convert quercetin-3-
107 
glucoside in quercetin (Schneider et al., 1999). 
108 
 
109 
Previous studies of rutin conversion by gut bacteria have involved screening strain collections, which 
110 
gives limited insight into identifying which bacteria are actually involved in metabolizing rutin in the 
111 
complex gut microbial community. In the present study, we identified cells in the gut microbiota that 
112 
became metabolically-active upon rutin amendment using the cellular activity marker L-
113 
Azidohomoalanine (AHA). By sorting active cells and profiling active and total communities using 
114 
16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, we were able to identify specific taxa enriched in rutin-treated 
115 
samples. We observed marked inter-individual variability in both the extent of rutin degradation 
116 
product formation as well as the abundance of the rutin-responsive microbial community. Our 
117 
findings present new insights into rutin metabolism by different microbiotas in healthy individuals, 
118 
which will be useful for future studies on flavonol metabolism in health as well as in disease 
119 
conditions.

Material and methods

123 
Sample collection  
124 
Fresh faecal samples were collected from 10 healthy subjects (7 females and 3 males, age mean±SD 
125 
:30.5±5.8; BMI:mean ±SD: 22.19±2.9). All participant followed an omnivore diet. Participants with 
126 
antibiotic, probiotic, or prebiotic usage in the previous six months were excluded. The study was 
127 
approved by, and conducted in accordance, with the University of Vienna ethics committee 
128 
(Reference number 00161) and written informed consent was signed by all enrolled participants. 
129 
 
130 
Anaerobic incubations 
131 
Fresh stool samples were immediately introduced into an anaerobic tent (85% N2, 10% CO2, 5% H2). 
132 
All reagents were introduced into the anaerobic tent two days before the experiment to ensure that 
133 
they were anaerobic by the start of the experiment. 10 ml Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added 
134 
to 1 g of faecal sample. The suspension was homogenized by vigorous shaking and vortexing. 
135 
Samples were left for 15 minutes to allow large particles to settle and subsequentally serially-diluted 
136 
1:10 twice. The serial 1:10 dilutions were necessary to avoid background noise and autofluorescence 
137 
in the Cy5 (BONCAT) signal. Samples were incubated in autoclaved Hungate tubes in the presence 
138 
of 1 mM of the non-canonical amino acid L-azidohomoalanine (AHA) (baseclick GmbH, Germany) 
139 
and 500µM rutin dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich). A negative control 
140 
containing DMSO and a positive control with 2 mg/ml of glucose were used for each experiment. An 
141 
abiotic control for each time point was included to assess the chemical stability of rutin under the 
142 
incubation conditions. Samples were incubated under anaerobic condition with a final volume of 5 
143 
ml for 0, 6 or 24 hours. Subsequently, samples were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min and the 
144 
supernatant was collected and diluted with equal volume of pure acetonitrile (ACN) in order to 
145 
stabilize the supernatant samples and then stored at -20 C for LC-HRMS analysis. Part of the sample 
146 
was frozen for nucleic acid extraction and part was washed twice in PBS and then fixed in 1:1 
147 
ethanol:PBS for FACS sorting.

Liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) 
152 
Acetonitrile (LC-MS grade) and formic acid were purchased from VWR. High purity water (18,2 
153 
MW cm-1) was used for dilution of samples and the preparation of mobile phases (Milli-Q 
154 
Synergy/Elix water purification system, Merck). Authentic reference standards of rutin, quercetin-3-
155 
O-glucoside and quercetin were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (> 94%, HPLC) and Extrasynthese 
156 
(> 99%, HPLC) respectively. Supernatants from the incubations were immediately centrifuged and 
157 
aliquots for HPLC analysis was removed and equal volume of ACN was added. Samples were kept 
158 
frozen until analysis. The ACN-stabilized samples were thawed and homogenized by vortexing. 100 
159 
µl was diluted 1:4 in water to decrease acetonitrile content to 10%. The diluted sample was filtered 
160 
through a 0.22 µm pore polytetrafluoroethylene syringe filter (Cronus, LabHut Ltd.), and 5 uL were 
161 
injected into the LC system. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Phenomenex Kinetex 
162 
EVO C18 100 x 2.1 mm, 2.6 µm column utilizing an Agilent 1200 HPLC system. The column was 
163 
operated at 30°C. The binary mobile phase consisted of H2O with 0.1% formic acid (eluent A) and 
164 
acetonitrile (eluent B). The flow rate was set to 0.4 mL min-1. Gradient separation was started at 5% 
165 
B and linearly increased to reach 90% in 9 min. The eluent was kept constant at 90% B until 11.5 
166 
min and then the column was re-equilibrated at the initial conditions for 11.5 min. The effluent of the 
167 
LC system was connected to an Agilent 6530 high-resolution, accurate mass quadrupole/time-of-
168 
flight mass spectrometer equipped with a dual sprayer electrospray ion source (ESI-Q/TOFMS). The 
169 
mass spectrometry was run in full scan (MS-only) mode scanning from m/z 50-1700 in negative 
170 
ionization mode. A continuous reference mass correction was applied using purine and HP-921 
171 
(Hexakis(1H,1H,3H-perfluoropropoxy)phosphazene) as reference substances. The ion source 
172 
temperature was maintained at 325 °C and capillary and fragmentor voltages were set to -4000 V and 
173 
140 V, respectively. The Mass Hunter Workstation software package (B02.01) was used for data 
174 
acquisition and data evaluation. Quantification of rutin, quercetin glucoside and quercetin were 
175 
performed with external calibration using reference standards.

BONCAT labelling of microbial cells 
178 
Cu(I)-catalyzed click labelling of chemically-fixed microbial cells was performed on slides as 
179 
described previously  (Hatzenpichler et al., 2014). Briefly, fixed samples were immobilized on glass 
180 
slides, dried in a 46°C hybridization oven, and dehydrated and permeabilized by placing slides for 3 
181 
min sequentially in 50, 80 and 96% ethanol. Then, 1.25 µl of 20 mM CuSO4, 2.50 µl of 50 mM 
182 
tris[(1-hydroxypropyl-1H-1,2,3-triazol-4-yl)methyl]amine (THPTA) (baseclick GmbH , Germany), 
183 
and 0.30 µl of alkyne dye (in DMSO) (Jena Bioscience, Germany) were mixed and allowed to react 
184 
for 3 min at room temperature (RT) in the dark. In the meantime, 12.5 µl of freshly-prepared 100 mM 
185 
sodium ascorbate (Sigma-Aldrich) and 12.5 µl of 100 mM aminoguanidine hydrochloride (Sigma-
186 
Aldrich) were added to 221 µl 1× PBS (pH 7.4). Then, the dye premix was added to this solution, the 
187 
tube inverted once and samples were covered by 30 µl of solution. Slides were transferred into a 
188 
humid chamber and incubated in the dark at RT for 30 min. Afterwards, slides were washed three 
189 
times for 3 min each in 1× PBS and then treated with an increasing ethanol series (3 min each in 50, 
190 
80 and 96% ethanol) and air-dried (Hatzenpichler et al., 2014).  1:1000 DNA stain, 4’, 6 diamidino-
191 
2-phenylindole (DAPI) solution, (in PBS) was applied for 5 min and then slides were washed in cold 
192 
MILLI-Q water (Millipore GmbH, Vienna, Austria). Samples were embedded with CitiFluor (Agar 
193 
Scientific Ltd., Stansted, UK) if used immediately or stored at -20°C. Representative BONCAT 
194 
pictures of a faecal sample incubated with rutin at 6 and 24h and a negative control containing 
195 
dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) are shown in Supplementary Figure 1. Representative BONCAT 
196 
picture of a faecal sample incubated with the glucose positive control is show in Supplementary 
197 
Figure 2. For FACS sorting, in-solution click labelling was performed immediately before FACS 
198 
sorting. For click labelling, 300-500 µl fixed samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min and 
199 
re-suspended in 96% ethanol. The pellet was left for 3 min at RT, then centrifuged 10,000 rpm for 5 
200 
min. A master mix containing the dye solution was prepared as described above. Samples were 
201 
suspended in 60-100 µl of solution and incubated in the dark at RT for 30 min. Afterwards, samples 
202

were washed three times by centrifugation with 1x PBS (Hatzenpichler et al., 2014). Immediately 
203 
before sorting, samples were filtered with a 35µm nylon mesh using BD tubes 12x75mm (BD,

Germany).

Fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS) 
213 
For flow cytometry sorting, bacteria were labeled in Cy5 dye as previously described, and analyzed 
214 
on an ultra high-speed cell sorter MoFlo Astrios EQ (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) using the 
215 
Summit v6.2 software (Beckman Coulter). To standardize the daily measurement and to assess the 
216 
size of the bacteria, calibration beads (silica beads 100, 500 and 1000 nm, Kisker Biotech, Steinfurt, 
217 
Germany) having a refractive index close to biological material were recorded. The sorting of Cy5-
218 
labeled bacteria was performed as follows: In a first scatter plot, the 561nm SSC Height-Log 
219 
parameter was set vs. the 488nm FSC1 Height-Log parameter. To reduce electronic noise the 
220 
triggering signal was set on the 561nm SSC parameter. A second dot plot 488nm FSC1-Height-Log 
221 
vs. 488nm SSC-Height-Log showed in a first measurement the different sizes of the silica beads and 
222 
in the following measurements the scattering of the bacteria. Bacteria were then pre-gated and 
223 
displayed on a third scatter plot with 488nm SSC area log axes vs. 640nm 671/30-Area-Log axes. 
224 
Cy5-positive bacteria were then sorted out into tubes with a maximum event rate of 50,000 events 
225 
per second. Reanalysis of the samples showed a purity of> 99%. A overview of the gating strategy 
226 
and FACS selectivity analysis are shown in Supplementary Figure 3 and 4. In order to assess the 
227 
technical reproducibility of the method, absolute cell count was performed with the cell sorter FACS 
228 
Melody (BD, Germany) and BONCAT-positive cells were counted in triplicate for each sample 
229 
(Supplementary Figure 5A and B). Absolute counting beads (CountBright™, Invitrogen, 
230 
ThermoFisher Scientific, Austria) were used for cell counts according to the manufacturer’s 
231 
instructions.

DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing 
234 
DNA extraction was performed for both total microbial community and the FACS-sorted fraction 
235 
using the QiAmp mini DNA extraction kit (Qiagen) following the protocols for bacteria according to 
236 
the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR amplification was performed with a two-step barcoding 
237 
approach according to Herbold et al, 2015 using 16S rRNA gene primers targeting most bacteria (V3-
238 
V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene) (S-D-Bact-0341-b-S-17 [5’-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3’] and 
239 
S-D-Bact-0785-a-A-21 [5’-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3’]. The barcoded amplicons were 
240 
purified with ZR-96 DNA Clean-up Kit (Zymo Research, USA) and quantified using the Quant-iTTM 
241 
PicoGreen® dsDNA Assay (Invitrogen, USA) (Herbold et al., 2015). An equimolar library was 
242 
constructed by pooling samples, and the resulting library was sent for sequencing on the Illumina 
243 
MiSeq platform at Microsynth AG (Balgach, Switzerland).

Sequence processing and data analysis 
246 
16S rRNA gene sequence data were sorted into libraries according to Herbold et al. (2015) and 
247 
processed into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using the Divisive Amplicon Denoising 
248 
Algorithm (DADA2) (Callahan et al., 2016) and classified using the RDP classifier (Wang et al., 
249 
2007) as implemented in Mothur (Schloss et al., 2009). Sequencing libraries were subsampled to a 
250 
smaller number of reads than the smallest library (subsampled to 1000 reads) to avoid biases related 
251 
to uneven library depth. 1,000 reads were sufficient to maintain a high coverage per library (mean 
252 
Good’s coverage: 99%). 16S rRNA gene sequence data has been deposited in the NCBI Short Read 
253 
Provisional
 
6 
Archive under PRJNA622517. Statistical analysis was performed using R statistical software 
254 
(https://www.r-project.org/). Statistical analysis to compare samples groups was performed using 
255 
ANOVA, and with the R package DEseq2 (Love et al., 2014). The statistical significance of factors 
256 
affecting microbiota composition was evaluated using non-parametric permutational multivariate 
257 
analysis of variance (perMANOVA), significant clustering of groups was evaluated with analysis of 
258 
similarities (ANOSIM), ordination was performed using redundancy analysis (RDA) and non-metric 
259 
multidimensional scaling (NMDS) in the vegan package in R (Oksanen et al., 2010). Relative 
260 
standard deviation (RSD) was calculated as standard deviation/mean to dermine inter-individual 
261 
variability across participants. Microbial community variation within participant was further 
262 
determined using the UpSetR package in R, which employs a matrix-based layout to show 
263 
intersections of sets and their sizes (Conway et al., 2017). Alpha and beta diversity metrics were also 
264 
calculated with the vegan package. Variables are expressed as mean ± SD (standard deviation). A 
265 
probability value (p-value) less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant and p-values were 
266 
adjusted with the False Discovery Rate method (FDR) in the case of multiple comparison. Statistical 
267 
analysis to compare producer groups was performed using ANOVA and Tukey test for multiple 
268 
comparisons.

Screening bacterial genomes for alpha-rhamnosidase genes 
271 
Uniprot (https://www.uniprot.org) was queried using “alpha-L-rhamnosidase” as a query and filtered 
272 
for “reviewed” entries. Entries were manually examined for confirmation of activity and to compile 
273 
pfam motifs (El-Gebali et al., 2019). A single pfam motif, Bac_rhamnosid6H (PF17389) was 
274 
common to all genes with confirmed alpha-rhamnosidase activity. Uniprot was subsequently queried 
275 
using PF17389 and filtered for “reviewed” entries to determine specificity. All reviewed entries for 
276 
PF17389 possessed confirmed alpha-rhamnosidase activity. All publicly-available genomes available 
277 
at NCBI as September 24, 2020 (N=268,330) were screened using PF17389 with hmmsearch 
278 
(hmmer3 , hmmer.org) and genes matching the pfam (E<10-6 and alignment over at least 300 amino 
279 
acids) were extracted from the genomes. All extracted genes were back-screened against the Pfam-A 
280 
database with hmmscan (hmmer3) and only those genes that matched PF17389 as a best-hit were 
281 
retained as putative alpha-rhamnosidase. Genus-level assignments were inferred by string-matching 
282 
genus labels (e.g. Escherichia) to the organism name associated to each searched genome.

Results

287 
Biotransformation of rutin by the gut microbiota 
288 
In order to characterize the biotransformation of rutin by the gut microbiota and to identify 
289 
metabolically-active bacteria after rutin amendment, we performed anaerobic incubations of freshly-
290 
collected stool contents amended with 500µM rutin (Figure 1). This concentration is in line with 
291 
previous studies and is consistent with a reasonable dietary intake (Amaretti et al., 2015; Zamora-
292 
Ros et al., 2016). Over the course of the incubations there was a slight but not statistically-significant 
293 
reduction in rutin (one-way ANOVA, p = 0.41, n = 10, molar recovery: 97%; Figure 2A), probably 
294 
caused by the high dilution applied in the anaerobic incubation. However, Q-glc (quercetin-3-
295 
glucoside) and Q (quercetin) appeared after incubation with biomass (p = 0.019 and 0.036 for Q-glc 
296 
and Q, respectively, n = 10; Figures 2B and C), indicating that rutin was actively transformed by the 
297 
gut microbiota. Low levels of Q-glc were present in all time zero samples (Figure 2B), which may 
298 
be because rutin preparations were either not completely purified or chemically degraded to Q-glc 
299 
during storage. Interestingly, there was substantial variation in the amount of Q-glc and Q formed 
300 
during incubation with the gut microbiota, suggesting considerable inter-individual variability in the 
301 
capacity to metabolize rutin by different gut microbial communities (Figure 2D and E). Moreover, 
302 
we have screened the samples for some known quercetin metabolites (dihydroxyphenyl propionic 
303 
acid hydroxyphenylacetic acid, dihydroxyphenylacetic acid, dihydroxybenzoic acid, hydroxybenzoic 
304 
Provisional
 
7 
acid) and only a few samples showed hits and only with minor signals. Therefore, these hits and 
305 
suspected metabolites were not included in the data evaluation.  
306 
 
307 
The core metabolically-active microbiota in rutin amendments 
308 
As the capacity of dietary polyphenols to modulate the activity and/or composition of the gut 
309 
microbiota is still poorly understood, we aimed to identify the metabolically-active microbial taxa 
310 
upon rutin amendment. Rutin amendment activated a subset of the microbiota, and active cells were 
311 
detected in almost all samples after 6 h of incubation with rutin, with an increasing number after 24 
312 
h (6h: 7.3 ± 7.3%, 24h: 26.9 ± 11.7% [mean ±SD]; t-test: p = 0.0003, n = 20) (Figure 3A,B). The 
313 
diversity and the composition of the total microbial community did not change significantly during 
314 
the short-term incubations (Figures 4A,B; perMANOVA, p = 0.99), indicating that the applied 
315 
incubation conditions did not appreciably modify the composition of the microbiota (i.e. there was 
316 
no strong “bottle effect”). However, the rutin-active fraction of the community, as determined by 
317 
BONCAT activity labeling and FACS sorting, was significantly different from the total community. 
318 
The diversity of the active fraction was lower than the total community (ANOVA, p < 0.0001 for the 
319 
alpha diversity metrics; Figure 4A), and the NMDS ordination showed a clear separation of samples 
320 
between the total community and the active fraction (perMANOVA, p < 0.0001; ANOSIM, p = 
321 
0.001) (Figure 4B). The most abundant taxa detected in the active fraction were Bacteroidaceae, 
322 
Enterobacteriaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Tannerellaceae, and Ruminococcaceae (Figure 5). We found 
323 
that members of the Enterobacteriaceae (Escherichia/Shigella), Tannerellaceae (Parabacteroides), 
324 
Erysipelotrichaceae (Erysipelatoclostridium), and Lachnospiraceae (Lachnoclostridium and 
325 
Eisenbergiella) were significantly increased in the active fraction compared to their respective total 
326 
community samples (negative binomial distribution, Wald test, p<0.05, n=20). We then determined 
327 
the prevalence of putative alpha-rhamnosidases (Bac_rhamnosid6H [PF17389]) in publicly-available 
328 
genomes belonging to genera that comprised the core rutin-active community.  
329 
We further determined the proportion of database genomes that encoded a-Rhamnosidase for rutin-
330 
active genera (Supplementary Figure 6A) and non-active genera (Supplementary Figure 6B) and, 
331 
in agreement with our experimental results, we found that putative alpha-rhamnosidases are common 
332 
in genomes belonging genera that responded to the rutin amendment. We then used a nonparametric 
333 
test to show that genomes randomly drawn from rutin-active genera would be expected to contain an 
334 
a-Rhamnosidase more often than genomes randomly drawn from rutin-nonactive genera (Mann-
335 
Whitney one-tailed test, p=0.041, Supplementary Figure 6C), suggesting that the active genera are 
336 
more likely to have database representatives that contain a-Rhamnosidase. 
337 
 
338 
Microbial community composition is associated with rutin transformation patterns 
339 
We observed that there was a large variability in the ASVs that were enriched in the active fraction 
340 
of the community across the incubations from different donors. Only 13/97 and 23/91 ASVs were 
341 
shared among all incubations at 6 h and 24 h, respectively (Supplementary Figure 7A,B). We 
342 
confirmed that the technical variability associated with the BONCAT procedure was much smaller 
343 
than the observed biological variability (ANOVA; Technical variation: r2=0.00085, p=0.878; 
344 
Biological variation: r2=0.999, p<0.0001; Supplementary Figure 5A and B). We compared the 
345 
inter-individual variability in the fraction of cells metabolically-activated by rutin with glucose, a 
346 
compound that can be fermented by a large fraction of the gut microbiota, and found that inter-
347 
individual variability was higher in rutin amendments than glucose amendments (RSD 24h rutin: 
348 
0.43, RSD 24h glucose: 0.22; Figure 3A, Supplementary Figure 2). Consistent with this, we found 
349 
that the microbial community varied significantly within participant in both the total community and 
350 
active fraction (perMANOVA: p <0.0001, Supplementary Figure 7C,D), which was in line with the 
351 
observation that different cell morphologies were observed in the active fraction from different 
352 
donors (Figure 3B).  Though stool incubations from all participants led to the production of rutin 
353 
degradation products, the pattern of rutin product formation was markedly different among different 
354 
participants. We therefore divided the samples into the following groups: "High Q-glc producers", 
355 
Provisional
 
8 
"High Q producers", and "Low producers". Based on these categories, we observed a significant 
356 
clustering of both total community and active fraction samples in the redundancy analysis ordination 
357 
(perMANOVA, total community: p = 0.001, active fraction: p = 0.001) (Figure 6A,B). In the active 
358 
fraction, we observed an enrichment of Enterobacteriaceae (Escherichia-Shigella) in High Q-glc 
359 
producers, and this taxon represented the majority of sequences recovered in the active fraction by 
360 
24 h (ANOVA: 0.0002, n=50). In contrast, High Q producers had an enrichment of Lachnospiraceae 
361 
(Lachnoclostridium and Eisenbergiella), which was the dominant taxon in the active fraction by 24 
362 
h (ANOVA: 0.0257, n=50). Low producers had a trend, though not statistically significant, towards 
363 
enrichment in Tannerellaceae (Parabacteroides) and Erysipelotricaceae (Erysipelatoclostridium) in 
364 
the active fraction (Figure 6C, Supplementary Figures 6 and 8).

366 
Discussion 
367 
The gut microbiota plays a key role in the conversion of dietary flavonoids. Though conversion of 
368 
flavonoids by the microbiota has gained increasing interest (Braune and Blaut, 2016), key microbial 
369 
players in flavonoid metabolism remain poorly characterized (Cardona et al., 2013). In this study, we 
370 
investigated metabolism of the flavonoid rutin by the human gut microbiota. Rutin conversion 
371 
products were detected in all tested donor stool incubations, but there was a dramatic variation in the 
372 
amount of Q-glc and Q produced by different microbiotas. This suggests inter-individual variability 
373 
in preference or capability for rutin metabolism. This is in line with a previous report of high person-
374 
to-person variation in the concentration of phenolic acids between 2 and 24 h incubation of stool with 
375 
rutin (Jaganath et al., 2009). 
376 
 
377 
Alpha diversity was lower in the active fraction compared to the total community, implying that a 
378 
subset of the community is metabolically-activated by rutin. Indeed, we were able to identify a 
379 
percentage of 7.3 and 26.9% at time 6 and 24h, respectively, of active cells with respect to the control 
380 
without any amendment. Translational activity was likely still possible by these cells in the absence 
381 
of additional amino acids in these short incubations via recycling of internal amino acids 
382 
(Hatzenpichler et al., 2016) 
383 
In our study, the significant bacterial taxa selected are: Lachnospiraceae (Lachnoclostridium, 
384 
Eisenbergiella), Enterobacteriaceae (Escherichia), Tannerellaceae (Parabacteroides), and 
385 
Erysipelotricaceae (Erysipelatoclostridium). These bacteria may represent a “core rutin-selected 
386 
microbiota” in healthy individuals. Members of the Enterobacteriaceae (Escherichia coli, 
387 
Escherichia fergusonii, and Enterobacter cloacae) have been previously implicated in the O-
388 
deglycosylation and dehydroxylation of different flavonoids (Miyake et al., 1997; Hur et al., 2000; 
389 
Zhao et al., 2014; Braune and Blaut, 2016). In a batch culture fermentation experiment, Tzounis et 
390 
al. found that catechin, a flavan-3-ol monomers, promoted the growth of  E. coli (Tzounis et al., 2008) 
391 
and Duda-Chodak found that rutin does not inhibit E.coli growth (Duda-Chodak, 2012). In 
392 
accordance with our findings, Parabacteroides distasonis has been shown to produce both quercetin-
393 
3-glucoside and quercetin (Bokkenheuser et al., 1987) and the Lachnospiraceae member Blautia and 
394 
Eubacterium were able to convert rutin in quercetin (Kim et al., 2014) and quercetin-3-glucoside in 
395 
quercetin, respectively. Based on rutin degradation capability and the identification of the core-rutin 
396 
selective microbiota, we divided the donor microbiotas into high Q-glc, high Q, and low producers. 
397 
We observed that members of Enterobacteriaceae (Escherichia) were associated Q-glc production, 
398 
putatively due to expression of alpha-rhamnosidases that can act on rutin. We also found that 
399 
Lachnospiraceae (Lachnoclostridium and Eisenbergiella) were associated with Q production, which 
400 
may be due to expression of beta-rutinosidase enzymes or a combination of alpha-rhamnosidase and 
401 
beta-glucosidase enzymes. In agreement with our experimental results, we found that putative alpha-
402 
rhamnosidases are common in genomes belonging genera that responded to the rutin amendment.  
403 
We did not screen for beta-rutinosidase and beta-glucosidase due to a lack of diagnostic bioinformatic 
404 
tools for confident general inference of these activities. Beta-rutinosidase has not been identified in 
405 
bacteria. The closest matches in Bacteria (by BLAST) of confirmed fungal beta-rutinosidases have 
406 
Provisional
 
9 
only weak homology (<30% identity) and are annotated as cellulases or cellulase-like enzymes. In 
407 
addition, the carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZY) database lists nine glycoside hydrolase families 
408 
with individual members that have been shown to possess beta-glucoside activity (EC 3.2.1.21). 
409 
However each of the nine families also contain members that don’t have known beta-glucosidase 
410 
activity. Consequently, it is unclear which of the beta-glucosidases that are listed in CAZY will cleave 
411 
glucose off of Q-glc specifically.  
412 
 
413 
According to our findings, we hypothesize that inter-individual variability in rutin metabolism is 
414 
driven by differences in the composition of the gut microbiota. Variation in rutin and other flavonoid 
415 
metabolisms in humans may also be caused by host and environmental factors as diet and genetic 
416 
polymorphism and differences in enzymatic activity (Almeida et al., 2018). In conclusion, individual 
417 
microbiotas exert distinct capability in rutin utilization, showing a higher response in certain 
418 
individuals, whereas others seem less capable in rutin utilization. Future research that takes into 
419 
account functional gene analysis, diet, and host physiology will advance our understanding of the 
420 
role of the gut microbiota in rutin degradation and provide opportunities to improve human health.
