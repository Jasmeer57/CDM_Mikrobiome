2

3

4

5

6

7

INTRODUCTION 
43 
Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is the most common cause of liver disease in the 
44 
pediatric population, affecting approximately 3 in 10 obese children worldwide (1). The disease 
45 
is characterized by macrovesicular steatosis in more than 5% of hepatocytes in an individual 
46 
without significant history of alcohol intake or other liver pathologies (2,3). Notably, NAFLD 
47 
drastically affects lipid and glucose metabolism, increasing the risk to insulin resistance and 
48 
dyslipidemia early in life (4,5). While the pathophysiology of NAFLD is unclear, genetic 
49 
contributions and gut microbiota are considered to be important players.  
50 
One of the strongest genetic contributors of NAFLD is a variant in the Patatin-like 
51 
phospholipase 3 gene (PNPLA3), a gene encoding for a protein called adiponutrin, which has 
52 
been found to confer susceptibility to increased hepatic fat levels and hepatic inflammation (6). 
53 
The PNPLA3 rs738409 variant is a single-nucleotide polymorphism in which there is a change 
54 
from cytosine to guanine that leads to the amino acid substitution of isoleucine to methionine at 
55 
the 148 position of the coding sequence, in the active site of the enzyme (6,7). Although the 
56 
association between the PNPLA3 gene and NAFLD has been known for almost a decade, the 
57 
physiologic role of adiponutrin and the mechanisms by which the rs738409 variant predisposes 
58 
NAFLD remain unclear (8,9). PNPLA3 rs738409 has been observed to confer predisposition to 
59 
hepatic steatosis in obese youth without increasing the level of insulin resistance (10). Allele 
60 
frequency of the PNPLA3 rs738409 risk allele is lower in African Americans, thus African 
61 
American obese youth possess some protection from liver steatosis (11,12).  
62 
 
Gut microbiota dysbiosis has been associated with obesity and, most recently, NAFLD 
63 
(13,14). This association has been attributed, in part, to disruption of gut epithelial tight junctions 
64 
through which bacteria and toxins gain access to the portal circulation and release inflammatory

5
cytokines to promote hepatic steatosis and inflammation (15). To our knowledge, only three 
66 
studies have investigated the gut microbiota composition in obese children with and without 
67 
NAFLD, which controls for the obesity component that is associated with gut microbiota 
68 
dysbiosis. Del Chierico et al. (16) observed increased hierarchy of ecological diversity in obese 
69 
pediatric patients compared to pediatric patients with non-alcoholic fatty liver (NAFL). The 
70 
researchers did not find significant differences between the two groups in the bacterial 
71 
abundance at the species and genus levels. In contrast, Michail et al. (17) noted that obese 
72 
children with NAFLD had more abundant Gammaproteobacteria and Prevotella compared to 
73 
obese children without NAFLD. In the Michail et al. study, no difference between the groups, in 
74 
Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes or their ratio, was detected. Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes have 
75 
become two phyla of interest after Gordon and colleagues discovered that Firmicutes 
76 
abundance increases while Bacteroidetes abundance decreases in obese subjects, as 
77 
compared to lean subjects (18,19). Schwimmer et al. (20) noted an association between NAFLD 
78 
and intestinal dysbiosis. The researchers observed lower alpha diversity in children with NAFLD 
79 
compared to the control group and more severe fibrosis in children with higher abundance of 
80 
Prevotella copri. These previous studies that assessed the effect of microbiota on NAFLD did 
81 
not account for the role of PNPLA3. In our study, we aimed to explore the potential relationship 
82 
between gut microbiota and NAFLD in a group of obese youth, while also considering the role of 
83 
PNPLA3 rs738409.

METHODS 
91 
Study cohort 
92 
A total of 73 obese (BMI ≥ 95th percentile) children and adolescents were recruited from the 
93 
Yale Pediatric Obesity Clinic. All participants completed an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) 
94 
to assess glucose metabolism and a fast-magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) abdominal scan to 
95 
assess body fat partitioning and intrahepatic fat content. In addition, fasting blood samples were 
96 
collected at the OGTT visit to obtain liver function tests, a lipid panel, and a genetic test. 
97 
Metabolic studies were conducted at the Yale Center for Clinical Investigation at 0800 h after a 
98 
12-hour overnight fast. A stool sample from each subject was also collected. Exclusion criteria 
99 
included known hepatic diseases (except for NAFLD), alcohol consumption, smoking, and 
100 
medication use. Written parental informed consent and written child assent were obtained from 
101 
all participants. Yale University Human Investigation Committee approved the study.  
102 
Imaging 
103 
Measurement of liver fat content was performed by MRI using the two-point Dixon method, and 
104 
hepatic fat fraction (HFF) was calculated from the mean pixel signal intensity data. MRI studies 
105 
were performed on a GE or Siemens Sonata 1.5 Tesla system.

Genotyping 
108 
Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes from 8 cc of whole blood using 
109 
the guanidine HCl DNA extraction protocol. To genotype the rs738409 SNP, the following pair of 
110 
primers was used: forward = 5′-GCCCTGCTCACTTGGAGAAA-3′ and reverse = 5′-
111 
TGAAAGGCAGTGAGGCATGG-3′. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using an 
112 
annealing temperature of 65.0 °C and PCR products were analyzed by automatic sequencing at 
113 
the Yale W.M. Keck Facility. 
114 
Microbial DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene sequencing 
115 
Samples were processed in two separate batches; the larger subset (60 samples) in 2016 and 
116 
the smaller subset (13 samples) in 2019. DNA was extracted using the MoBio PowerMag Soil 
117 
96-well kit (MoBio Laboratories, Carlsbad, California) for the larger subset of samples. The 
118 
smaller subset samples were extracted using the DNeasy PowerSoil Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, 
119 
Germany), as the MoBio kit was no longer available. Based on available information, the two 
120 
kits only differ in the method of purification. The lysis step in both methods is identical, including 
121 
size of beads for mechanical lysis and the composition of the lysis buffer.  
122 
Using either the MoBio PowerMag Soil 96 well kit or the Qiagen DNeasy PowerSoil kit, 
123 
DNA was extracted from 0.25g of fecal sample. DNA extracts were quantified using the Quant-
124 
iT PicoGreen kit (Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific) for the larger subset of samples and the 
125 
Qubit High Sensitivity kit (Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific) for the smaller subset. The V4 
126 
region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers described in Caporaso et al. (21) with 
127 
dual-indexed adapters for indexing and 30 ng of input DNA. Samples were amplified in triplicate 
128 
using Phusion High-Fidelity PCR master mix (New England BioLabs, Ipswich, MA) with the 
129 
addition of 1 µg BSA (New England BioLabs, Ipswich, MA). The PCR reaction was incubated at 
130 
95˚C for 3.5 min, 30 cycles of 30s at 95°C, 30s at 50°C and 90s at 72°C, followed by final

149

150

151

152

153

Statistical analyses 
156 
The distribution of continuous variables was evaluated for skewness and kurtosis, and by using 
157 
a histogram with the normal curve imposed. When appropriate, non-parametric tests were 
158 
utilized. When needed, data were transformed into ranks to reduce the influence of outliers. 
159 
Subjects were divided into two groups, HFF < 5.5% (n= 29) and HFF ≥ 5.5% (n= 44). Student’s 
160 
t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, and Chi-square tests were utilized to evaluate differences in the 
161 
characteristics of the two groups. The following characteristics were compared using Student’s 
162 
t-tests (parametric) between the two HFF groups: age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), 
163 
BMI z-score, 2-hour glucose, cholesterol, HDL, LDL, HFF, and visceral fat. Mann-Whitney U 
164 
tests (non-parametric) were utilized for the following characteristics: fasting glucose, fasting 
165 
insulin, whole-body insulin sensitivity index (WBISI), triglycerides, ALT, and subcutaneous fat. 
166 
Chi-square tests were utilized to evaluate differences between the two groups in sex, race, and 
167 
PNPLA3 genotype. When appropriate, data are presented as mean (standard deviation). 
168 
Shannon-Wiener diversity index values, an alpha diversity measure that accounts for the 
169 
number of species and evenness of their abundance, were calculated for all stool samples using 
170 
the R package phyloseq. The values were compared between the two HFF groups using 
171 
Welch’s t-test, to account for unequal variances between the two groups. The abundance of 
172 
each bacterial phyla and genera was compared between the two HFF groups using Student’s t-
173 
test or Mann-Whitney U test. Phyla where a phylum did not reach 1% abundance in at least one 
174 
sample was excluded from the analysis. Therefore, eight out of 13 phyla were included in the 
175 
analysis. Genera in which a genus did not reach 1% abundance in at least one sample was not 
176 
included in the analysis. Thus, 51 out of 125 phyla were included in the analysis. The 
177 
abundance of each phylum and genus was compared between the two groups using Mann-
178 
Whitney U test for all phyla and genera, except for Firmicutes and Faecalibacterium, in which 
179 
Student’s t-tests were used.

191

192 
were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of age (p= 0.616), sex 
193

DISCUSSION 
269 
Our data substantiate recent findings that NAFLD is associated with changes of the intestinal 
270 
microbiota. Subjects with NAFLD were found to have decreased bacterial diversity and richness 
271 
compared to those without NAFLD. Additionally, we observed a significantly higher Firmicutes to 
272 
Bacteroidetes ratio and lower abundance of Bacteroidetes, Prevotella, Gemmiger, and 
273 
Oscillospira in the group with NAFLD, compared to the group without NAFLD. When controlling 
274 
for PNPLA3 genotype, race, and BMI z-score, regression analyses demonstrated that F/B ratio, 
275 
Bacteroidetes, Gemmiger, and Oscillospira were all individually associated with HFF. These 
276

regression models with PNPLA3 genotype, race, BMI z-score, and F/B ratio, Bacteroidetes, 
277 
Gemmiger, or Oscillospira explained over 20% of the variation in HFF. We also discovered an 
278 
additive effect on HFF from PNPLA3 and Gemmiger, and PNPLA3 and Oscillospira.  
279 
Our results on fecal microbiome alpha diversity agree with those of Del Chierico et al. 
280 
(16) and Schwimmer et al. (20), where participants with NAFLD also showed decreased 
281 
bacterial diversity compared to participants without NAFLD (p<0.05). Since the gut microbiota 
282 
protects the host from colonization and invasion of other pathogens, having a greater bacterial 
283 
diversity may better protect against injurious colonization of other pathogens and prevent 
284 
bacteria and toxins from gaining access to the portal circulation and releasing inflammatory 
285 
cytokines in the liver. For over a decade, studies of lean and obese mice have proposed that gut 
286 
microbiota dysbiosis may increase energy harvest from the diet and alter how this energy is 
287 
utilized and stored (18, 36). In pediatrics, the gut microbiota of obese youth has been observed 
288 
to have a higher capacity to oxidize carbohydrates, as compared to lean youth (37). Therefore, 
289 
it is a possibility that the bacteria found in obese children with NAFLD have greater pathogenic 
290 
or opportunistic tendencies and outcompeted bacteria that were more beneficial to the host. For 
291 
example, in our obese participants with NAFLD, we observed a decreased abundance of 
292 
Bacteroidetes, which has repeatedly been found in greater abundance in lean subjects 
293 
compared to obese subjects (18,19).  
294 
We observed a significantly lower abundance of Oscillospira in the group with NAFLD, 
295 
compared to the group without NAFLD, which is consistent with the findings from the Michail et 
296 
al. (17) study. In the study, an Oscillospira decrease coupled to up-regulation of 2-butanone was 
297 
identified as signature of NAFLD onset, although this was based on comparisons with lean 
298 
controls. While the abundance of Oscillospira was higher in obese children without NAFLD 
299 
compared to those with NAFLD, the study does not disclose if this difference was significant. 
300 
Our multiple linear regression model, which controlled for PNPLA3 genotype, race, and BMI z-

This could be due to having a greater sample size to make statistical inferences. Additionally, 
307 
Del Chierico et al. (16) reported a higher abundance of Bacteroidetes in children with NAFL 
308 
compared to obese children, similar to our observations, but the difference was not statistically 
309 
significant. The differences in results could also be explained by our use of ASVs (Amplicon 
310 
Sequence Variants) as opposed to OTUs (Operational Taxonomic Units), which were utilized in 
311 
Del Chierico et al. (16). It has been suggested that ASVs provide higher resolution and better 
312 
accuracy than OTUs, thus, in some cases, allowing for additional genus and species level 
313 
assignments to be made (23). Additionally, the choice of hypervariable region has been shown 
314 
to have a significant effect on accurately determining diversity and resolution for microbiome 
315 
profiling studies (38,39), making it difficult to compare results when different regions are used. 
316 
Primer biases can also play a role when different hypervariable regions are targeted, excluding 
317 
or overrepresenting particular taxa (40). Michail et al. (17) used a DNA microarray for 16S rRNA 
318 
profiling but this approach has its own limitations, namely the fact that the microarray itself is 
319 
based on known sequence variants. This raises the potential for missing diversity that would 
320 
otherwise be detected by Next Generation Sequencing (41). Additionally, environmental factors 
321 
specific to our study population, such as diet, could account, at least in part, for our findings.  
322 
While Schwimmer et al. (20) also found a significant difference in the abundance of 
323 
Bacteroidetes between the two groups, the direction of the relationship was opposite to that of 
324 
our results. The researchers observed greater abundance of Bacteroidetes in obese children 
325 
with NAFLD compared to those without NAFLD, while we observed higher abundance of 
326

Bacteroidetes in the group without NAFLD. Additionally, Schwimmer et al. (20) found that high 
327 
abundance of Prevotella copri was associated with more severe fibrosis, while our study noticed 
328 
lower abundance of Prevotella in the group with NAFLD, compared to the group without NAFLD. 
329 
When we accounted for PNPLA3 genotype, Prevotella was no longer predictive of NAFLD 
330 
status. The discrepancy between our findings and that of Schwimmer et al. (20) could possibly 
331 
be explained by differences in the methods of stool DNA extraction and downstream analysis. In 
332 
their study, the stool samples were placed in RNAlater and rocked overnight at 4C and stored 
333 
at –80C until isolation (20). In contrast, our samples were collected and directly stored at –20C 
334 
until isolation. A study by Bahl et al. (42) revealed that in seven of nine cases, the Firmicutes to 
335 
Bacteroidetes ratio was significantly higher in fecal samples that had been frozen compared to 
336 
identical samples that had not. According to the researchers, in order to make comparisons 
337 
within a study, all samples should be frozen in a similar manner, which was the case with our 
338 
study. The Schwimmer et al. (20) study also used the V1-V3 hypervariable region of the 16S 
339 
rRNA gene and clustered sequences into OTUs, as described above, which may contribute to 
340 
some differences. Because of the differences in DNA extraction and in the hypervariable region 
341 
used, comparisons of the gut microbiome between these research projects may not be feasible. 
342 
In addition, direct comparisons are further complicated if the taxa of interest are less abundant.  
343 
This study provides important insights regarding the interaction of the microbiome and 
344 
NAFLD. A diverse gut microbiome correlates with reduced prevalence of NAFLD, providing 
345 
further support for microbial community richness improving host health. Our results show that 
346 
the F/B ratio and certain bacterial genera have statistically significant associations with HFF and 
347 
NAFLD status, even when controlling for factors associated with NAFLD, including PNPLA3 
348 
rs738409. Additional information, such as liver biopsies and shotgun metagenome data for more 
349 
patients, would strengthen the current study and include limitations of our study; this will be 
350 
pursued in the future. Another limitation of this study is its descriptive nature; thus, future

descriptive, two important strengths of our study are the thorough phenotype that allows
