The Gut Microbiome and Butyrate Differentiate Clostridioides difficile Colonization and Infection in Children
AbstractBackgroundSymptomatic Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) can cause significant morbidity and mortality. Conversely, patients can be colonized with toxigenic C. difficile in the absence of symptoms, termed asymptomatic colonization. We previously demonstrated that the presence and function of C. difficile toxins do not differentiate between asymptomatic colonization and CDI in children, suggesting the influence of other factors. This study aimed to interrogate the intestinal microbiome and butyrate in stool samples from children with CDI and asymptomatic colonization.MethodsDesign: case-control study. Setting: tertiary care children's hospital. Participants and measures: asymptomatic children had stool tested for C. difficile by nucleic-acid amplification-based testing (NAAT) and were considered colonized if positive (n = 50). Residual stool was also obtained from symptomatic children who tested positive for C. difficile by NAAT (n = 55). The microbiome was assessed via 16S rRNA sequencing and butyrate via liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry.ResultsCompared with clinical covariates and comorbidities, C. difficile symptom status (ie, asymptomatic colonization vs symptomatic CDI) demonstrated the strongest differential abundance association on gut microbes. Symptomatic CDI was associated with increased abundance of Escherichia/Shigella (Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted q = 3.94 × 10−5), Haemophilus (q = 0.022), and Gemella (q = 0.085), and depleted abundance of gut commensals such as Faecalibacterium (q = 0.041), Blautia (q = 0.041), and Bifidobacterium (q = 0.063). We also observed depletion in the abundance of microbial butyrate producers and fecal butyrate in participants with symptomatic CDI versus asymptomatic colonization.ConclusionsThe gut microbiota and butyrate differ between participants with asymptomatic C. difficile colonization and symptomatic CDI, suggesting their potential role in symptom development.Keywords: toxins, pediatric, carriage, metabolite
The gut microbiota and butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid derived from the microbial metabolism of dietary fibers, differ between patients with asymptomatic colonization and symptomatic C. difficile infection.
Clostridioides difficile is a gastrointestinal pathogen associated with substantial morbidity and mortality [1]. Alternatively, due to unclear mechanisms, C. difficile can colonize the intestinal tract without causing symptoms, referred to as asymptomatic colonization. Children with the highest rates of asymptomatic colonization include those with cancer, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and cystic fibrosis (CF), who are also at risk for diarrhea from alternative causes [2–4]. No testing algorithm has reliably differentiated symptomatic C. difficile infection (CDI) from those with asymptomatic colonization with diarrhea from an alternative source. Although C. difficile toxins are classically used in diagnostic testing algorithms, our previous work demonstrated that they were unable to differentiate symptomatic CDI from asymptomatic colonization in a pediatric cohort [5]. In addition, the same C. difficile strains and bacterial burden associated with asymptomatic colonization in some patients can be associated with severe disease in others [6]. This, therefore, highlights an important question; if the presence of C. difficile and toxins are similar between those with asymptomatic colonization and symptomatic disease, what then differs? This question is essential for 2 reasons: (1) to improve diagnostic testing and (2) to understand protective mechanisms that influence C. difficile pathophysiology.Despite pathogenesis tightly linked to the intestinal microbiome [7], little data have been published on alterations in the intestinal microbiome and associated metabolites in pediatric patients with CDI versus asymptomatic colonization. Previously published studies have been limited by small sample sizes [8, 9] or performed exclusively in adults [10, 11].Using a well-characterized cohort of pediatric patients, we compared the intestinal microbiome and butyrate levels in children with symptomatic CDI and asymptomatic colonization to better understand the pathophysiology of this important bacterium.METHODSStudy DesignPediatric participants, ages 1 to 18 years, were prospectively enrolled from July 2017 through December 2019 at Monroe Carell Jr. Children's Hospital at Vanderbilt (MCJCHV) after informed parental consent and patient assent. The Vanderbilt Institutional Review Board approved the study. Data were kept confidential using a REDCap database (REDCap software, Vanderbilt University) [12].Asymptomatic Colonized CohortAsymptomatic children with a diagnosis of cancer, CF, or IBD were eligible for enrollment. To be considered asymptomatic, participants had to be without diarrhea (Bristol stool type 6 or 7) [13] and not undergoing C. difficile testing or treatment. A stool sample was obtained, and an aliquot was tested for C. difficile using nucleic-acid amplification-based testing (NAAT) in the clinical laboratory. If NAAT was positive, the child was considered colonized.Symptomatic CohortSymptomatic children with diarrhea (≥3 unformed stools in 24 hours and an acute change in symptoms) who underwent clinical laboratory testing and tested positive for C. difficile by NAAT were enrolled. This included previously healthy children and those with additional comorbidities. After consent, residual stool from the clinical laboratory was collected and stored in the research laboratory. Alpha- and beta-diversity metrics were calculated with the R package vegan [24] at the ASV level. Difference associations in alpha-diversity metrics are based on nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis tests. Significant association between beta-diversity (Bray–Curtis) was assessed using PERMANOVA with 99 999 permutations. Significant associations between metadata and bacterial taxa were evaluated at the phylum, genus, and ASV levels. These were based on microbiome multivariable linear regression as implemented in the R package MaAsLin2 [25]. We primarily adjusted for covariates including age, sex, comorbidities, and exposure history (antibiotics, acid blockers, hospitalization, and surgery), but conducted additional sensitivity analyses where (1) we stratified by comorbidity groups to ensure that the associations were consistent, and (2) we adjusted for different subsets of covariates, including both unadjusted analyses and analyses adjusting for only age, sex, and comorbidity, again to ensure that findings were consistent. Multiple testing across different microbial taxa were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Findings were filtered to remove microbes present in <5 samples.Associations were considered significant if the P value or q value (as appropriate) was <.05 and <.10, respectively.Butyrate Measurements by Liquid Chromatography-mass SpectrometryExtracted butyrate from fecal samples were derivatized using dansylhydrazine and then analyzed using targeted reverse phase liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry. Instrument and extraction method details are in supplemental methods. Quantitation was based on single reaction monitoring detection of butyrate dansylated analogs: butyrate, m/z 336 → 171, CE 30; butyrate -d5  m/z 341 → 170, CE 30. Data analysis was conducted using Thermo-Finnigan Xcaliber and quantitation levels determined using a calibration curve. Samples were normalized against an internal standard (butyrate-d5) and by fecal weight for final pmol/mg values. Statistical analyses were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests through GraphPad Prism.RESULTSStudy PopulationThe study population included 50 pediatric participants with asymptomatic C. difficile colonization and 55 with symptomatic CDI (Table 1). The decreased abundance of butyrate-producing microbes in CDI was mirrored by the depletion of butyrate in the symptomatic CDI samples based on LC-MS (Figure 4C). Due to the potential differences in water content among samples, a subset underwent lyophilization, and LC-MS was repeated. The trend was maintained, with asymptomatic colonized participants harboring ∼10-fold higher butyrate concentrations than participants with symptomatic CDI (Supplementary Figure 7). Although a toxin-mediated disease, there are reports of patients with a high abundance of both C. difficile and its toxins who remain without clinical symptoms, deemed asymptomatic colonization [30]. It is well established that these patients may serve as an infectious vector and create diagnostic challenges if they develop diarrhea from an alternative cause [2, 6]. However, they may also provide insights into factors that protect against the development of symptomatic CDI and warrant targeted study.Our prior work has evaluated the role of toxins in pediatric patients with asymptomatic colonization compared with those with CDI. Toxins are a necessary component of symptomatic CDI because they damage the colonic epithelium, resulting in fluid secretion and diarrhea. Contrary to the association of toxins with inflammation, our prior cohort of asymptomatic colonized pediatric patients demonstrated similar toxin presence and activity compared with those with symptomatic CDI [31]. Similarly, in this study that expands upon the prior cohort, toxin presence and activity could not differentiate symptom status. There were also no differences in alpha-diversity based on toxin ELISA or Vero cell rounding. Although toxin ELISA and Vero cell positivity reflected the increased abundance of C. difficile in the sample, prior studies have demonstrated that asymptomatic colonized patients can have a high relative abundance of C. difficile present yet remain asymptomatic [32], again suggesting the role of non-C. difficile related factors that impact symptom status.Unlike our study of C. difficile toxins, we identified significant differences in the intestinal microbiome and butyrate that effectively differentiated the 2 conditions. The results from our study suggest that the intestinal microbiome may play a critical role in symptom development.In our cohort, participants with asymptomatic colonization had a higher relative abundance of common gut commensals such as Faecalibacterium (q = 0.041), Blautia (q = 0.041), and Bifidobacterium (q = 0.063) than those with symptomatic disease. Patients with symptomatic CDI had a higher relative abundance of Proteobacteria phyla and Escherichia/Shigella genera. Crobach et al. previously evaluated differences in adult patients with CDI, those with asymptomatic colonization, and healthy controls and reported that patients with CDI had higher relative abundance of Bacteroides and Veillonella and lower abundance of genera belonging to the Ruminococcaceae family and Actinobacteria phylum. Although this study did not report metabolites, the manuscript noted that many lower-abundance genera in those with CDI were known short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producers, including butyrate producers. Zhang et al. also compared gut microbiota between 8 adult patients with CDI and 8 with asymptomatic colonization [8]. Although smaller numbers than our study, they confirmed similar findings with the gut microbiota of patients with CDI consisting of a greater proportion of Proteobacteria and a smaller proportion of Bacteriodetes and Firmicutes than those with asymptomatic colonization. They also demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of Escherichia/Shigella in CDI patients versus asymptomatic carriers (23.9% vs 8.7%, P < .05), as demonstrated in our pediatric cohort. Zhoe et al. performed a longitudinal study of adult ICU patients with C. difficile colonization and noted an increase in the relative abundance of SCFA and lactic acid-producing bacteria when patients transitioned from C. difficile negative to C. difficile positive. The authors hypothesized that the intestinal microbiota may react to C. difficile colonization by eliciting potentially protective responses such as an increased abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria.Importantly, the trends in our gut microbiome dataset were maintained even when analyzed within specific patient cohorts such as IBD or cancer. In fact, symptom status was the most significant driver of the intestinal microbiome even when considering various patient factors including medications, comorbidities, age, and sex. Microbial associations with symptom status were also not sensitive to which patient factors were adjusted for in the analyses. These together suggest that the intestinal microbiome is strongly and consistently associated with C. difficile symptom status, even among patients with different comorbidities.Like the Crobach et al. study, phyla, families, and genera classically associated with butyrate production had higher relative abundance in children with asymptomatic colonization in our study. Concordantly, butyrate was higher in stool samples from children with asymptomatic colonization than those with symptomatic disease. SCFAs, including butyrate, are the major metabolic product of fiber metabolism and have demonstrated an important role in intestinal inflammation [9]. Butyrate can decrease intestinal permeability and enhance colonic defense barriers by increasing mucin production and antimicrobial peptide levels, thus preventing the development of infection [33]. Therefore, depletion of butyrate-producing bacteria could lead to epithelial dysfunction and a higher osmotic load in the intestinal lumen, leading to the diarrhea seen in CDI [8]. The role of butyrate has been studied in a variety of animal and in vitro model systems. Fachi et al. demonstrated that butyrate supplementation could protect against CDI in mice, due to a direct effect on intestinal epithelial cells [26]. Specifically, butyrate increased the resistance of intestinal cells to C. difficile toxins by increasing and stabilizing hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α). HIF-1α reduces intestinal epithelial damage and improves the immune response; both relevant factors to attenuate the intestinal inflammation characteristic of CDI. Notably, although butyrate has been shown to inhibit the growth of diverse C. difficile strains in vitro [27], it may also induce sporulation and toxin release [34, 35], suggesting the need to better understand the highly complex role of butyrate in CDI, particularly if considered as a potential therapeutic.Of note, other studies have demonstrated alternative metabolic pathways that may be relevant in C. difficile colonization and CDI. Fishbein et al. demonstrated that asymptomatically colonized patients’ microbiomes were enriched in Clostridia species and had altered carbohydrate metabolism [36]. However, the asymptomatic cohort in this study was defined differently than in our study and included adult patients with diarrhea who tested negative for toxin by EIA. As previously demonstrated, the presence or absence of toxins does not adequately differentiate colonization from disease in our pediatric cohort. The Fishbein et al. study may be evaluating a slightly different clinical scenario, ie, comparing patients who are symptomatic but toxin-negative versus symptomatic and toxin-positive, which would explain the discrepancies in results between our studies. There remain challenges to best diagnose asymptomatic colonization. However, our definition, the detection of C. difficile in the absence of diarrhea, is commonly used in the field [37–39].Our study has some limitations. Patient samples from a single pediatric center may not represent the larger population. Also, although we used strict clinical criteria to define CDI, patients with additional comorbidities have a wide range of potential etiologies of diarrhea and, thus, our cohorts are at risk of misclassification bias, with patients with C. difficile colonization defined as CDI and vice versa. There may also be risks for residual confounding, given our different recruited comorbidities (and, by extension, comorbidity-related factors such as medication) in asymptomatic and symptomatic patients. We consider such concerns likely to be secondary, because (1) the single-center patient recruitment lends to an otherwise homogeneous population, (2) our stratified analyses demonstrate similar, robust findings between different comorbidities, (3) confounding by differing interventions, though difficult to fully account for [40], is alleviated given adjustment over exposure history and related sensitivity analysis findings, and (4) the observed asymptomatic-to-symptomatic differences further agree with the shift from normal to C. difficile-colonized, dysbiotic gut microbial states, and thus unlikely to be driven purely by technical confounding. Lastly, analyses of the presence and function of the gut microbiota was done via 16S rRNA sequencing and measurement of a single stool metabolite, butyrate. The intestinal microbiome's function can be more fully captured through metagenomics and metabolomics, which should be addressed through future studies.In conclusion, through this study comparing the intestinal microbiome and butyrate of children with asymptomatic colonization versus CDI, we identify that the microbiome, and not toxin, better differentiates between these 2 conditions.
