Abstract 
14 
Gut microbiota influences many aspects of host health including immune, metabolic and gut health. 
15 
We examined the effect of a fermented whey concentrate (FWC) drink rich in L-(+)-Lactic acid, 
16 
consumed daily, in 18 healthy men (n=5) and women (n=13) in free-living conditions. 
17 
Objective: The aims of this 6-week pilot trial were to (i) identify changes in the gut microbiota 
18 
composition and faecal short chain fatty acid (SCFA) profile, and (ii) to monitor changes in glucose 
19 
homeostasis. 
20 
Results: Total faecal SCFA (mM) concentration remained constant throughout the intervention. 
21 
Proportionally, there was a significant change in the composition of different SCFAs compared to 
22 
baseline. Acetate levels were significantly reduced (-6.5%; p<0.01), coupled to a significant increase 
23 
in the relative amounts of propionate (+2.2%; p<0.01) and butyrate (+4.2%; p<0.01), respectively.

25 
were observed in glucose homeostasis in response to an oral glucose tolerance test. 
26

27 
SCFA metabolite profile, indicating some potential prebiotic activity. These changes did not result in 
28

29

1 
Introduction 
37 
Over the past decades, an increasing amount of evidence has been emerging on the importance of the 
38 
gut microbiota driving many biological functions that are directly associated with host health (1). 
39 
This has subsequently led to an interest in understanding mechanisms and to potentially modulating 
40 
the microbiota for health benefits.  
41 
Within a healthy individual, the intestinal microbiota is reasonably stable in composition, but there is 
42 
potential for targeted modification towards a more favourable community composition to optimise 
43 
health benefits. Human intervention studies have shown a clear link between dietary intake and the 
44 
gut microbiota, where the species composition will adapt in response to dietary change, determined 
45 
by competition for substrates and gut conditions (2,3). Therefore, dietary intake is considered the 
46 
primary modulator of long-term microbiota composition. This association has been observed in long-
47 
term prospective studies. These longitudinal data contribute to the increasing causal evidence for the 
48 
role of the gut microbiota and its metabolites (including short-chain fatty acids, SCFA) on various 
49 
clinically important indicators of health, such as immune cell function and gut barrier integrity (4–7). 
50 
In addition, evidence from human intervention studies targeting modification of the microbiota has 
51 
shown encouraging effects on systemic inflammation, metabolic health, allergies, and a variety of 
52 
gastrointestinal diseases (8–15). The most salient evidence for the direct link between the gut 
53 
microbiota and host health comes from the overwhelming success of invasive faecal microbiota 
54

transplants (FMT) in the treatment of recurrent Clostridium difficile infection (16). This approach 
55 
focuses on correcting the underlying gut microbiota dysbiosis via drastic changes in the microbiota 
56 
by replacing host microbiota with those of a healthy donor.  
57 
For less invasive approaches, specific foods and dietary patterns can also favourably influence the gut 
58 
microbiota and metabolite production. In specific instances these health effects have been attributed 
59 
to prebiotics. A prebiotic is defined as a ‘selectively fermented ingredient that results in specific 
60 
changes in the composition and/or activity of the gastrointestinal microbiota, thus conferring 
61 
benefit(s) upon host health’ (23,24). Prebiotic supplementation can affect the composition and the 
62 
activity of the gut microbiota (17,18). Much of the research on these compounds has focused on 
63 
dietary fibers (particularly oligosaccharides) but other non-carbohydrate compounds, mainly derived 
64 
from plant materials as well as polyphenols, have been investigated as naturally-occurring prebiotics 
65 
(19,20). An example are cocoa-derived flavanols since they are reported to stimulate lactic acid 
66 
bacteria in vivo and in vitro in healthy volunteers (n=22) consuming them daily for 4 weeks (21). 
67 
Further research has shown that lactic acid bacteria in the human gut can convert phenolic 
68 
compounds such as flavonoids to biologically active metabolites, and it is believed that these 
69 
metabolites confer the health benefits to host health. Consumption of a fermented milk product over a 
70 
4-week period, increased the production of bacterial metabolites such as butyrate and other short 
71 
chain fatty acids (SCFAs) in participants with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS-Constipation type 
72 
Rome III criteria). Patients (n=28) also experienced improvements in self-reported symptoms (i.e. 
73 
abdominal distention, acceleration of colonic transit time), further strengthening the role of this 
74 
fermented milk in improving gut health (22). The study authors suggest that these findings were 
75 
compatible with the observation that butyrate (a short chain fatty acid) has previously been shown to 
76 
also improve intestinal motility and visceral sensitivity in IBS-C (23–26). Consequently, renewed 
77 
efforts are investigating fermentation-derived metabolites in foods, assessing their ability to 
78 
favourably modulate microbiota composition and activity, thereby acting as potential prebiotics.  
79 
Fermented foods are produced through controlled microbial growth, and these food and drink 
80 
products  have recently surged in popularity due to their proposed health benefits (27). Yet, there is 
81 
limited evidence on the real-world effectiveness of most commercially available fermented food and 
82 
beverages on gastrointestinal health. Several studies have shown that microorganisms from 
83 
fermented foods can reach the gastrointestinal tract, in the same way as probiotics which are 
84 
considered “live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health

86 
appears to be transient when consumption is discontinued in animal and human studies (29,30).  
87 
At present, there is insufficient clinical evidence to make evidence-based recommendations for the 
88 
consumption of fermented foods to improve the gut microbiota profile. A recent critical review by 
89 
Stiemsa et al, 2020 identified 19 human interventions using fermented foods without probiotic 
90

91 
variety of foods investigated, highlighted the need to study the bioactive compounds and other 
92 
beneficial by-products of fermentation individually to ascertain their relative role in mediating health 
93

94 
In this study we evaluate a deproteinised whey concentrate, high in L-(+)-Lactic acid, fermented by a 
95 
member of the genus Lactobacillus, establishing its potential as a putative prebiotic. The main aim 
96 
was to investigate the effect of daily consumption of the whey product on faecal short chain fatty acid 
97 
composition, on gut microbiota metabolism and composition, and determine if these could be linked 
98 
to improvement in glycaemic control. We also explored the potential underlying physiological 
99 
mechanisms via assessment of self-reported intestinal function (regularity and consistency of bowel 
100 
movements).

Material and Methods

2.1 
Study Design 
103 
The study was a six-week single-arm intervention study, whereby participants acted as their own 
104 
control. Participants were 18 healthy adults who attended study visits at the Human Nutrition Unit at 
105 
the Rowett Institute (University of Aberdeen, UK, Figure 1.). Volunteer eligibility was assessed with 
106 
a health questionnaire during the screening process. The inclusion criteria limited age to 18-65 years 
107 
and BMI to 18-40 kg/m2. Any chronic, uncontrolled disease or use of antibiotics within the last 3 
108 
months were deemed exclusion criteria. Enrolled participants were instructed to cease consumption 
109 
of any probiotics/prebiotics or other food supplements two-weeks prior to study commencement 
110 
(washout period) and during the entire intervention. At baseline (t1) and completion (t6), blood 
111 
pressure, heart rate and weight measurements were taken (Figure 2). 
112 
Blood pressure monitored with the use of an automated system (Omron M5-1; Omron Healthcare 
113 
Inc, Bannockburn, IL), the average of 3 measures taken was recorded in the supine position.

2.2 
Fermented Whey Concentrate Intervention 
125 
The investigated product, Molkosan® (A.Vogel, Roggwil, Switzerland) is made of deproteinised 
126 
fermented organic whey (FWC) concentrate rich in L-(+)-Lactic acid (70g/L)(32). This filter-
127 
sterilised concentrate is naturally lactose-free and fermented by a member of the genus Lactobacillus. 
128 
The concentrate was provided to the participants in 1 L bottles to take home, and they were asked to 
129 
drink 20ml of the supplement every morning and evening, diluted in approximately 200ml water. 
130 
Participants were provided with 20ml measuring cups to allow for accurate measuring of this step. 
131 
Compliance of daily consumption of the supplement was assessed by the return of empty bottles. The 
132 
daily supplement intake added 8kcal in energy intake (approximately 400kcal for the whole study 
133 
period, more detailed nutritional information is shown in Table 1). Apart from the high lactic acid 
134 
content, the SCFA profile of the FWC was quantified to reveal low levels of acetate in addition to the 
135 
lactic acid (see supplementary table 2)

2.4 
Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) 
150 
Volunteers were requested to fast from 10 pm the previous evening prior to study visits on days 0 
151 
(t1), 21(t3) and 42 (t6). After baseline blood samples were taken participants were given 113 ml of 
152 
Polycal (Nutricia Advanced Medical Nutrition, UK), dissolved in 87 mL of water, corresponding to 
153 
75 g of anhydrous glucose to drink. An oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was then carried out by 
154 
collecting 5ml of venous blood at 0, 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, 120, 150 and 180 minutes post-glucose 
155 
intake, via a cannula into lithium heparin tubes (S-Monovette®, Cat #04.1936, Sarstedt, Germany). 
156 
Plasma was analysed for glucose and insulin concentration (see section 2.4). The total area under the 
157 
curve (tAUC) was calculated using the trapezoid method for (a) the total duration of the OGTT and 
158 
(b) each incremental area under the curve (iAUC). For any missing data during OGTT, values were 
159 
imputed based on the mean of the value recorded on the preceding and subsequent time point. 
160 
Statistics were performed using log-transformed data for a two-way ANOVA using Genstat 17 
161 
Release 17.1 (Lawes Agricultural Trust, VSN international Ltd, Hemel Hempstead, UK).

2.7 
Short chain fatty acid quantification 
177 
The SCFA content of the samples was determined by capillary gas chromatography following 
178 
conversion to t-butyldimethylsilyl derivatives. SCFA were quantified in mM against authentic 
179 
standards of acetate, propionate, butyrate, valerate and the branched chain fatty acids iso-butyrate and 
180 
iso-valerate. Samples were analysed using a Hewlett Packard gas chromatograph fitted with a fused 
181 
silica capillary column with helium as the carrier gas. The lower limit for reliable detection of each 
182 
product was taken as 0.2 mM. The method used has been described previously (33). 
183 
2.8 
MiSeq-based 16S rRNA gene sequencing 
184 
The V1-V2 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR using primers containing adaptors 
185 
for downstream Illumina MiSeq sequencing. Primers used were 27F and 338R. The pooled PCR 
186 
amplicons were precipitated and re-suspended in TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 0.1 mM EDTA). 
187 
Quantification was performed using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit and Qubit fluorometer 2.0 
188 
(Invitrogen, USA). Prior to sequencing samples underwent purification using AmPure XP beads 
189 
(Beckman Coulter, USA) Amplicon samples were provided to the Centre for Genome Enabled 
190 
Biology and Medicine (Aberdeen, UK) and sequenced using the Illumina MiSeq v2 flowcell 
191 
(Illumina, CA, USA) producing 250 bp paired end reads.

Study group characteristics and participant anthropometry 
214 
Of the 22 participants enrolled, 18 completed the study (two were excluded due to antibiotic intake, 
215 
two due to time constraints; Figure 1). Group characteristics are summarised in Tables 2 and 3. 
216

Discussion 
290 
We present the results of a diet study investigating the effects of dietary supplementation with a 
291 
fermented whey product containing bacterially derived L-(+)-Lactic acid on various health 
292 
parameters and microbial activity. Following daily FWC supplementation in healthy adults, a higher 
293 
proportion of butyrate and propionate were detected in faecal samples, although total SCFA levels 
294

304

305

306

307

308

309 
microorganisms (49). There is considerable uncertainty on the biodistribution of orally (exogenous) 
310 
ingested L-(+)-lactic acid in humans due to the continual flux of naturally occurring lactate from 
311 
metabolic processes, even at rest (50). Compared to endogenously produced lactic acid, the lactic 
312 
acid consumed from the FWC will have a substantially different biodistribution, as it is subjected to 
313 
the multitude of digestive processes all along the human gastrointestinal tract. In particular, the initial 
314 
stages of exogenous lactate digestion (absorption and distribution) will differ to endogenous lactate, 
315 
which is directly released from the human cell into circulation (51,52). In healthy adults, plasma 
316 
lactate is primarily cleared by the liver and mitochondria-rich tissue such as skeletal muscle (53,54).

Probiotics and prebiotics are established dietary interventions seeking to correct underlying 
387 
microbiota dysbiosis and leading ultimately to improved host health. However, a potentially new 
388 
subtype of gut-directed supplements aiming to correct underlying microbiota dysbiosis in order to 
389 
influence host health are postbiotics. The term postbiotic refers to “soluble factors (products or 
390 
metabolic byproducts) secreted by live bacteria or released after bacterial lysis” (61). The key 
391 
difference is that while probiotics, by definition, need to contain viable bacterial cells, postbiotics do 
392 
not (28). Postbiotics must originate from bacteria, differentiating them from prebiotics, which tend to 
393 
be fermentable carbohydrates. At present the few studies which have investigated the effectiveness of 
394 
postbiotics indicate positive effects on host immune response both in animal and humans (61,62).  
395 
This project was undertaken to evaluate the effect of bacterially derived FWC, with a high L-(+)-
396 
Lactic acid content, on microbiota composition and activity and determine potential effects on host 
397 
health. Based on our data it is likely that the effects of FWC might be linked to the high lactic acid 
398 
content specifically stimulating species of the resident microbiota. Consequently, we could consider 
399 
FWC as a putative prebiotic based on the changes in microbial activity, or as a putative postbiotic 
400 
since the lactic acid component is a product of the Lactobacillus fermentation of the whey, and is safe 
401 
for human consumption at these concentrations. Confirmation of the FWC in either category would 
402 
require establishment of a definitive health benefit. Future controlled human studies are required to 
403 
establish our exploratory data in a larger participant group, with additional biological measures to 
404 
establish the biodistribution of L-(+)-Lactic acid post-ingestion, and establish a health benefit. Taken 
405 
together, this will help us understand if microbiota driven effects, such as the observed changes in 
406 
SCFA profile, are directly beneficial to the host.
