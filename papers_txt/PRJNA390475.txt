1. Introduction
Sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction 
caused by a dysregulation of the host response to an 
infection and is a major health care problem, affecting and 
killing millions of people each year (Rhodes et al., 2017). 
The gut is hypothesised to play an important role in the 
pathogenesis of sepsis (Haak and Wiersinga, 2017). The 
gut barrier is the body’s first line of defence in shielding 
the body from bacteria and their toxins. Inflammation can 
negatively influence the gut barrier and cause bacterial 
translocation, fuelling systemic inflammatory processes 
(Vancamelbeke and Vermeire, 2017). In sepsis, patients 
experience a systemic inflammatory response syndrome 
due to overly stimulated and dysregulated immune 
reactions such as cytokine production and oxidative stress 
(Angus and Van der Poll, 2013). The composition of the 
intestinal microbiome is hypothesised to influence gut 
barrier function and bacterial translocation, and therefore 
also systemic inflammation (Brandl and Schnabl, 2015). 
Data from the ICU Microbiome Project show that critical 
illness leads to significant and rapid dysbiosis (McDonald 
et al., 2016). Sepsis is associated with a loss in microbial 
diversity, an increase in potential pathogens and a decrease

Abstract
The gut is hypothesised to play an important role in the development and progression of sepsis. It is however unknown 
whether the gut microbiome and the gut barrier function is already altered early in sepsis development and whether 
it is possible to modulate the microbiome in early sepsis. Therefore, a randomised, double blind, placebo-controlled 
pilot study to examine the alterations of the microbiome and the gut barrier in early sepsis and the influence of a 
concomitant probiotic intervention on dysbiosis at this early stage of the disease was conducted. Patients with early 
sepsis, defined as fulfilling the sepsis definition from the 2012 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines but without 
signs of organ failure, received multispecies probiotic (Winclove 607 based on Omnibiotic® 10 AAD) for 28 days. 
Gut microbiome composition, function, gut barrier and bacterial translocation were studied. Patients with early 
sepsis had a significantly lower structural and functional alpha diversity, clustered differently and showed structural 
alterations on all taxonomic levels. Gut permeability was unaltered but endotoxin, endotoxin binding proteins and 
peptidoglycans were elevated in early sepsis patients compared to controls. Probiotic intervention successfully 
increased probiotic strains in stool and led to an improvement of functional diversity. Microbiome composition and 
function are altered in early sepsis. Probiotic intervention successfully modulates the microbiome and is therefore 
a promising tool for early intervention in sepsis.
Keywords: microbiome, diversity, gut barrier, inflammation

This dysbiosis impacts on the metabolic function of the 
microbiome and may cause gut barrier dysfunction as 
reviewed elsewhere (Kitsios et al., 2017). Therefore, the 
microbiome might be the missing link between infection 
and a deranged systemic inflammatory reaction in sepsis, 
leading to a vicious circle of gut barrier dysfunction and 
bacterial translocation fuelling inflammation even when 
the origin of the sepsis is not the gut.
However, most available data on microbiome composition 
in sepsis so far are derived from patients in advanced 
stages of the disease with multi-organ failure on intensive 
care units, who already received a significant amount of 
pharmaceutical and non-pharmaceutical therapies that 
may influence the microbiome composition in various ways 
(Kitsios et al., 2017). It is not known whether the observed 
changes in microbiome composition precede the onset of 
organ failure or if they are the consequence of organ failure 
and therapy. This knowledge, however, is of importance 
when searching for strategies to modulate the microbiome 
as the central ´organ´ in sepsis development.
One possible strategy to modulate the microbiome is the 
use of probiotics, which are live microorganisms that 
confer health benefits to the host when administered in 
adequate amounts (FAO/WHO, 2001). Probiotics can alter 
microbiome composition by inhibiting pathogen growth, 
maintaining a balanced composition of the microbiome, 
niche competition and strengthen the gut barrier with 
their metabolites (Bron et al., 2017). Clinical benefits of 
multispecies probiotics have already been shown in critically 
ill patients (Morrow and Wischmeyer, 2017). However, the 
use of probiotics is also under debate in severely ill patients 
due to safety concerns (Morrow and Wischmeyer, 2017; 
Stadlbauer, 2015).
Therefore, we conducted a randomised, double blind, 
placebo-controlled pilot study to examine the alterations of 
the microbiome and the gut barrier in early sepsis (defined 
as sepsis without organ dysfunction) and the influence of 
a concomitant probiotic intervention on dysbiosis at this 
early stage of the disease.
2. Materials and methods
Patients
The prospective intervention study was conducted between 
September 2015 and June 2016 at the clinical ward of 
the Department of Gastroenterology and Hepatology, 
Medical University of Graz. Adult patients with early 
sepsis were included after giving written informed consent 
when they fulfilled the applicable sepsis definition at the 
time of study start (the 2012 Surviving Sepsis Campaign 
guidelines; Dellinger et al., 2013): presence of a systemic 
inflammatory response syndrome (2 out of the four: 
elevated heart rate (tachycardia) >90 beats per minute at 
rest; body temperature either high (>100.4 F or 38 °C) or 
low (<96.8 F or 36 °C); increased respiratory rate of >20 
breaths per minute or a reduced PaCO2 (partial pressure 
of carbon dioxide in arterial blood level); abnormal white 
blood cell count (>12,000 cells/µl or <4,000 cells/µl or 
>10% bands [an immature type of white blood cell]) and 
a known or suspected infection. Patients with severe 
sepsis or septic shock, inability to understand and sign 
an informed consent, admission to any intensive care unit 
or intermediate care unit for any reason, procalcitonin >2 
ng/ml and lactate >2.2 mmol/l, positive beta-D-glycane 
test, patients receiving (par)enteral nutrition, presence or 
suspicion of acute pancreatitis, pregnancy or women of 
childbearing age without adequate contraception, women 
who are breast-feeding, known malignancy or any other 
condition or circumstance, which, in the opinion of the 
investigator, would affect the patient’s ability to participate 
in the protocol were excluded. As controls age and sex 
matched healthy persons without evidence of any acute 
or chronic diseases were included.
The study was conducted according to the principles of 
the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by 
the institutional review board of the Medical University 
of Graz (26-496 ex 13/14) and registered at clinicaltrials.
gov (NCT02469571) on 11 July 2015.
Patients were randomised in a 1:1 ratio to either receive 
a multispecies probiotic or a placebo. The randomisation 
scheme was generated by using the website Randomization.
com (http://www.randomization.com). Randomisation was 
performed by permuted blocks. The randomisation list was 
generated by Winclove Probiotics B.V. (Amsterdam, the 
Netherlands), who produced the study medication. Study 
product packages were sequentially numbered and study 
products were used in consecutive order by the clinical 
trial coordinator. Participants, care providers and outcome 
assessors were blinded to the assignment of the intervention. 
The randomisation sequence was revealed after the last 
patient had finished the study and all laboratory analyses 
were finished. The multispecies probiotic contained 5 g of 
Lactobacillus plantarum W1, Lactobacillus paracasei W20, 
Bifidobacterium bifidum W23, Lactobacillus salivarius W24, 
Lactobacillus acidophilus W37, Bifidobacterium lactis W51, 
Enterococcus faecium W54, Lactobacillus acidophilus W55, 
Lactobacillus plantarum W62, Lactobacillus rhamnosus 
W71 at a concentration of 1.1×109 cfu/g in a matrix of maize 
starch, maltodextrins, inulin, vegetable protein, potassium 
chloride, magnesium sulphate, fructo-oligosaccharides 
(FOS), enzymes (amylases), vanilla powder and manganese 
sulphate and the placebo was a similar looking and tasting 
powder without bacteria. Patients received a package with 
56 sachets to take the study product twice daily for 4 weeks.

sepsis markers over 4 weeks. The study was designed as 
a pilot trial; therefore no sample size calculation was 
performed. The trial was terminated early due to difficulties 
in recruitment and high drop-out rates after the trial was 
reviewed by the funding agency in June 2016.
Microbiome analysis
Total DNA was isolated from frozen stool samples using 
MagnaPure LC DNA Isolation Kit III (Bacteria, Fungi) 
(Roche, Mannheim, Germany) according to manufacturer’s 
instructions including mechanic and enzymatic lysis 
(Klymiuk et al., 2017). Hypervariable regions V1-2 were 
amplified in a target specific PCR using the primers 27F 
and R357 (27F-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG; R357-
CTGCTGCCTYCCGTA) and sequenced with the Illumina 
MiSeq technique (Illumina, Eindhoven, the Netherlands) 
(Klymiuk et al., 2017). Resulting FASTQ files were used for 
data analysis. Sequencing was done in cooperation with 
the Core Facility for Molecular Biology at the Center for 
Medical Research in Graz.
Gut permeability, inflammation and bacterial 
translocation
Enzyme linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) were used to 
measure faecal calprotectin, faecal zonulin, serum diamine 
oxidase (Immundiagnostic AG, Bensheim, Germany), 
soluble (s)CD14 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), 
and lipopolysaccharide binding protein (LBP) (Hycult 
biotech, Uden, the Netherlands). All assays were performed 
according to manufacturers’ instructions.
Bacterial products (endotoxin, peptidoglycan and bacterial 
DNA) were detected in serum using HEK-Blue hTLR4, 
HEK-Blue hNOD2 and HEK-Blue hTLR9 reporter cells 
(Invivogen, Toulouse, France), respectively. Cells were 
cultured according to manufacturer’s instructions. For 
detection, 5×104 cells/well were transferred to 24-well 
plates (Nalgene/Nunc, Rochester, NY, USA) and incubated 
with 2 ml selection medium for 24 h. Selection medium 
was replaced by 950 μl HEK-Blue Detection (Invivogen) 
and 50 μl standards, blanks, spikes and samples. After 21 
h photometrical assessment of the supernatant at 650 nm 
was used for analysis. For quantification a standard 1:2 serial 
dilution of either Endosafe Endotoxin standards (Charles 
River, Wilmington, MA, USA) ranging from 50 EU/ml to 
3.13 EU/ml, L18-MDP (Invivogen) ranging from 10 ng/ml 
to 0.625 ng/ml for peptidoglycans, or ODN2006 (Invivogen) 
ranging from 50 µM to 3.13 µM for bacterial CpG motifs 
was used. An increasing concentration of recombinant 
alkaline phosphatase was added (0, 250 and 500 U/l) to 
each sample and linear regression was used to account for 
endogenous alkaline phosphatase in serum.
Statistics
The generated FASTQ were prepared for analysis using 
Qiime tools, (Caporaso et al., 2010) implemented in Galaxy 
(https://galaxy.medunigraz.at) (Afgan et al., 2016). High 
quality reads were clustered based on 97% similarity. 
Unfiltered OTUs rarefied to 19,741 reads per sample 
were used for alpha diversity assessment with Chao1 
index, observed species, Shannon, Simpson, Simpson 
reciprocal and phylogenetic diversity (PD) whole tree and 
low abundance filter with 0.005 minimum count fraction 
and at least two different hosts were applied before beta 
diversity analysis. Low abundance filter reduced the average 
reads from 195,783±116,466 to 16,737±847.1; rarefaction 
was based on a minimum count per sample of 15,169 
reads. Principal coordinate and redundancy analysis were 
based on Bray Curtis dissimilarity. For OTU abundance 
comparisons Mann-Whitney-U-tests were performed. 
Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was used to account for 
multiple testing. The web-based software Calypso 7.14 
(http://cgenome.net/calypso) was used for RDA and LEfSe 
calculations as well as the visualisation of thereby obtained 
results (Zakrzewski et al., 2017). PICRUSt implemented in 
Galaxy was used to predict Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes 
and Genomes (KEGG) functional pathway abundance 
content from 16s rDNA data using a closed reference OTU 
table created in QIIME and the Greengenes reference 
database version 13.8 (Langille et al., 2013). Beta diversity 
on PICRUSt data was calculated using R with analysis of 
similarity (ANOSIM) test based on Bray-Curtis distance 
to prove the group differences (https://www.R-project.
org). For the analysis of the presence of probiotic strains, 
we received the 16s rRNA gene sequences of the strains 
from the company (Winclove Probiotics B.V.) after signing 
a confidentiality agreement and searched for the sequences 
in our table of sequences. The sequence of Bifidobacterium 
lactis W51 could not be identified with more than 66%

3. Results
Fifteen patients with early sepsis as defined above 
were included. Only nine patients finished a four-week 
intervention with either a probiotic (n=5) or a placebo 
(n=4). The reasons for dropout were diarrhoea (n=1, 
placebo group), nausea (n=3, two placebo, one probiotic) 
and withdrawal of consent after randomisation but before 
the first dose of the study product was taken (n=2, both 
probiotic). Dropouts were therefore not related to the 
intervention. The study flowchart is shown in Figure 1. 
Reasons for early sepsis were pneumonia (n=4), cholangitis 
(n=2), bloodstream infections (n=3), gastrointestinal 
infections (n=3), cellulitis (n=2) and unknown focus 
(n=1). Antibiotic therapy was performed with either 
aminopenicillin/beta-lactamase inhibitor combinations 
or quinolones. None of the patients had a central venous 
line at the time of the study. The first available stool sample 
after admission was collected. This happened within 
48 h of commencement of antibiotic therapy in all cases.
Microbiome composition, markers of gut permeability 
and bacterial translocation at baseline were compared

Differences in microbiome composition between patients 
with early sepsis and healthy controls
Patients (n=14, one sample was insufficient for microbiome 
analysis) with early sepsis showed significantly decreased 
alpha diversity compared to healthy controls as estimated 
by the Chao1 index (P=0.001), observed species (P=0.002), 
Shannon (P=0.003), Simpson (P=0.004), Simpson 
reciprocal (P=0.005) and PD whole tree (P=0.001) already 
at baseline. Beta diversity analysis based on a Bray-Curtis 
dissimilarity matrix revealed significant clustering (P=0.01) 
and redundancy analysis showed significant differences 
between sepsis patients and healthy controls in their overall 
pattern (P=0.001) (Figure 2A and B). Clostridia diversity 
was significantly decreased in sepsis patients (P<0.0001 for 
Chao1 and observed species).
Gut microbiome composition differed between sepsis 
patients and healthy controls throughout all taxonomic 
levels. The phyla Actinobacteria (P=0.019) and 
Firmicutes (P=0.029) were significantly increased, the 
phyla Bacteriodetes (P=0.019) and Tenericutes (P=0.019) 
significantly reduced in patients with early sepsis.
On class level, Bacilli were significantly increased in 
the sepsis group (P=0.013), classes Betaproteobacteria 
(P=0.017), Mollicutes (P=0.035) and Bacteroidia (P=0.035) 
were significantly reduced in the patient group compared 
to healthy controls. Orders Lactobacillales (P=0.019) and 
Bacillales (P=0.034) were significantly higher abundant 
in patients compared to controls. Orders Burkholderiales 
(P=0.021), NB1-n (from the class of Mollicutes, P=0.035) 
and Bacteroidales (P=0.035) were significantly reduced in 
patients with early sepsis.
On family level, Streptococcaceae (P=0.031), Bacillales 
Family XI (P=0.031) and a not further classifiable family 
of Bacillales (P=0.031) were significantly increased, while 
Alcaligenaceae (P=0.031), Christensenellaceae (P=0.031), 
Rikenellaceae (P=0.031), Porphyromonadaceae (P=0.031), 
and a not further classifiable family of NB1-n (P=0.033) 
were significantly reduced in patients with sepsis compared 
to healthy controls. Differences on phylum, class, order 
and family level are visualised in Supplementary Figure S1.
Differences at genus level are shown in Table 2. One 
not further classifiable genus of the order Bacillales 
(P=0.043) that was significantly increased in patients 
with sepsis and one not further classified genus from the 
Mollicutes order NB1-n (P=0.049) that was significantly 
reduced in sepsis patients compared to healthy controls 
are not included in the table. Supervised learning based 
on Random Forest Classifier identified Parabacteroides, 
Lachnospira, Coprococcus 2, incerta sedis, Lachnospiraceae 
ND03007, Lachnospiraceae NK4A136, Suterella, 
Gemella, Streptococcus and Eisenbergiella as the 10 
most discriminatory genera. LefSe analysis showed 44 
discriminative features before internal Wilcoxon analysis, 
however none of them reached an absolute LDA score >2.0.
Sixteen OTUs were differentially abundant between 
sepsis patients and healthy controls. Of these 16 OTUs 
seven qualified within the ten most discriminatory OTUs 
as identified by supervised learning based on Random 
Forest Classifier. These most significant differentially 
abundant OTUs were Roseburia faecis, Roseburia 
inulinivorans, Lactobacillus rogosae, two not further 
classifiable Roseburia species as well as two not further 
classifiable Faecalibacterium species. All of these OTUs 
were significantly less abundant in patients with early sepsis 
(Figure 2C). LefSe analysis showed 308 discriminative 
features before internal Wilcoxon analysis, however none 
of them reached an absolute LDA score >2.0.

Effect of multispecies probiotic on the gut microbiome

Alpha diversity was significantly reduced in patients at 
baseline compared to controls as described above. After 
seven days alpha diversity was further reduced (not 
significantly) in the placebo group but not in the probiotic 
group. Beta diversity did not change during the study. 
Four weeks after inclusion in the study in both groups 
alpha diversity was higher than baseline (not statistically 
significant) (Figure 4A). PICRUSt revealed a significantly 
increased alpha diversity of functional content after 28 days 
of probiotic intervention compared to the previous time 
point (Figure 4 B). Beta diversity of functional content did 
not change during the study.
The intervention led to enrichment of probiotic strains in 
the probiotic but not in the placebo group. Compared to 
the placebo group, patients in the probiotic group showed 
significantly higher abundance of L. plantarum, L. salivarius 
and L. acidophilus at day 7. After 28 days of probiotic intake 
the probiotic strains were still enriched, however, due to 
the small sample size and the inter- and intra-individual 
variations the differences were only significant for L. 
plantarum and L. salivarius. E. faecium was significantly 
enriched in the probiotic group after 28 days only (Figure 5). 
In an untargeted approach, no other changes in taxonomic 
microbiome composition or microbiome function could 
be detected. Biomarker of gut inflammation, gut barrier 
or bacterial translocation were not different between 
placebo or probiotic group at baseline and did not change 
significantly during the intervention (Table 5).
4. Discussion
We demonstrate that patients with early sepsis show 
profound changes in gut microbiome composition. This 
finding is novel since previous studies only included patients 
at a more advanced stage of the disease. Structural changes 
in the gut microbiome are associated with a reduction 
in functional content. We also demonstrate that early 
intervention with a multispecies probiotic is able to enrich 
the microbiome with lactobacilli and to increase functional 
diversity.
As in many microbiome-disturbing diseases, (Mancabelli 
et al., 2017) alpha diversity is significantly reduced in our 
cohort, irrespective of the focus of infection. Microbes 
beneficial to the gut barrier, such as Lachnospiraceae or 
Ruminococcaceae on family level and butyrate producing 
Roseburia species and Faecalibacterium species are 
reduced in these patients. Microbiome changes often 
persisted through the taxonomic hierarchy and changed 
the presence of whole phyla. Especially the phyla Firmicutes 
and Bacteroidetes were affected, which adds even more 
significance to the presented alterations, because 
they represent the vast majority of bacteria in the gut 
microbiome. In comparison to previously published cohorts 
of sepsis patients on intensive care units (McDonald et al., 
2016), the increase of potentially pathogenic species was 
less prominent in our early sepsis cohort, suggesting that 
the loss of beneficial species, such as butyrate-producing 
Roseburia species and Faecalibacterium species precedes 
the increase of pathogenic species. Whether this loss of 
beneficial species is a cause or a consequence of sepsis, 
cannot be answered by our study design. In a study in 15 
critically ill patients with infection and sepsis (septic shock 
was present in 40%), after one week of antibiotic therapy an 
overgrowth with Enterococcus was seen (Iapichino et al., 
2008). Experiments in mice showed, that anti-anaerobic

were reduced in sepsis, but the microbiome at this early stage 
of disease seems to be able to exert all necessary functions. 
A possible explanation for this observation could be an 
overgrowth of opportunistic bacteria, exerting no particular 
function. Metabolomic studies in sepsis reveal alterations in 
several metabolites, such as metabolites of aromatic amino 
acids, which can be attributed to functional changes of the 
gut microbiome (Beloborodova et al., 2018). Further studies 
on the functional changes on the gut microbiome in sepsis 
are clearly necessary.
In our study, despite dysbiosis was clearly present, we were 
not able to detect increased biomarkers of gut permeability 
and intestinal inflammation in the serum and stool of 
patients, possibly due to the small sample size, the large inter-
individual variations, and the lack of optimal biomarkers 
for gut permeability in humans. Zonulin, a tight-junction 
regulating protein, has been shown to be elevated in stool of 
patients with pneumonia, (Cangemi et al., 2016) and critically 
ill sepsis patients (Klaus et al., 2013). The increase in zonulin 
was paralleled by an increase in endotoxin levels (Cangemi et 
al., 2016). We could not demonstrate a statistically significant 
increase in zonulin levels, but we could demonstrate elevated 
endotoxin and peptidoglycan levels, as well as increased levels 
of endotoxin binding proteins in our cohort, pointing towards 
increased bacterial translocation. However, since these 
biomarkers cannot distinguish between endotoxin derived 
from gut bacteria or from other sites of bacterial invasion, 
we cannot prove that the detected bacterial products stem 
from the intestine.
Intestinal inflammation is thought to be the link between 
dysbiosis and gut barrier dysfunction in liver disease 
(Brandl and Schnabl, 2015). Microbiome changes have been 
associated with increased gut permeability and bacterial 
translocation in animals in gut-derived and also in non-
gut derived sepsis (Earley et al., 2015; Yu et al., 2014). The 
underlying mechanism is not completely clear yet. Changes 
in the integrity of the intestinal epithelium due to oxidative 
stress, dysregulated apoptosis, tight junction dysregulation 
and a disturbed mucus layer have been associated with sepsis 
in animal and human studies (Mittal and Coopersmith, 
2014). In septic animals, butyrate was able to restore gut 
barrier function (Mittal and Coopersmith, 2014), indicating 
a possible causal link between the loss of butyrate producing 
bacteria and increased gut permeability. The loss of barrier 
strengthening bacteria in our patient cohort may therefore 
lead to gut barrier dysfunction, supporting the concept that 
gut derived, translocated toxins or bacteria fuel local and 
systemic inflammatory processes in sepsis. Stool calprotectin 
as a marker of intestinal inflammation was elevated above 
50 ng/ml in the majority of our patients, however, due to the 
large variations between patients and the small sample size, 
the comparison to healthy controls did not reach statistical 
significance. While serum calprotectin has been established 
as an early sepsis biomarker, not much is known about stool 
calprotectin in sepsis from literature (Jonsson et al., 2017). 
In humans it therefore still remains to be shown, if dysbiosis 
in sepsis causes gut barrier disruption and inflammation 
and consecutive bacterial translocation.
We sampled the first stool sample available from the patients 
after admission to the hospital, which was within 48 h 
after commencing antibiotic treatment in all cases. Study 
intervention (probiotic or placebo) was started immediately 
after collecting the stool sample. Since antibiotic treatment 
has to be started as early as possible according to the 
Surviving Sepsis Campaign recommendation, (Dellinger 
et al., 2013) and since we waited for spontaneous bowel 
movements, it was not feasible to obtain stool samples at 
an earlier timepoint. For further studies on this topic taking 
rectal swabs or rectoscopy samples before starting antibiotic 
therapy would be a possibility to overcome the problem of 
the time gap between commencing antibiotic therapy and 
the next spontaneous bowel movement of patients. The 
changes in microbiome structure were already detectable at 
this early time point and persisted over at least one month, 
although the antibiotic treatment lasted for 7-14 days 
only, and none of the patients was hospitalised or received 
antibiotics any more at the end of the study. Previous studies 
showed microbiome changes by antibiotic therapy as early as 
after 4 days of antibiotic treatment (Mikkelsen et al., 2015). 
Our study can therefore not answer the question whether 
the observed changes in microbiome composition are a 
causative or a consequential factor in sepsis and whether it 
is driven by the disease or by the treatment of the disease. 
However, the very early detection of microbiome changes in 
our study suggests, that the disease itself causes at least part 
of the dysbiosis, which then may be further aggravated by 
the treatment of the disease. It is well known that antibiotics 
cause dysbiosis and thereby may trigger adverse events 
such as antibiotic associated diarrhoea and C. difficile 
infections, which impairs prognosis and increases health 
care costs (Heimann et al., 2015). In our small cohort no 
cases of C. difficile infection were detected, but one patient 
in the placebo group developed diarrhoea during antibiotic 
therapy. A recent meta-regression analysis showed that 
probiotic treatment is effective in reducing the rate of C. 
difficile infections by more than 50% when the probiotic 
treatment starts within the first 2 days of antibiotic therapy 
(Shen et al., 2017). Our study shows significant enrichment 
of probiotic strains in the microbiome as early as after 7 days 
of intervention. Furthermore, the probiotic intervention 
could maintain alpha diversity during antibiotic therapy and 
even increase functional diversity at the end of the study, 
which could not be observed in the placebo group. If the 
protective function of probiotics on the richness of species 
and functional content in the microbiome holds true in 
larger studies, this might be an important mechanism for the 
prevention of antibiotic associated diarrhoea by probiotics.

Probiotics have been proven to be safe, clinically effective 
and cost-saving in preventing antibiotic associated 
diarrhoea and C. difficile infection and the most recent 
review from the Cochrane group suggests that despite the 
need for further research, hospitalised patients, particularly 
those at high risk of C. difficile infection, should be informed 
of the potential benefits and harms of probiotics (Blaabjerg 
et al., 2017; Goldenberg et al., 2017; Lenoir-Wijnkoop et 
al., 2014; Videlock and Cremonini, 2012). A probiotic 
(Ecologic® AAD, Winclove Probiotics B.V.) that is very 
similar to the one used in our study (the strain L. plantarum 
W1 was added in our product) was shown to be effective 
in reducing diarrhoea in healthy volunteers treated with 
amoxicillin (Koning et al., 2008). In an uncontrolled post 
marketing surveillance study the addition of Ecologic AAD 
to antibiotic therapy in a hospital in Austria resulted in a 
very low incidence of antibiotic associated diarrhoea of 
0.5%, which is normally expected to be between 5-39% 
(Lang, 2010). Furthermore, in a retrospective case report 
series of 10 patients with C. difficile infection (5 with 
recurrent C. difficile infection) the addition of Ecologic 
AAD to vancomycin resulted in complete response in all 10 
patients (Hell et al., 2013). Further studies are necessary to 
define which product will be most useful in this indication.
The limitation of our study is clearly the small sample size. 
Therefore, the study can only serve as a proof of concept 
study to open up new study fields. However, despite the 
small sample size, our study provides novel data that 
underpin the important role of the microbiome in sepsis. In 
conclusion we show early structural and functional dysbiosis 
in sepsis and provide first evidence that microbiome 
modulation with probiotics is feasible in this setting.
