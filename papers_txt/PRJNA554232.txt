Objectives: Patients with Cystic Fibrosis (CF) suffer from pancreatic insuﬃciency, lipid malabsorption and 
gastrointestinal complaints, next to progressive pulmonary disease. Altered mucosal homoeostasis due 
to malfunctioning chloride channels results in an adapted microbial composition of the gastrointestinal 
and the respiratory tract. Additionally, antibiotic treatment has the potential to distort resident micro- 
bial communities dramatically. This study aims to investigate early life development of the gut microbial 
community composition of children with CF compared to healthy infants and to study the independent 
effects of antibiotics taking into account other clinical and lifestyle factors. 
Study design: Faecal samples from 20 infants with CF and 45 healthy infants were collected regularly 
during the ﬁrst 18 months of life and microbial composition was determined using 16S rRNA based se- 
quencing. 
Results: We observed signiﬁcant differences in the overall microbiota composition between infants with 
CF and healthy infants ( p < 0.001). Akkermansia and Anaerostipes were signiﬁcantly more abundant in con- 
trol infants, whereas Streptococci and E. coli were signiﬁcantly more abundant in infants with CF, also 
after correction for several clinical factors ( p < 0.05). Antibiotic use in infants with CF was associated with 
a lower alpha diversity, a reduced abundance of Biﬁdobacterium and Bacteroides , and a higher abundance 
of Enterococcus . 
Conclusion: Microbial development of the gut is different in infants with CF compared to healthy infants 
from the ﬁrst months of life on, and further deviates over time, in part as a result of antibiotic treatment. 
The resulting dysbiosis may have signiﬁcant functional consequences for the microbial ecosystem in CF 
patients.

1. Introduction

caused by mutations in the CFTR (Cystic Fibrosis Transmembrane 
conductance Regulator) gene. Pulmonary infections and inﬂamma- 
tion are the most important factors driving morbidity and mortal- 
ity. We have previously described that the respiratory tract micro- 
biome in young children with CF is signiﬁcantly different when

is rapidly expanding [7–9] . Importantly, recent studies suggest a 
link between microbial composition in the gut and respiratory out-

described the gut microbial composition in children with CF, the 
bio-chemical effects of the CFTR mutation and the effects of re- 
peated antibiotic use on the gut microbiome are still incompletely 
understood. A two year follow-up of two children by Duytschaever 
et al. showed lower stability and species richness in the predom- 
inant faecal microbiota in infants with CF [14] Studies by Madan 
et al. and Hoen et al. even show a relation between the develop- 
ment of the gut microbiota and respiratory microbiota over time 
[15 , 10] . Repeated and chronic use of antibiotics has been shown to 
inﬂuence the microbial composition in both the respiratory tract 
and the gut of CF patients [1 , 16 , 17] . In addition, several factors 
which are known to contribute to the gut microbial composition, 
such as dietary intake and pancreatic function, are different in CF 
patients [6 , 18] . To study the independent effects of CF disease (i.e. 
the CFTR channel dysfunction), antibiotics and other clinical and 
lifestyle factors on the gut microbiome, studies with longitudinal 
follow-up are needed. A better understanding of the differences in 
gut microbiota composition and microbial stability between CF pa- 
tients and healthy controls might enable further optimization of 
the choice and timing of antibiotic therapies and might impact on 
nutritional supplementation. In order to investigate the early de- 
velopment of the gut microbiota and the impact of CF and antibi- 
otic use, we set up a longitudinal study in which we followed chil- 
dren with CF and healthy controls from birth on. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Study population 
Between 2011 and 2013 all children that were referred to one 
of the two participating CF treatment centres were invited to par- 
ticipate in the study, as previously described by Prevaes et al. 
[1] . A total of 20 infants with CF were included shortly after a 
positive new born screening for CF. From the WHeezing-Illnesses- 
STudy-LEidsche-Rijn (WHISTLER) cohort (a cohort of healthy new- 
born children from the Netherlands), 45 age and sex matched con- 
trols were included [1] . Faecal samples were collected from all chil- 
dren monthly during the ﬁrst half year of life and at months 8, 
10, 12, 15 and 18, as well as during and one month after clinical 
signs of respiratory infection. Questionnaires on clinical parame- 
ters were ﬁlled out at every sampling moment. This study was ap- 
proved by the medical ethics committee of the University Medical 
Centre Utrecht. 
2.2. Sequencing 
All faecal samples were stored at −80 °Celsius. DNA was iso- 
lated using the Agowa Mag Mini DNA Isolation Kit in combina- 
tion with phenol and bead-beating and eluted in elution buffer. 
(See appendix for further details) Amplicon libraries of the V4 hy- 
pervariable region were constructed and sequenced using the Illu- 
mina MiSeq platform (Illumina, Eindhoven, the Netherlands). The 
sequence data was processed using Mothur as previously described 
by Kozich et al. 2013 and further described in the appendix [19 , 20] . 
MastiType Multi Kit (ThermoFisher scientiﬁc) was performed, to 
conﬁrm that this taxon represented E. coli. 
2.3. Statistical analysis 
2.3.1. Microbial development over time in healthy children and 
children with CF 
Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 3.3.1). Alpha 
diversity was calculated for all samples using the Shannon index 
and the Chao1 index. Using a Smoothing Spline Analysis of Vari- 
ance (SS-ANOVA,R-package: ﬁtTimeSeries), periods were identiﬁed 
during which the alpha-diversity was signiﬁcantly different be- 
tween healthy infants and infants with CF [21] Mean relative abun- 
dances for the most abundant OTU’s were calculated per month for 
healthy and control samples. To determine speciﬁc periods during 
which genera were differentially present in healthy and CF sam- 
ples, the read counts were normalized using metagenomeSeq and 
an SS-ANOVA was performed (R-package: ﬁtTimeSeries) [21] The 
Benjamini Hochberg procedure was used to correct for multiple 
testing [22] . A non-metric dimensional scaling (nMDS) plot was 
made to visualise the diversity of the overall microbial composi- 
tion (R-package: Vegan). This non-parametric method is used for 
dimension reduction, whereby per sample the complete microbiota 
composition is depicted as a single point in a two-dimensional 
space. This method also allows the plotting of the most abundant 
species into this same space, showing how their abundance in- 
ﬂuences the distribution of microbial data. A permutational mul- 
tivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) of all samples, with 
time of sampling as strata, was used to test for signiﬁcant differ- 
ences between microbial community proﬁles of healthy samples 
and CF samples. PERMANOVA was also used to test the effects 
of age within the stratiﬁed CF and control groups, using individ- 
ual study participants as strata (R-package: Vegan; Adonis). A den- 
drogram of all samples was constructed using the Bray-Curtis dis- 
similarity, with the optimal number of clusters deﬁned by the Sil- 
houette index (R-package: cluster). Differences between the num- 
ber of CF and control samples within clusters were tested using 
chi-square tests, and corrected for multiple testing with the Ben- 
jamini Hochberg procedure. To visualize gut microbiota develop- 
ment in children over time an alluvial plot was constructed using 
the Alluvial package in R (R-package: Alluvial). The microbial sta- 
bility over time was evaluated using the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity 
between consecutive samples from the same child. A linear mixed 
effects model of these Bray-Curtis dissimilarities was used to de- 
termine the difference in stability between infants with CF and 
healthy infant over time. (R-package: lmerTest). To compare micro- 
biota development over time in healthy infants and infants with 
CF we generated and cross validated a Random Forest based mi- 
crobiota aging model, using the control group as reference. (see 
appendix for further details) This model was used to estimate (re- 
construct) the microbial age of the CF group over time [23] . It was 
then used to predict the age of children with CF using the micro- 
bial composition of their faecal samples. (R- package: RandomFor-

2.3.2. Effects of antibiotics on the CF gut microbiota 
Effects of antibiotic use on the alpha diversity was assessed us- 
ing the Shannon index. Hereby, differences between samples taken 
during or within one month after antibiotic use versus samples 
taken not during or shortly after antibiotic use, corrected for age, 
were detected by SS-ANOVA. A mixed effects model correcting for 
age and repeated measurements was used to evaluate cumulative 
antibiotic effects. (R-package: lmerTest) Relative abundances were 
log transformed to achieve approximate normality, after which a 
linear mixed effects model was ﬁtted to determine the effect of 
cumulative antibiotic use on the relative abundance of the top-50 
genera, corrected for age and repeated measurements. 
3. Results 
Baseline characteristics of the study population, as previously 
described by Prevaes et al. are summarized in Table 1. (1) 
Infants in the control group had signiﬁcantly more siblings, at- 
tended day-care more frequently and received breastfeeding for a 
longer period of time. In the control group there was very little an- 
tibiotic use, whereas most infants in the CF group used antibiotics 
during the ﬁrst 18 months of life (Supplementary Fig. 1). A total of 
717 faecal samples were sequenced: 214 samples from CF children 
and 503 samples from healthy controls. The number of collected CF 
and control samples per month is summarised in Supplementary 
File 1. The mean number of reads per sample was 25443 (range 
821–60,930). 
3.1. Microbial diversity and composition 
The alpha diversity, calculated by the Shannon index gradually 
increased between month 5 and month 13 in both CF and control 
samples with age. ( Fig. 1 A) The Shannon diversity was signiﬁcantly 
higher in infants with CF compared to controls between month 1 
and month 13 (SS-ANOVA: p < 0.05). Evenness more than richness 
determined the difference in alpha diversity between groups, since 
richness (Chao1 index) was only different between CF and control 
children between month 3 and 6 (SS-ANOVA: p < 0.05). 
With respect to the overall microbial community composi- 
tion, we observed a signiﬁcant difference between samples from 
healthy children and children with CF, after correction for age 
(PERMANOVA: R-squared 0.06; p < 0.001), as well as an effect of 
age after stratiﬁcation for CF status (PERMANOVA: CF group: R- 
squared 0.07; p < 0.001, control group: R-squared 0.29; p < 0.001). In 
Fig. 2 all samples are depicted in an nMDS plot, suggesting a dif- 
ferent time dependant shift in community composition for CF and 
control samples, as well as a higher beta diversity in CF samples. 
Next, all samples were clustered using hierarchical clustering, 
using the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity. We observed 17 different clus- 
ters or community proﬁles (Supplementary Fig. 2). The biggest 
cluster contained 432 samples and was dominated by Biﬁdobac- 
terium , more commonly present in control children (chi-square 
test: adjusted p < 0.001), followed by a Biﬁdobacterium and Blau- 
tia dominated cluster,. Several smaller clusters were dominated by 
a variety of different bacteria such as Blautia, E. coli, Streptococ- 
cus and Enterococcus, and were more commonly observed in CF 
(chi-square test: all adjusted p -values < 0.001). Microbiota develop- 
ment over time in control children and children with CF is visu- 
alized in Supplementary Fig. 3. This ﬁgure shows how the micro- 
biota proﬁles developed over time. In the control group most chil- 
dren started in the Biﬁdobacterium cluster and, from 8 months on, 
tended to make the transition to the Biﬁdobacterium-Blautia clus- 
ter (logistic mixed effects model: p < 0.001). In the CF group, most 
children also started in the Biﬁdobacterium cluster. However, after 
18 months, CF children are more explicitly divided over the Biﬁ- 
dobacterium cluster, the Blautia cluster, the Biﬁdobacterium-Blautia 
combined cluster, and several other smaller clusters.

ity and resilience) was calculated as the Bray Curtis distance be- 
tween two consecutive samples of the same child. We observed a 
higher Bray Curtis distance between consecutive CF samples, indi-

ing in the ﬁrst months of life. Since part of the children had not 
yet used antibiotics up to 4–6 months of age, this suggests a direct

4.1. Differences between infants with CF and control infants 
Most of the healthy children in this study initially showed a Bi- 
ﬁdobacterium dominated proﬁle, from which they switch to a pro- 
ﬁle dominated by both Biﬁdobacterium and Blautia between month 
8 and month 18. In the CF group, although most children also start 
with a Biﬁdobacterium dominated proﬁle, part of the children have 
predominance of Streptococcus or E. coli from one month of life on. 
Also from the ﬁrst months of life children with CF show a differ- 
ence in microbiota aging, combined with reduced ecosystem stabil- 
ity suggesting aberrant maturation, which becomes more apparent 
with older age. 
During the ﬁrst 18 months of life Akkermansia, Eggertella, 
Clostridia IV , and Anaerostipes were more abundant in the healthy 
group than in the CF group, even after correction for antibiotic use, 
breastfeeding and day care attendance and siblings. Both Clostridia 
IV as well as Anaerostipes are butyrate producing bacteria, support- 
ing the gastro-intestinal epithelial barrier [24] . Akkermansia is a 
mucus degrading bacteria, associated with a healthy mucosal layer 
[25] . Decrease of these commensals might weaken the epithelial 
layer of the gut in infants with CF. As in previous literature Biﬁ- 
dobacterium was more abundant in healthy infants, however, af- 
ter correction for age, antibiotic use, day-care attendance, siblings 
and breastfeeding this difference was no longer signiﬁcant, indi- 
cating that this difference is likely to be caused largely by a lack of 
breastfeeding in the ﬁrst months of life as well as cumulative an- 
tibiotics use later on [14 , 26] . Our data are supported by de Freitas 
et al., who also described a signiﬁcantly lower abundance of Biﬁ- 
dobacterium in children with CF who used antibiotics, compared to 
healthy children, but in general found no independent difference 
between healthy children and children with CF [27] . Also, several 
other studies ﬁnd do differences in Biﬁdobacterium abundance be- 
tween healthy children and children with CF, though most of these 
studies lack information on the independent effects of antibiotics 
[14 , 28 , 29] . 
In contrast, pro-inﬂammatory bacteria such as E. coli were more 
abundant in the faecal microbiota of children with CF from the 
start, also after correction for antibiotic use, which is in line with 
previous observations by Nielsen et al. [29] In mice, high fat diets

intestines [31 , 32] . Differences in pH in the CF gut or possibly the 
growth of bacteria outside their typical niche (the growth of typi- 
cal “small intestine bacteria” in the large intestines) may also play 
a role [32] . Lastly, the use of antibiotics may also have an effect 
on the bacterial density of the gut, possibly lowering the bacterial 
load of the large intestines [33] . 
4.2. Effects of antibiotic treatment on the gut microbiota in children 
with CF 
From the literature we know that antibiotic treatment will of- 
ten lead to long lasting changes in the gut microbial composition 
and probably contributes substantially to the decreased microbial 
stability in the CF group. In this study we found a higher alpha 
diversity in infants with CF compared to healthy controls between 
month 2 and month 13, in contrast to previous literature, where 
CF has been associated with a lower alpha diversity in the gut 
[16 , 34 , 35] . However, further analyses showed that from 8 months 
on CF infants on antibiotics have a signiﬁcantly lower alpha di- 
versity than CF infants that do not use antibiotics. This may sug- 
gest that the alpha diversity of infants with CF only starts to de- 
cline when they are treated with repetitive or chronic antibiotic 
courses. A decreased alpha diversity has indeed been associated 
with cumulative antibiotic use in many diseases [17] . Additionally, 
Madan et al. found that type of feeding also plays an important 
role in microbial diversity, something we did not correct for in this 
study [15] . Due to our relative small group size we were unable to 
correct for several sources of heterogeneity within the CF group, 
while analysing the effects of antibiotics on the microbial com- 
position. among other factors, the pancreatic enzyme replacement 
therapy used by 75% of the CF group in this cohort might addition- 
ally have an effect on the microbial community composition and 
diversity. 
Cumulative antibiotic exposure in infants was associated with 
a decrease of Biﬁdobacterium. Biﬁdobacterium has been related to 
intestinal health and decrease of the abundance of Biﬁdobacterium 
has been observed in several medical conditions, including Crohn’s 
disease and type 2 diabetes [36 , 37] . Functions of Biﬁdobacterium in 
the gut include immune maturation, production of vitamin B and 
antioxidants, and protection against pathogens through the pro- 
duction of short chain fatty acids [38–40] . Two recent probiotic 
studies in CF patients, which included among other bacteria Biﬁ- 
dobacteria spp, indeed showed a decrease in intestinal inﬂamma- 
tion and pulmonary exacerbation, suggesting a positive clinical im- 
pact of Biﬁdobacterium supplements in CF patients [41 , 42] . In ad- 
dition to the negative association between Biﬁdobacterium and an- 
tibiotic use, several butyrate producing bacteria such as Eggerthella, 
Anaerostipes, Butyricicoccus and Ruminococcus also decreased upon 
antibiotic use. Based on the microbial community composition in 
faecal samples from patients with CF, Coffey et al. predicted that 
multiple genes involved in short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) produc- 
tion are enriched in CF faecal material, as well as butyrate, pro- 
duced by Ruminococcus, to be reduced [28] . All this is in line with 
the observation by Vernocchi et al. that stool samples from CF pa- 
tients contained less butyrate compared to healthy stool samples 
[43] . Potential consequences of lower concentrations of butyrate 
are a compromised mucosal barrier function, as well as increased 
inﬂammation levels in the gut [44 , 45] . 
In previous studies in other patient populations, different types 
of antibiotics have been associated with a lower abundance of En- 
terococci . However, in our study Enterococcus is signiﬁcantly more 
abundant in infants that used antibiotics. We hypothesize that an- 
tibiotic resistance of Enterococci species in children with CF may 
explain this observation. Children with CF generally receive pro- 
longed antibiotic courses, which probably facilitates antibiotic re- 
sistance development when compared with studies including pa- 
tient populations receiving short course antibiotic treatment [46–
48] . Although it cannot be ruled out that speciﬁc types of antibi- 
otics might have differential effects on Enterococcus and other bac- 
teria, unfortunately, this study lacks the power to analyse the ef- 
fects of different classes of antibiotics. However, several types of 
antibiotics such as cotrimoxazole and azithromycin, which are fre- 
quently used in this patient group, seem to have little effect on 
Enterococci spp . It might be worthwhile however, to study this as- 
pect in future studies in more detail, since antibiotic treatment is 
currently still the corner stone of treatment in CF. And since we 
cannot do without, it would be worth to ﬁnd classes that are ef- 
fective, but induce least harm on an ecological level. 
Recent literature has also shown a direct relation between gut 
microbiome, respiratory microbiome and respiratory health: both 
Hoen et al. and Madan et al. found associations between the mi- 
crobial composition in the gut and in the respiratory tract in young 
children with CF. Hoen et al. found microbial shifts in the overall 
gut microbial composition prior to exacerbations, and a signiﬁcant 
decrease in Parabacteroides abundance prior to colonization with 
Pseudomonas aeruginosa [10] . Madan et al. found that for several 
genera there was a direct correlation between the abundance in 
the gut and the respiratory tract, and that for other genera, such 
as Escherichia, Enterococcus and Blautia colonization of the gut pre- 
ceded the presence in the respiratory tract [10 , 11] .In our current 
dataset we lacked power to ﬁnd associations between the gut mi- 
crobial composition and respiratory outcomes. However, a study in 
healthy infants by Reyman et al. showed that early life gut mi- 
crobial composition is related to the subsequent number of res- 
piratory infections, with more infections in infants with low abun- 
dance of Biﬁdobacterium and a higher abundance of Klebsiella and 
Enterococcus [49] . In this study we observe, especially early in life, 
a lower abundance of Biﬁdobacterium in infants with CF, as well 
as a higher abundance of Enterococcus , possibly contributing to a 
more pro-inﬂammatory proﬁle [50] . 
4.3. Strengths and weaknesses 
The longitudinal set-up of this study allowed us to get a better 
understanding of differences in the development of the microbial 
composition over time between CF and non-CF infants. Because 
sampling started during the ﬁrst months of life, before start of an- 
tibiotics in most children, it was possible to evaluate the effect of 
the CF pathophysiology on the microbial composition in early life 
in an independent manner. In order to study differences between 
healthy and CF children, independent of other treatment or envi- 
ronmental factors, we corrected for the use of antibiotics. However, 
because of the sporadic use of antibiotics in the control group, and 
the repeated use of antibiotics in almost all CF infants above a cer- 
tain age, appropriate correction is diﬃcult. We therefore used dif- 
ferent statistical approaches to strengthen our conclusions. Finally, 
our cohort of infants with CF is quite small, limiting the power for 
speciﬁc analyses This is, in part, compensated by frequent resam- 
pling.

5. Conclusion 
This longitudinal study identiﬁed marked differences in gut 
microbiota between healthy and CF infants, both in terms of 
composition and in dynamic development over time. In addition, 
within the CF group, antibiotic treatment was independently asso- 
ciated with distinct differences in the gut microbiome composition. 
Hence, both the direct effects of the malfunctioning chloride chan- 
nels as well as the effects of cumulative antimicrobial treatment 
seem to play a role in the decreased abundance of beneﬁcial bacte- 
ria, and the decreased microbial stability observed in children with 
CF. The resulting deviation from healthy controls might indicate an 
increasing state of dysbiosis, which may have signiﬁcant functional 
consequences in metabolic function, barrier function and inﬂam- 
mation status of the host.
