Abstract 
22 
Background 
23 
The gut microbiota of infants changes over time and is affected by various 
24 
factors during the early life. However, rare study has explored the gut 
25 
microbiota development and affecting factors in the Chinese infant population. 
26 
Materials and methods 
27 
We enrolled 102 infants and collected stool samples from them at birth, 42 
28 
days, 3 months, and 6 months after delivery in order to characterize the 
29 
microbiota signatures and the effects of different factors that modulate the gut 
30 
microbiota diversity, composition and function over time. DNA extracted from 
31 
the bacteria in the stool samples was subjected to high-throughput sequencing 
32 
and bioinformatics analysis. 
33 
Results 
34 
Microbial richness and diversity increased significantly during the first six 
35 
months of life. Beneficial microbes such as Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and 
36 
Blautia were found to be increased in the infant’s gut at six months while 
37 
pathological 
bacteria 
such 
as 
Escherichia-Shigella, 
Enterobacter, 
38 
Staphylococcus, and Klebsiella decreased over time. The changes in the infant 
39 
delivery mode and infant-feeding mode only produced changes in the 
40 
microbial composition, whereas changes in bacterial richness, diversity and 
41 
effects sizes on the microbial architecture were all time-dependent. A 
42 
comparison of infant delivery modes conveyed a decrease in abundance of the 
43 
Downloaded from www.physiology.org/journal/physiolgenomics at American Univ of Beirut (193.188.128.021) on June 30, 2019.
3 
 
Bacteroidetes over time in the gut of infants born via C-section while the 
44 
Bifidobacterium was the most dominant genus in the vaginal delivery group.  
45 
Conclusions: The gut microbiota of infants changed extensively during the 
46 
first 6 months. Delivery and feeding modes were strong factors that 
47 
significantly affected microbial architecture and functions.

Introduction  
61 
The gut microbiota is involved in several important physiological functions 
62 
throughout life, including nutrient absorption, metabolism, vitamin production, 
63 
and immunity (25, 29). The relationship between the gut microbiota and 
64 
various diseases has been extensively investigated (9, 13, 15, 17); however, 
65 
relatively few studies have explored the changes in the composition and 
66 
function of the gut microbiota caused by natural events and environmental 
67 
pressures in infants. Yassour et al investigated the natural history of infant gut 
68 
microbiome during the first 3 years in Finland and described the effects of 
69 
multi-factors that shape the gut microbiota (34). Wang described the gut 
70 
microbiomes of 98 Swedish mothers and their infants during the first year and 
71 
identified the signatures of microbial functional maturation (6). Recently, the 
72 
gut microbiota of more than 10,000 infant stool samples from United States 
73 
and European in a large cohort (TEDDY study) showed that the gut microbiota 
74 
experienced a three-phase alteration before stabilization and breast milk was 
75 
the most significant factor that shape the gut microbiota composition (28). 
76 
Evidence has shown that dysbiosis of the gut microbiota in the early stages of 
77 
life are associated with diseases, such as malnutrition, allergic disorders, 
78 
inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, and necrotizing enterocolitis (3, 7, 22, 24, 
79 
26).  
80 
Many factors, such as delivery mode, feeding mode, and drug treatment, affect 
81 
the diversity and architecture of the gut microbiota (6). Madan et al. confirmed 
82 
Downloaded from www.physiology.org/journal/physiolgenomics at American Univ of Beirut (193.188.128.021) on June 30, 2019.
6 
 
that infant delivery and feeding modes were significantly and independently 
83 
associated with gut microbiota architecture at six weeks of age (23). The 
84 
authors reported that vaginal delivery was characterized by higher 
85 
abundances of Bacteroides and Pectobacterium, and lower abundances of 
86 
Staphylococcus, Rothia, and Propionibacterium. In addition, they also 
87 
suggested that the delivery mode has a comparative greater impact on the 
88 
infant gut microbiota compared to feeding mode. The Lactococcus genus is a 
89 
group of species that produces lactic acid from glucose fermentation. They 
90 
have been identified to act as the key discriminatory microbe between different 
91 
feeding modes (23). The Bifidobacterium genus and Collinsella genus were 
92 
discovered to appear later in the gut of infants born via cesarean section 
93 
compared to those born via vaginal delivery (10). Although many studies have 
94 
highlighted the close association between delivery mode and the gut 
95 
microbiota in infants, some controversy still remains. For example, after 
96 
sampling and sequencing the microbiota of multiple sites(stool, oral gingiva, 
97 
nares, skin and vagina) in the human body, Chu et al. suggested that body 
98 
location rather than delivery mode was the crucial factor in determining the 
99 
infant gut microbiota within six weeks after birth (8). In a large cohort study, 
100 
Gerber et al. demonstrated that antibiotic use during the first six months after 
101 
birth was not associated with diseases that involved changes in the gut 
102 
microbiota (16). In all the aforementioned studies, we noted that the sampling 
103 
time of the samples was all different which might have contributed to the 
104 
Downloaded from www.physiology.org/journal/physiolgenomics at American Univ of Beirut (193.188.128.021) on June 30, 2019.
7 
 
inconsistencies in data obtained. Another factor that has been recently brought 
105 
to attention is the effect of race and ethnicity on diversity and composition of 
106 
microbiota. Cemal et al. carried out a study that compares the sulfidogenic 
107 
bacterial 
abundance 
between 
African 
Americans 
and 
non-Hispanic 
108 
Caucasians. The results demonstrated that there was a higher abundance of 
109 
the sulfidogenic bacteria in the African American group. Therefore, ethnicity 
110 
has also been considered as an important factor in gut microbiota studies, 
111 
which could shape the gut microbiota’s composition and diversity (27). 
112 
However, there are no current published studies that investigate the changes 
113 
in microbiota composition and abundance in Chinese infants.  
114 
Hence, to provide additional evidence for the signatures of gut microbiota 
115 
shortly after birth and investigate potential impacts of various factors on the gut 
116 
microbiota, we performed a study that focuses on the changes in gut 
117 
microbiota belonging to 102 Chinese infants. Our study primarily explores the 
118 
factors that influences the gut microbiota during the first 6 months after birth. 
119 
We collected fecal samples from Chinese infants and sequenced the microbial 
120 
DNA. The samples were collected at four different time points from the same 
121 
infant. By analyzing the microbial DNA, we compared the effects of sex, 
122 
delivery mode, feeding mode, development, and antibiotic use on the changes 
123 
in gut microbiota over time.

Materials and Methods 
126 
Downloaded from www.physiology.org/journal/physiolgenomics at American Univ of Beirut (193.188.128.021) on June 30, 2019.

1. Enrollment of infants and specimen collection 
127 
In this study, we enrolled infants that were born in Shanghai Tenth People’s 
128 
Hospital affiliated to Tongji University. After obtaining signed informed consents 
129 
from parents of the infants, we subjected every infant to checkups and were 
130 
vetted to be healthy by obstetricians. Stool samples were collected at birth, 42 
131 
days, 3 months, and 6 months after birth. Collected stool samples were 
132 
immediately transferred on ice from the hospital or home to the laboratory to 
133 
be analyzed within two hours. Stool samples were stored at -80°C until further 
134 
analysis. The infants were prospectively monitored for infections, allergic 
135 
symptoms such as wheezing and eczema, antibiotic treatment, and feeding 
136 
mode. Information regarding the height and weight were recorded at all four 
137 
time periods, and evaluated to be lower, medium and higher weight according 
138 
to the growth standards used in China (Supplementary Table 1). Information 
139 
associated with the infant delivery was obtained from the digital medical 
140 
records system at the hospital. The study protocol was approved by the ethics 
141 
committee of Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital and was performed in 
142 
accordance with the ethical standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and 
143 
its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. 
144 
 
145 
2. DNA extraction, sequencing, and bioinformatics analysis 
146 
The stool procedures in this study were carried out in the same manner as 
147 
those that have been detailed in a previous study (14). In brief, DNA was 
148 
Downloaded from www.physiology.org/journal/physiolgenomics at American Univ of Beirut (193.188.128.021) on June 30, 2019.
9 
 
extracted from the stool samples using a E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega 
149 
Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA). Extracted DNA was used for amplifying the 
150 
hypervariable V3-4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene by using primers 515F and 
151 
806R. The final DNA concentration and purity were verified using a NanoDrop 
152 
2000 UV-vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, MA, 
153 
USA). DNA quality was confirmed by subjecting it to a 1% agarose gel 
154 
electrophoresis. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was then set and performed 
155 
according to the following parameters: 3 minutes of denaturation at 95°C; 
156 
followed by 27 cycles of 30 s at 95°C, 30 s of annealing at 55°C, and 45 s of 
157 
elongation at 72°C; and a final extension at 72°C for 10 minutes. All the 
158 
aforementioned procedures stemming from DNA collection up to PCR 
159 
amplification reactions to obtain a final volume of 20 μL were performed in 
160 
triplicates for each sample. The PCR products were extracted and purified 
161 
using an AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, 
162 
USA) and quantified using QuantiFluor™-ST (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) in 
163 
accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols. 
164 
Purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar amounts and sequenced 
165 
(paired-end; 2 × 300) on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, 
166 
USA). Raw FASTQ files were de-multiplexed, quality-filtered with Trimmomatic, 
167 
and merged with FLASH. Bases lower than 20 on the tails of the reads were 
168 
filtered, the minimum overlap was 10 bp when merging the paired reads, the 
169 
maximum mismatch ratio was 0.2 in the overlap, and the maximum barcode 
170 
Downloaded from www.physiology.org/journal/physiolgenomics at American Univ of Beirut (193.188.128.021) on June 30, 2019.
10 
 
and primer mismatch number were set to 0 and 2, respectively. Operational 
171 
taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at a threshold of 97% similarity using 
172 
the UPARSE platform (version 7.1; http://drive5.com/uparse/). Chimeric 
173 
sequences were then identified and removed using Uchime (version 4.2.40; 
174 
http://drive5.com/usearch/manual/uchime_algo.html), 
and 
a 
taxonomic 
175 
analysis for representative sequences was performed for each OTU. The 
176 
taxonomy of each 16S rRNA gene sequence was analyzed using an RDP 
177 
Classifier algorithm (http://rdp.cme.msu.edu/). After OTU identification, a series 
178 
of analyses were performed; including bacterial abundance (Ace and Chao 
179 
index) and bacterial diversity (Shannon index) comparisons in the gut 
180 
microbiota. A linear discriminant analysis coupled with effect size (LEfSe) was 
181 
used to identify the taxa that were significantly increased in each group. 
182 
Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity 
183 
(adonis) was used to evaluate differences in microbial structure. The number of 
184 
permutations for comparing microbial differences was set to 999. 
185 
Co-abundance analysis was performed using the Cytoscape platform (version 
186 
3.4.0; http://www.cytoscape.org/). Multivariate analysis was carried out using 
187 
the association between continuous or categorical variates and gut microbiota 
188 
composition (based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity) in the vegan R package. 
189 
Multivariate analysis of variance and linear correlations were used to analyze 
190 
categorical and continuous variates, respectively (12). Effect size was 
191 
evaluated according to the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity (35). Function prediction

197 
3. Statistical analysis 
198 
All data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0 (IBM, New York, NY, USA) and 
199 
GraphPad Prism 7.00 (San Diego, CA, USA). Continuous variables were 
200 
expressed as a mean ± standard deviation. The chi-square test or Fisher’s 
201 
exact test was used to assess categorical variables difference. Differences in 
202 
continuous variables were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance. The 
203 
Pearson and Spearman correlation tests were used to explore associations 
204 
between bacterial genera. The Mann-Whitney U-test was used to compare 
205 
discrepancies at taxonomic levels between different groups. Differences with P 
206 
values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All comparisons were 
207 
corrected for the false discovery rate using the Bonferroni method.

Evaluation of microbiota diversity, enterotypes, and impact factors 
223 
during the first 6 months 
224 
The number of OTUs, bacterial abundance and α-diversity (Shannon index)

and the three-month time period. Finally, we found eight different enterotypes

232

233

To obtain a general overview of these factors that affected the gut microbiota,

375

Discussion  
385 
Although a similar investigation on infant gut microbiota has been reported 
386 
before, our study is the first one to report on gut microbiota changes in 
387 
population and diversity in large sample size of Chinese infants and 
388 
encompasses four different time points. The present study showed that the 
389 
microbiota abundance and diversity all increased after birth and remained 
390 
relatively constant until another significant increase at the six month time 
391 
period. Our findings were consistent with other published data that nutrition 
392 
and delivery mode showed profound impact on the gut microbiota (6). Our 
393 
study also showed that the gut microbiota became more diverse over time, 
394 
which was in line with the changes in the Shannon index. The 
395 
Escherichia-Shigella and Bacteroides population diminished significantly, while 
396 
Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium increased gradually over time. This 
397 
suggests that the early gut environment in infants was suitable to facilitate 
398 
facultative anaerobic metabolism and Escherichia growth. Through time, the 
399 
gut environment gradually transformed into one that was suitable for the 
400 
growth of bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium. These 
401 
observations reflect the transformation of a dynamic microbial community into 
402 
a relatively stable one. During our study, we also noted that Staphylococcus 
403 
was also found in our study, which we speculate that it was derived from the 
404 
mother’s skin (11).  
405 
Our investigation demonstrated that the delivery modes played a role in 
406

21 
 
altering microbiota composition during the early 6 months of infant. We found 
407 
that microbial diversity was lower at 42 days and three months compared to 
408 
the six-month time period. By examining the taxonomic changes associated 
409 
with delivery mode, our results show that the most abundant phylum in the 
410 
vaginal delivery group was the Bacteroidetes. Its relative abundance was 
411 
lower in the cesarean delivery group compared to those in the vaginal delivery 
412 
group at all time points. At the genus level, Escherichia coli was the most 
413 
dominant microbe in the vaginal delivery group at all time points, which was 
414 
consistent with previous reports (6) (1). On the other hand, the Bacteroides 
415 
genus was less abundant in the cesarean group than in the vaginal delivery 
416 
group, and remained at lower proportions at 3 and 6 months. Our findings 
417 
contrasts to that of other studies, which show a different pattern of Bacteroides 
418 
bacterial growth. Studies have demonstrated that the abundance of 
419 
Bacteroides was lower in the cesarean section groups, however, a study 
420 
carried by Moran et al study showed a higher relative abundance of 
421 
Bacteroides (34). The same study by Moran et al study also suggested that the 
422 
low Bacteroides signature was also not associated with the mothers’ antibiotic 
423 
use, the gestational age, nor the enemas before surgery. By studying the 
424 
relationship between the low abundance of Bacteroides with antibiotics use, 
425 
we found that there was a negative correlation between the two. Although we 
426 
could not identify possible reasons for this controversy, it is quite necessary to 
427 
study it in other dependent infant cohorts.

We also observed that the abundances of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium 
429 
increased with time. This phenomenon could be explained by the presence of 
430 
oligosaccharide substrates obtained from human milk, which supports the 
431 
growth of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium rather than the growth of 
432 
Bacteroides (18). Bifidobacterium colonization in the gut has been reported to 
433 
be beneficial for the host, where it was found to present in higher abundance in 
434 
infants born via vaginal delivery. (30). However, another study provided 
435 
evidence  that high abundance of Bifidobacterium was associated with 
436 
increased risk of allergic disorders later in life (22). One study suggested that 
437 
the structure difference of gut microbiota within the different delivery modes 
438 
was absent at 42 days of age; however, our data showed that the difference 
439 
was still present at 6 months (8). The predicted microbial functions significantly 
440 
differed between the two delivery modes. 
441 
In addition to the delivery mode, our investigation demonstrates that the 
442 
feeding mode was also another important factor that shapes the gut microbiota. 
443 
The richness and diversity of the gut microbiota did not change significantly 
444 
when studying the effect of feeding mode, which deserve more investigation in 
445 
the future. Moreover, the infant gut microbiota had been reported to be 
446 
affected by leptin and insulin in human milk, which are known to be associated 
447 
with intestinal permeability and inflammation, thus contributing to the 
448 
differences in the gut microbiota (20).  Microbiota function annotations 
449 
revealed that lipopolysaccharide (LPS) biosynthesis–associated genes were

enriched in the BFD group in this study. Cross-continent cohort studies has 
451 
shown that exposure to LPS derived from Bacteroides frequently translates to 
452 
a stronger immune response which was associated with the activation of 
453 
innate immunity. This has therefore been linked to an increase in the risk of 
454 
developing autoimmune diseases; however, exposure to LPS from E. coli had 
455 
opposite effects (31). In contrast, lower concentrations of LPS in the feces 
456 
were associated with atopy and wheezing later in infant (5).Therefore, the 
457 
relationship between LPS and clinical outcome needs to be further evaluated 
458 
in our potential future studies. 
459 
Antibiotic use is a continuously occurring issue in the world because of its 
460 
involvement in several issues, one of which is antibiotic resistance. Resistance 
461 
to antibiotic can occur in one of three mechanisms; natural resistance, genetic 
462 
mutation, or through acquiring from another species. One study has shown 
463 
that antibiotic resistance can be increased by Bacteroides colonization in early 
464 
life and induced by increased Enterobacteriaceae in preterm neonates (4, 32). 
465 
Many studies have identified an association between disrupted gut microbiota 
466 
in early life and subsequent long-term risk of disorders, such as high body 
467 
mass index and inflammatory bowel diseases (19, 21). We were unable to find 
468 
any existing relationships between antibiotic use and alteration of gut 
469 
microbiota structure at 6 months. This may have been a result of long-term 
470 
antibiotic dose, limited number of antibiotic cases and long-time period 
471 
(average, 89.1 days [median, 108.5 days] after exposure to antibiotics in our

study), (6). Several studies have confirmed the impact of of antibiotics on the 
473 
occurrence of common diseases. Yamamoto-Hanada et al. revealed that 
474 
antibiotic treatment in the first two years of life resulted in an increase in the 
475 
rate of allergic disorders, such as asthma, atopic dermatitis, and allergic rhinitis 
476 
at the age of 5 years (33). Ahmadizer et al concluded from a meta-analysis that 
477 
antibiotics use was correlated to an increased risk of developing allergic 
478 
symptoms (2). 
479 
To sum up, our study characterizes gut microbial signatures and dynamic 
480 
traces at birth, 42 days, three months, and six months after birth. We explored 
481 
the influence of important factors such as delivery mode, feeding mode, growth 
482 
and development, and antibiotic use on the architecture and function of gut 
483 
microbiota. The findings of our study have provided extensive evidence and 
484 
valuable insights into the alterations of infant gut microbiota caused by the 
485 
aforementioned various factors. We hope that our study provides sufficient and 
486 
necessary evidence for reducing potentially occurring disorders in later life by 
487 
modulating the gut microbiota at an earlier stage during the infancy stage.
