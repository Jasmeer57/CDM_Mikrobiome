Abstract 
Aim: Microbiota dysbiosis in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) have been widely 
reported. Gut microbiota connect diet to metabolism by producing small molecules 
through diverse metabolic pathways. However, little is known about the correlation 
among metabolic activity, microbiota composition, and dietary components in IBD.  
Methods: We investigated the dietary preferences of patients in an IBD cohort (including 
ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD) patients) and analyzed their gut 
microbiota using 16S rRNA gene sequencing and metagenomic analysis of fecal and 
biopsy samples. We then examined the metabolite profiles of the samples using gas and 
liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry analyses.  
Results: Daily intakes of folic acid, niacin, vitamin C, vitamin D, calcium, and selenium 
differed significantly between IBD and healthy controls. Detection of metabolites 
indicated that the decrease of long-chain fatty acids (arachidic acid, and oleic acid et al.), 
medium-chain fatty acids (sebacic acid and isocaproic acid), and bile acid in IBD patients. 
Compared with healthy people, 21 microbial species (such as Sulfolobus acidocaldarius, 
and Clostridium clostridioforme CAG132 et al.) were identified to correlate to diet and 
metabolites in UC group, and the number is 37 in CD group (such as Bacteroides fragilis,

and Fusobacterium nucleatum et al.). Bacteroides fragilis, which was an example, 
enriched in IBD patients and associated with multi-nutrients, and 21 metabolites 
including 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 and taurolithocholic acid. 
 
Conclusions: This study provides a network to identify key micronutrients, microbiota 
components and metabolites that contribute to IBD.

Introduction 
 
The inflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs), which include Crohn’s disease (CD) 
and ulcerative colitis (UC), is a chronic inflammatory condition of the gastrointestinal 
tract. The incidence of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) varies from 0.60–3.44 per 
100,000 in Asia [1], and has increased in Hong Kong from 1 case per million in 1985 
to over 30 per million in 2014 [2]. The generally-accepted pathogenesis of IBD is 
complex, resulting in an excessive immune reaction to the gut luminal microbiota in 
genetically-susceptible individuals. Decreases in biodiversity and the relative 
abundance of butyrate-producing bacterial genera such as Faecalibacterium, 
Bifidobacterium, and Lactobacillus have been reported in IBD [3]. Furthermore, 
some researches have indicated that the structure of mucosa-associated bacterial 
communities varies from those found in feces [4], and noted differences between 
inflammatory and non-inflammatory regions of the gut [5,6].  
Dietary preference is recognized as an important environmental factor affecting 
microbial community composition. Long-term dietary patterns affected the ratios of 
Firmicutes, Bacteroides, and Prevotella in the gut more so than short-term changes 
[7], while a strict vegan or vegetarian diet was associated with obvious decreases in 
the abundance of Enterobacteriaceae, Bacteroides, and Bifidobacterium species [8]. 
Children fed a high-fiber, plant-based diet in Burkina Faso had a markedly divergent 
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
 
gut microbial composition compared with their European counterparts, whose daily 
diet was richer in sugar, fat, and protein [
 
9]. In addition, a diet rich in red meat 
aggravated dextran sodium sulphate-induced colitis in mice, resulting in higher 
disease activity and histopathological scores [10]. Therefore, alterations in the gut 
microbiota community structure and function as a result of westernized diets may 
trigger and maintain autoimmunity by promoting the emergence of pathobionts.  
De Preter et al. [11] found fewer metabolites overall and decreased 
concentrations of pentanoate, hexanoate, heptanoate, octanoate, and nonanoate in the 
gut of IBD patients. However, little is known about the correlations among metabolic 
activity, microbiota composition, and dietary components in IBD, or whether these 
factors are related to IBD pathogenesis. 
Therefore, in this study, we investigated the dietary preferences of patients from 
an IBD cohort before analyzing the gut microbiota of the same patients using fecal 
specimens and inflammatory and non-inflammatory mucosal biopsy samples. We 
then examined the metabolic profiles of the patients using both gas and liquid 
chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC/MS and LC/MS) analyses to explore the 
interactions among gut microbiota composition, dietary components, and metabolites 
in relation to IBD.

Methods 
 
Participants  
Participants (n = 322) were recruited between April 2014 and April 2015 from 
Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University, China. Study participants comprised 
healthy controls (HC, n = 42), ulcerative colitis patients (UC, n = 107), and Crohn's 
disease patients (CD, n = 173). The UC and CD patients formed the IBD cohort. 
Informed consent was obtained from all subjects in accordance with the institutional 
research ethics committee guidelines. The protocol was approved by the Medical 
Ethics 
Committee 
of 
Nanfang 
Hospital, 
Southern 
Medical 
University 
(NFEC-2014-040). Inclusion criteria included age >18 years and confirmed IBD 
diagnosis using standard endoscopy, radiology, and histopathology results. 
Participants meeting the following criteria were excluded from the study: 1) use of 
antibiotics, probiotics, prebiotics, or yoghurt within the last 4 weeks; 2) current 
infectious gastroenteritis; 3) advanced organ disease; 4) taking part in other clinical 
trials; 5) BMI >25; and 6) pregnant or lactating. Clinical data on age, sex, smoking 
status, extent of colitis, disease duration, disease activity (Crohn’s disease activity 
index (CDAI) and Mayo score), extra-intestinal manifestations, complications, 
previous surgery, and current treatments (anthropometric measurements, clinical 
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
 
history and status, and medication history) were collected. Active UC patients were 
defined as Mayo score >2, while active CD was defined as CDAI >150. 
 
Dietary and nutritional assessment  
The 2010 Chinese Residents of Nutrition and Health Status Monitoring 
Semi-Quantitative Food Questionnaire (CNHS2010-J1) was applied to assess 
participants’ habitual dietary intake. The mean frequency and quantity of food or 
beverage items consumed by participants during the past year were investigated. 
Nutrient intake was obtained by multiplying the frequency of consumption of each 
food item by its nutrient content based on tables provided by the China Nutrition 
Society. The food nutrition calculator designed by the Nutrition and Food Safety 
Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention was used to identify subjects at 
risk of malnutrition.  
Collection of fecal and mucosal Sampling 
Fresh fecal specimens were collected in sterile 1.5-mL tubes prior to preparation 
of the bowel for biopsy. Mucosal biopsies were taken from inflammatory and 
non-inflammatory regions of the bowel of each patient. All samples were separately 
placed into sterile cryovials containing RNAlater solution (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) 
and stored at −80°C until DNA extraction. 
16S rRNA gene sequencing and quality control of reads 
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
 
Total DNA was isolated from the feces and biopsy samples with DNA Stool and 
DNA Extraction kits (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), respectively, according to the 
manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentrations and purity were determined by 
spectrophotometric analysis (ND-2000 spectrophotometer, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 
Waltham, MA, USA). 16S rRNA gene sequencing targeting the V4 variable region 
was done using the Illumina MiSeq system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with 
barcoded primers. Paired-end reads of 250 bp in length in each direction were 
generated. Then the overlapping reads were stitched. Pandaseq was used to assemble 
the raw paired-end reads. Then resulting high-quality sequences were treated to filter 
singletons and remove chimeras. 
 
Metagenomic sequencing and quality control of reads 
Metagenomic DNA libraries were constructed from 2-μg aliquots of genomic 
DNA extracted from each sample with a NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for 
Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Library quality was assessed 
using an Agilent bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with a 
DNA LabChip 1000 kit. Using the Illumina HiSeq X10 system sequencing was 
performed to get 7 Gb of sequence per sample, producing 150-bp paired-end reads.  
Raw paired-end reads were processed. SOAPaligner version 2.21 was used for 
sequence alignment with the parameters “-m 100 -× 1000”, which correspond to 
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
 
“reporting all repeat hits, minimal insert size of 100 bp, maximum insert size of 
1000”. 
The 
detailed 
information 
can 
be 
found 
at 
 
http://soap.genomics.org.cn/soapaligner.html. Further analysis was done with the 
resulting high-quality reads. 
Diversity and differentially-abundant feature identification 
Microbial diversity analyses were implemented in QIIME, a Vegan package for 
R. The assembled reads were clustered into operational taxonomic units using scripts 
from USEARCH (version 7.0.1090), defined at a 97% similarity level. The Shannon 
index was used to evaluate the α diversity, while principal coordinate analysis was 
used to analyze the β diversity. Linear discriminate analysis effect size (LEfSe) 
analysis was used to determine differentially-abundant features. 
De novo assembly and gene catalog construction 
SOAPdenovo 28 (version 2.04) was applied to assemble the sequences into 
contigs using the parameters “-M 3 -u -d 1 -F -K 53”. Detailed information on the 
parameters used can be found at http://soap.genomics.org.cn/soapdenovo.html.  
MetaGeneMark31 (version 3.26) was applied to identify open reading frames 
within the contigs generated for every sample using a length threshold of 100 bp. A 
non-redundant gene catalog was then constructed using CD-HIT32 (version 4.5.7), 
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
 
and redundant genes were removed using a cut-off of 0.95 in sequence identity and 
an aligned length coverage of >90% of the shorter sequences. 
 
Metagenomic species analysis 
For comparison of the fecal microbiomes of the CD and HC cohorts, 
differentially-abundant genetic markers were identified (P-values obtained by 
Wilcoxon rank sum test of <0.05). Comparisons between the UC and HC cohorts 
were performed using the same method. Genes were clustered into metagenomic 
species (MGS) as described previously [12]. Genes with a Spearman correlation 
co-efficient (rho) value of > 0.8 using complete-linkage clustering were clustered, 
then clusters which had more than 25 genes that had a rho >0.9 were fused. 
Gene functional classification 
Putative amino acid sequences of the predicted genes were aligned against 
proteins in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database 
(released 2014-12-09) using the basic local alignment search tool. Based on the 
highest annotated scoring hit(s) containing at least one HSP scoring over 60 bits, each 
gene was distributed to one or more EC. To calculate the abundance of the KEGG 
ortholog groups, we added together the abundance of each of the proteins assigned to 
the same KEGG ortholog group and designated the resulting value as the abundance 
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
 
of the corresponding KEGG ortholog group. Profiles of the KEGG ortholog groups 
were then generated. 
 
Metabolomics data acquisition  
Metabolomics assays and analyses were performed by Biotree Biological 
Technology Co. (Shanghai, China). Fecal polar metabolites were analyzed with an 
Agilent 7890 gas chromatography system coupled with a Pegasus HT time-of-flight 
mass spectrometer. Fecal molecular lipids were analyzed using an UHPLC system 
(1290, Agilent Technologies) with a UPLC BEH amide column (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 
mm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) coupled to a Triple TOF 6600 system (Q-TOF; AB 
Sciex, Concord, Ontario, Canada).  
Metabolomics data analysis 
Retained peaks were normalized to the quality control sample to ensure quality 
during metabolic profiling. Principal component analysis (PCoA) and orthogonal 
projections to latent structure-discriminate analysis (OPLS-DA) were done to 
discriminate patients from control subjects using the SIMCA version 14.1 software 
package (MKS Data Analytics Solutions, Umea, Sweden). Metabolites responsible 
for differences between patients and controls in the metabolic profile scan were 
defined based on a variable importance in projection threshold of 1.0 from the 10-fold 
cross-validated OPLS-DA model. The KEGG database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) 
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
 
and MetaboAnalyst (http://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) were used to annotate and 
identify the pathways for each of the metabolites.  
 
 
Statistical analyses 
Data were analyzed using SPSS version 19.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). 
Non-parametric Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon tests were performed to examine the 
differences in age at recruitment, duration, daily intake per capita of different food 
groups, and daily intake per capita of nutrients between the patient groups. 
Differences in other clinical characteristics were examined using χ2 tests. The 
morbidity rates associated with fistulas, intestinal stenosis, and enterobrosis, as well 
as the daily intake per capita of milk, were compared between the groups using 
Fisher’s exact test. Differences in laboratory examinations among the participants 
were examined by one-way analysis of variance. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was 
applied to find differentially-abundant genes between IBD patients and health 
controls. Significantly different metabolites in patients and controls were identified 
by independent sample t-test. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to 
analyze the correlation between dietary components and microbiota composition, and 
the correlation between microbiota composition and metabolites. A P-value of <0.05 
was considered statistically significant.

Results 
 
Clinical information 
In total, 280 patients with IBD (107 UC, 173 CD) and 42 healthy individuals 
were enrolled in this study. As a group, CD patients had the lowest body mass index 
(BMI). The percentages of patients with a BMI <18 was 20.6% and 36.4% for the UC 
and CD groups, respectively. Only 11.1% of patients with IBD took mineral 
supplements and 11.8% took vitamins. Mineral supplements mainly consisted of 
calcium, compound minerals, and iron. Usually, patients receive supplements of 
minerals and vitamins when treated with Glucocorticoid and 5-aminosalicylic acid, 
but the supplementation of minerals and vitamins is not related to disease activity, 
duration or distribution (Table 1). Laboratory examinations showed significant 
differences in serum hemoglobin, albumin, globulin, calcium, magnesium, 
phosphorus, copper, iron, and folic acid levels among the groups, with the lowest 
serum levels observed in the CD patients (Table S1). In addition, deficiency of  
vitamin D was observed in the IBD group overall, but was most prominent in the CD 
cohort. 
Dietary categories and nutrient intake  
Mean consumption of each food item over the year prior to the study was 
investigated, with results classified into 10 dietary categories and by nutrients. 
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
 
Reduced daily intake of vegetables, fish, and shellfish and increased intake of eggs 
was observed for the IBD patients, especially those with CD. In addition, the daily 
intake of milk and dairy products, mainly yogurt and formula powder for nutritional 
therapy, was increased among CD patients (Table S2). Daily intakes of dietary 
nutrients were calculated according to the standard measurement provided by the 
China Nutrition Society and the food nutrition calculator (Table 2). CD patients had 
the lowest intake of folic acid and vitamin C, while niacin and vitamin D intakes were 
lowest in the UC and HC groups, respectively. Although there were differences in the 
intake of some nutrients, there was no obvious difference in the intake of energy and 
major nutrients (consisting of protein, fat, and carbohydrate) between IBD patients 
and normal people. 
 
Microbial dysbiosis in IBD 
To compare the differences between mucosa and feces, we selected patients who 
provided both mucosa and feces samples for further analysis. Thus, a subgroup of 113 
participants, including 31 UC patients, 58 CD patients and 24 healthy individuals 
(Table S3) were enrolled. Totally, 324 samples including 113 fecal samples, and 211 
biopsies samples (including different regions from one patient) (detailed information 
in Table S3) were detected by 16S rRNA sequencing.

Shotgun metagenome-based identification of microbial gene differences and 
metabolic pathways 
A subset of 81 fecal samples used for 16S rRNA gene sequencing (UC = 25, CD 
= 41, HC = 15) were then subjected to whole-genome shotgun sequencing. Bacterial 
community composition varied significantly as a function of the overall gene richness, 
with a loss of richness associated with IBD [4]. Results showed that gene richness 
was much lower in samples from IBD patients compared with the HC (UC = 175,596, 
CD = 186,789, and HC = 237,757 genes). Microbial genes were clustered into MGS 
according to their abundance profiles to further explore their correlations with UC 
and CD. Compared with the HC, 36 and 21 MGS were enriched in the UC (Figure 3a) 
and CD (Figure 3b) samples, respectively. A high proportion of MGS, including 
Bacteroides (n = 10) and Streptococcus (n = 3) were enriched in the UC fecal samples, 
while the majority of MGS enriched in the CD samples belonged to the genera 
Clostridium (n = 8) and Bacteroides (n = 5).

metabolism was the most abundant KEGG ortholog, followed by amino acid

observed a global decrease in orthologs including the metabolism of carbohydrates, 
lipids, other amino acids, nucleotides, terpenoids and polyketides, cofactors, and 
vitamins in the UC and CD samples. Patients with CD had an increased abundance of 
genes involved in energy metabolism, glycan biosynthesis and metabolism, and the 
biodegradation and metabolism of xenobiotics compared with the HC. In comparison, 
in the abundance of genes associated with metabolism in the UC samples any 
significant differences was not observed (Figure 4b). 
 
KEGG pathway analysis also identified differences between the samples (Figure 
4c-d). Pathways associated with acid anhydrides were enriched in the UC samples 
(Figure 4c), while enriched pathways in the CD samples (Figure 4d) contributed to 
glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, the pentose phosphate pathway for carbohydrate 
metabolism, lipopolysaccharide metabolism, and glycan biosynthesis and metabolism, 
as has been observed previously [15]. Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, which showed 
decreased abundance in IBD patients compared with the HC, contributed pathway 
components related to the metabolism of energy, carbohydrates, amino acids, 
nucleotides, cofactors, and vitamins.

relationship between diet, gut microbiota and metabolites in IBD patients, which also

Discussion 
 
In the present study, we assessed the macronutrients and micronutrients, 
investigated the feces-associated and mucosa-associated microbiota and metabolites 
of IBD patients, and provided a network to identify key micronutrients, microbiota 
components and metabolites that contribute to IBD.  
The intestinal microbiota is connected to diet through bacterial metabolism, 
producing numerous small molecules via metabolic pathways that mediate the effects 
of dietary nutrients on human health [16,17]. We found that vitamin deficiencies are 
general in IBD patients. The daily intake of minerals including calcium, kalium, 
magnesium and selenium were also lower in IBD than HC. Further analysis showed 
the correlation between dietary nutrients especially vitamins and minerals and many 
genera in IBD.  
Sequencing indicated dysbiosis and low richness of the complex gut microbial 
community from IBD samples. Enterococcus was increased in the feces and mucosa 
of IBD patients. Several genera enriched in IBD also resided in the inflamed mucosa. 
Such as, Halomonas, Lactobacillus and Shewanella were enriched in CD, and 
Bdellovibrio and Rhodobacter were increased in UC. The pathogenic or resistant 
effects of these inflamed enriched organisms should be validated in future studies. In 
addition, the abundance of B. fragilis was found to increase in both the UC and CD 
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
 
samples, and further analysis suggested that this species was correlated with the 
abundance of 25-hydroxyvitamin D3. Previous studies reported that Bacteroides 
participatied in bile acid metabolism by deconjugating bile acid [18], and it also has a 
positive linear trend with maternal plasma 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 concentration 
during pregnancy [19]. These results indicated the important regulation role of 
Bacteroides. Another differentially-abundant bacterial species in the current study 
was F. nucleatum, which was negatively correlated with diet and associated with 12 
different dietary metabolites. Studies have shown that Fusobacterium is involved in 
putrescine and H2S production pathways that function to metabolize undigested 
proteins. Fusobacterium species are also associated with putrefaction products such 
as putrescine and cresol, implicated them in the disease pathogenesis of colorectal 
cancer [
 
20]. Our analysis indicated that increased consumption of fish and shellfish 
might decrease the relative abundance of F. nucleatum and its positively-correlated 
metabolites such as lithocholic acid in patients with CD.   
Dysbiosis of intestinal flora in IBD patients results in changes of metabolites. 
Here long-chain fatty acids including arachidic acid, oleic acid and and tridecanoic 
acid, and medium-chain fatty acids including sebacic acid and isocaproic acid showed 
to reduce in IBD samples. Medium-chain fatty acids and modified polyunsaturated 
fatty acids of long-chain fatty acids were shown to bind to peroxisome 
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
 
proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR)-γ and -α [
 
21]. Activation of PPAR-γ and -α 
suppressed NF-κB activation and the production of proinflammatory cytokines [22]. 
Binding of conjugated linoleic acid to PPAR-γ inhibited inflammation and alleviated 
experimental colitis and active CD [23,24]. Therefore, the microbiota-dependent 
metabolism of medium- and long-chain fatty acids is related to PPAR-γ activation 
and subsequent suppression of inflammation. We also found that bile acids including 
lithocholic acid, chenodeoxycholate, and taurolithocholic acid were consistently 
significantly decreased in the IBD samples. Lithocholic acid could active vitamin D 
receptor, which directly intervened in dendritic cell differentiation and 
proinflammatory cytokines production to protect the gut from bile acid toxicity 
[25,26]. Lithocholic acid and taurolithocholic acid could also active transmembrane 
G protein-coupled receptor 5 [27], which inhibited the production of some cytokines 
and might be involved in regulating immune responses in IBD patients [28]. 
Therefore, the decrease in bile acid production triggered by intestinal dysbiosis might 
lead to the loss of anti-inflammatory effects on intestinal epithelial cells.  
    Based on the analysis of nutrients, gut microbiota and metabolites in IBD 
patients, we construct the diet-microbiota-metabolite interaction networks. As shown 
in Fig. 5, dietary nutrients especially vitamins and minerals correlated with many 
genera in IBD. Vitamin A and vitamin D had the most correlations with microbiota. 
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
 
There were eight and five key species were found in UC and CD respectively. We 
noticed that the key species correlated with 20 or more metabolites, which indicated 
the important regulation roles of these species. On the other hand, these key species 
also related with food and nutrients during IBD, suggesting dietary regulation may 
not be ignored.  In conclusion, the data presented here help to understand the 
diet-microbial dysbiosis-metabolic dysbiosis pathways that correlate with IBD. The 
network will also provide to develop guiding principles for diet modification and the 
microbiological treatment of IBD.
