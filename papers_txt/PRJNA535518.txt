At a Glance Commentary
Scientific Knowledge on the Subject: 
Antiretroviral therapy has improved survival in persons with HIV (PWH), but chronic 
obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) still causes morbidity and mortality. HIV infection 
may be an independent risk factor for COPD; however, the underlying mechanisms are 
poorly understood. The lung is exposed to the oral microbiome via microaspiration, and 
changes in the oral microbiome may impact lung disease. The gut microbiome can have 
distant effects on other end-organs via impact on the immune response and inflammation, 
by translocation, or through metabolic products. 
What This Study Adds to the Field:
We performed bacterial 16S rRNA sequencing of saliva and stool in the Pittsburgh site of 
the Multicenter AIDS cohort study (MACS) of PWH and HIV-uninfected individuals, with 
and without COPD. We identified oral microbiome differences by HIV and smoking status. 
We discovered a novel association of oral microbiome to lung function measured by post-
bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in one second/forced vital capacity (FEV1/FVC) 
or reduced diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLco). There were no 
significant relationships of stool microbiome to lung function. This study suggests that the 
oral microbiome may play an important role in HIV COPD pathogenesis, possibly by 
stimulating inflammation and promoting lung damage.

Abstract
Rationale: Mechanisms of HIV-associated chronic obstructive pulmonary disease 
(COPD) are poorly understood. The oral microbiome shapes the lung microbiome and 
gut dysbiosis can impact lung diseases; however, relationships of the oral and gut 
microbiome to COPD in HIV have not been explored. 
Objectives: To examine alterations in the oral and gut microbiome associated with 
pulmonary disease in persons with HIV (PWH).
Methods: 75 PWH and 93 HIV-uninfected men from the Multicenter AIDS cohort study 
performed pulmonary function testing. Sequencing of bacterial 16S rRNA in saliva and 
stool was performed. We used non-metric multidimensional scaling, permutational 
multivariate analysis of variance and linear discriminant analysis to analyze communities 
by HIV and lung function. 
Measurements and Main Results: Oral microbiome composition differed by HIV and 
smoking status. Alterations of oral microbial communities were observed in PWH with 
abnormal lung function with increases in relative abundance of Veillonella, Streptococcus 
and Lactobacillus. There were no significant associations between the oral microbiome 
and lung function in HIV-uninfected individuals. No associations with HIV status or lung 
function were seen with the gut microbiome.
Conclusions: Alterations of oral microbiota in PWH were related to impaired pulmonary 
function and to systemic inflammation. These results suggest that the oral microbiome

may serve as a biomarker of lung function in HIV and that its disruption may contribute to 
COPD pathogenesis.

Introduction
Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has improved survival in persons with HIV (PWH), but 
lung disease, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), remains a cause 
of morbidity and mortality (1–4). The underlying mechanisms involved in COPD 
pathogenesis are poorly understood. Lifestyle choices such as increased cigarette 
smoking contributes to disease, but cannot fully explain of the pathogenesis of COPD in 
PWH (2, 5–7). 
The composition and function of resident bacteria can have a profound impact on 
the immune system, inflammation, and metabolite production that may lead to disease or 
exacerbate pre-existing conditions (8). Lung bacterial communities largely resemble 
those in the mouth due to subclinical microaspiration and dispersion along bronchial 
mucosa (9–11), and alterations of oral bacteria have been implicated in COPD in the HIV-
uninfected population (12). The Lung HIV Microbiome Project (LHMP) demonstrated 
alterations in the oral bacterial microbiome in HIV and in smokers (9, 13–17). Given the 
contribution of oral microbes to the lung environment, it is possible that changes in the 
oral microbiome could stimulate inflammation that contributes to lung disease, particularly 
in smokers or PWH. Prior work has shown that healthy individuals with oral taxa 
predominance in bronchoalveolar lavage have increased inflammatory mediators (18), 
but no studies have evaluated the relationship between oral bacterial microbiota and 
COPD in PWH. In addition, oral samples are easily accessible and modification of the 
oral microbiome could indirectly impact lung bacteria, making examination of oral samples 
useful for investigating and potentially modifying the lung microbiome in disease.

Recent evidence shows that gut microbiome and its metabolic products in PWH 
differs from that in HIV-uninfected individuals, and that these alterations correlate with 
immune defects and chronic inflammation (19–21). Dysbiosis in gut microbes may impact 
lung disease via effects on systemic inflammation, microbial translocation, or production 
of various metabolites (22–26), but their relationships have not been evaluated in COPD 
associated with HIV. 
We investigated the relationship between oral and gut microbiome and lung 
function in PWH and HIV-uninfected individuals. We collected saliva and stool from PWH 
and HIV-uninfected individuals with or without COPD and performed next-generation 
sequencing of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene. Some of the results of these studies have 
been previously reported in the form of abstracts (27–30). 
Methods
Study participants
We recruited 168 participants (75 PWH and 93 HIV-uninfected individuals) from 
the Pittsburgh site of the Multicenter AIDS cohort study (MACS). The MACS is a 
longitudinal cohort study of men with or at risk for HIV (http://aidscohortstudy.org). All 
participants without a contraindication to pulmonary function testing were eligible.  
Exclusion criteria included individuals with active respiratory infection, those receiving 
antibiotic treatment in the previous 5 days, and individuals receiving systemic steroid 
therapy in the previous 5 months. We also performed analyses excluding individuals who 
had used antibiotics within six months. As results were not significantly different, we used 
the five-day exclusion to maintain sample size. Demographic, physical and laboratory 
Page 7 of 58
 AJRCCM Articles in Press. Published on 04-November-2019 as 10.1164/rccm.201905-1016OC 
 Copyright © 2019 by the American Thoracic Society 
variables were collected during the regular MACS visit. All participants provided written 
informed consent. The University of Pittsburgh institutional review board approved this 
study, and detailed information regarding recruitment is available in the online supplement.
Pulmonary Function Testing
        Participants performed pre-bronchodilator and post-bronchodilator spirometry using 
360 mcg of albuterol, per American Thoracic Society (ATS) guidelines (31, 32). The third 
National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey equations were used for spirometry 
reference values (31). Diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLco) was 
measured following American Thoracic Society (ATS)/European Respiratory Society 
(ERS) standards (33). DLco was adjusted for hemoglobin and carboxyhemoglobin, and 
reference values for DLco were calculated using Neas equations (34). All pulmonary 
function tests (PFTs) were performed by trained research technicians while acceptable 
quality was confirmed by a central reading center, as per ATS guidelines (32).
Sample collection
Saliva samples were collected at time of pulmonary function testing. Participants 
refrained from eating, drinking, smoking or chewing gum for 30 minutes prior to saliva 
collection. Unstimulated saliva samples were collected in DNA/RNA stabilizing buffer 
(DNA/RNA Shield, ZYMO Research, Irvine, CA). Stool samples were collected at home 
in the stabilizing buffer within a week of pulmonary function testing. All samples were 
aliquoted and frozen at -80C within 30 days of collection.

 AJRCCM Articles in Press. Published on 04-November-2019 as 10.1164/rccm.201905-1016OC

Sample processing and sequencing
        We extracted bacterial DNA directly from saliva and stool samples using DNeasy 
PowerSoil Kits (Qiagen, Germantown, MD). We amplified the bacterial 16S rRNA gene 
V4 subunit using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (35, 36), and we performed 
sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq platform (35). Negative controls were used per batch 
of DNA extraction and PCR to examine possible contamination (Figure S1).
Sequence Processing
        We de-multiplexed reads onboard the sequencer using standard Illumina software 
and performed post-sequencing quality control—including sequence filtering and 
trimming on raw 16S sequences from samples as well as negative controls—using  an 
in-house pipeline developed by the University of Pittsburgh Center for Medicine and the 
Microbiome (CMM), utilizing open source software including dust, fastx toolkit, and 
cutadapt (37–39). We applied an in-house mothur pipeline to generate operational 
taxonomic units and ribosomal database project classified sequences (40, 41).  
Plasma biomarker detection
        Plasma levels of six biomarkers were measured with a Luminex assay (Bio-Rad, 
Hercules, CA), including interlukin-8 (IL-8), granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (GCSF), 
interferon-gamma (INF-γ), monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP1), macrophage 
inflammatory protein-1beta (MIP-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). Plasma 
levels of IL-6, sCD163 and endothelin-1 were measured with ELISA (R&D Systems, 
Page 9 of 58
 AJRCCM Articles in Press. Published on 04-November-2019 as 10.1164/rccm.201905-1016OC 
 Copyright © 2019 by the American Thoracic Society 
Minneapolis, MN). These biomarkers were selected as having previous relationships with 
HIV-associated COPD (42–48).
Statistical Analysis
        We compared participant’s characteristics between PWH and HIV-uninfected 
individuals using t-test, Chi-square, Mood’s median test, and Fisher exact test, as 
appropriate. All participants were stratified by HIV and smoking status (current smokers, 
former smokers and non-smokers). We selected FEV1/FVC<0.70 as the Global Initiative 
for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) definition for airway obstruction and DLco 
<80% predicted as the clinical definition of at least mild impairment. We also used the 
lower limit of normal (LLN) as a cut-off, and compared the results generated by each 
definition. 
We performed ecological analysis of alpha diversity (by Shannon index) with the 
R vegan package (49). We applied the non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) plot 
using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity index to visualize clustering of samples. Univariate 
permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) testing was performed to 
compare the microbiome between different groups with the R vegan package. Multi-
variate PERMANOVA controlling for age, race, smoking status, CD4 count and HIV viral 
load was performed using a custom pipeline based on previous studies indicating that 
these variables could influence microbiome compositions (50–53). Lung function 
parameters were used as continuous variables in PERMANOVA test. Linear discriminant 
analysis effect size (LEfSe) was performed on Galaxy to identify genera that were 
differentially abundant between groups (54). We set alpha values for the factorial Kruskal-
Page 10 of 58
 AJRCCM Articles in Press. Published on 04-November-2019 as 10.1164/rccm.201905-1016OC 
 Copyright © 2019 by the American Thoracic Society 
Wallis at 0.05 and linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score threshold for discriminative 
features at 2.0.
All biomarker levels were normalized. Abundances of genera were transformed 
with the additive log ratio (ALR) transform to mitigate spurious correlation among taxa 
due to the compositional nature of the microbiota profiles. The transformation permits 
each taxonomic abundance to be treated as independent (from each other) and normally 
distributed (55). Multiple linear regression was used to examine associations of 
biomarkers to ALR of signature genera and lung function parameters, correcting for age, 
race and HIV viral load.
Results
Subjects
Seventy-five PWH and 93 HIV-uninfected participants were included. PWH and 
HIV-uninfected individuals were similar in age, race, ethnicity, and smoking status (Table 
1). One hundred and sixty-two participants (96%) had acceptable spirometry per 
ATS/ERS criteria, and 156 (93%) participants had acceptable DLco testing. Participants 
who had acceptable PFTs were not significantly different from those who did not in age, 
race, ethnicity, and smoking status.
Oral microbiome analyses
One hundred and sixty saliva samples were available for analysis. The most 
dominant genera detected within the oral microbiome were Prevotella, Veillonella, 
Neisseria, Pasteurellaceae (family level, genus not classified), Fusobacterium, Rothia 
Page 11 of 58

and Streptococcus, which accounted for 80% of the overall abundance of bacteria in the 
mouth.
Comparison between PWH and HIV-uninfected individuals
        Alpha diversity in saliva from PWH was significantly reduced compared to HIV-
uninfected individuals (t-test, p=0.00083; Figure 1A). Beta diversity was also different 
between groups in both univariate (PERMANOVA p=0.01, R2=0.016) and multivariate 
analyses controlling for age, race and smoking status (PERMANOVA p=0.0006, 
R2=0.028; Figure 1B). Among PWH, neither alpha nor beta diversity differed by ART, CD4 
count, or HIV viral load (data not shown). We visualized the relative abundance of 
dominant taxa at the genus level in the oral microbiome of individuals by HIV status 
(Figure 1C). Abundance of Veillonella, Rothia, and Streptococcus were significantly 
increased in the oral microbiome of PWH, while abundance of Neisseria was significantly 
decreased (Figure 1D). 
Comparison by smoking status
We then compared alpha diversity of the oral microbiome by smoking status. 
Among PWH, those who were actively smoking had reduced alpha diversity in saliva 
compared to non-smokers (t-test, p=0.013, Figure 2A). In HIV-uninfected individuals, oral 
alpha-diversity of current smokers was higher than that of former smokers, although it 
was not significant (t-test, p=0.06; Figure 2A). In both univariate analysis and multi-variate 
analyses controlling for age, race, CD4 count and HIV RNA viral load, there was a strong 
difference in overall oral microbiome community structure between current smokers, 
former smokers and non-smokers among PWH (PERMANOVA p=0.017, R2=0.034; and

PERMANOVA p=0.0004, R2=0.12 using univariate and multi-variate analyses, 
respectively; Figure 2B). In HIV-uninfected individuals, we observed a similar relationship 
between the oral microbiome and smoking status using both models (PERMANOVA 
p=0.022, R2=0.0323; and PERMANOVA p=0.011, R2=0.052 using univariate and multi-
variate analyses, respectively; Figure 2C). Relative abundance of Top 15 dominant taxa 
pointed toward shared and unique taxa of oral microbiome (Figure 2D) in each group. 
Oral microbes such as Veillonella, Streptococcus and Lactobacillus were more abundant 
in PWH who were current smokers compared to non-smokers, while Neisseria, Prevotella, 
Pasteurellaceae (family level, genus not classified) and Fusobacterium were relatively 
decreased. HIV-uninfected current smokers had less Neisseria in the oral microbiome 
(Figure 2E).
Comparison by lung function
We evaluated the relationship between the oral microbiome and lung function in 
PWH and HIV-uninfected individuals. There was no significant difference in alpha 
diversity of the oral microbiome between groups with or without reduced post 
bronchodilator FEV1/FVC regardless of HIV status (t-test, not significant; Figure 3A). Beta 
diversity was different among PWH with airway obstruction as measured by reduced post-
bronchodilator FEV1/FVC compared to those without (in univariate analysis, 
PERMANOVA p=0.0281, R2=0.033; in multivariate analysis, PERMANOVA p=0.036, 
R2=0.037; Figure 3B). In HIV-uninfected individuals, no differences were seen by 
FEV1/FVC using multivariate analysis controlling for age, race, smoking status (Figure

Among PWH, those with impaired DLco had significantly lower oral alpha diversity 
(t-test, p=0.033, Figure 3D). Beta diversity also differed in these individuals when

We visualized the relative abundance of dominant taxa (Top 15) at the genus level

Associations of inflammatory biomarkers, PFTs and microbiome in PWH
        In PWH, plasma TNF-α, endothelin-1 and MIP-1β were negatively correlated with 
lung function parameters (Figure 4). We then examined the associations of these three 
plasma biomarkers to key genera. Plasma TNF-α and MIP-1β were increased in PWH 
with more abundant oral Streptococcus and Lactobacillus. Plasma endothelin-1 levels 
were also positively correlated with oral Streptococcus, Veillonella and Lactobacillus 
abundance (Figure 5).

Discussion
We investigated oral bacterial microbiota alterations in PWH and COPD. We 
observed oral microbiome differences by HIV and smoking status. Importantly, we found 
significant differences in the oral bacterial community that were associated with lung 
function abnormalities in PWH, and correlation of plasma inflammatory biomarkers with 
lung dysfunction and oral taxonomic signature. These findings suggest that changes in 
the oral microbiome may be involved in the pathogenesis of lung disease in PWH, 
possibly by stimulating inflammation; and that the oral microbiome may serve as an easily 
accessible marker of lung dysfunction.
As in previous work, the oral microbiome of PWH differed from that of HIV-
uninfected individuals (13, 15, 51). PWH had decreased oral microbiome alpha diversity, 
and there were differences in beta diversity when compared to HIV-uninfected controls. 
The LHMP compared the oral and lung microbiome of PWH to HIV-uninfected individuals 
and reported increased abundance of oral Streptococcus, Actinomyces and Rothia in 
PWH (14). Our results were similar in identifying increases in oral Veillonella, Rothia, 
Streptococcus and decreased Neisseria in PWH. Prior work also reported decreased oral 
microbial alpha diversity in PWH with low CD4 counts after 24 weeks of ART (52). We 
did not find similar differences, possibly because most of PWH in our cohort were on long-
term ART and had normal CD4 cell counts. Mechanisms explaining compositional 
differences in the oral microbiome between PWH and HIV-uninfected are not known, but 
could reflect the aberrant immune activation seen in PWH (56), leading to an altered host 
Page 15 of 58
 AJRCCM Articles in Press. Published on 04-November-2019 as 10.1164/rccm.201905-1016OC 
 Copyright © 2019 by the American Thoracic Society 
response to oral bacteria. Other factors such as dental health, oral hygiene, ART, or diet 
could also have an impact.
Smoking was associated with alterations in oral microbiome communities as well. 
Current smokers had a reduced oral microbiome alpha diversity compared to non-
smokers only among PWH. And we observed overall compositional alterations in the oral 
microbiome when comparing current smokers to former smokers and non-smokers in 
both PWH and HIV-uninfected individuals. It has been proposed that smoking promotes 
bacterial colonization of the upper airways via facilitating epithelial binding and impairing 
mucociliary clearance in healthy participants (57, 58). Our study suggested that the 
effects of smoking may be even more pronounced in PWH.
Changes in the oral microbiome may be important in lung function. In the HIV-
uninfected population, oral health and oral bacteria have been postulated to play a role in 
COPD (12, 59). Prior work has found alterations in oral health associated with COPD 
symptoms and severity, presumably due to changes in oral bacteria (60), although one 
study demonstrated no overall structural differences in periodontal plaque microbiome 
between individuals with and without COPD (61). Studies of periodontal interventions to 
improve COPD have shown some improvement in lung function, but have not directly 
assessed changes in the oral microbiome (62). 
We discovered significant differences in the oral microbiome related to pulmonary 
function in PWH. Among PWH, alterations in the oral microbiome beta diversity were 
strongly associated with both FEV1/FVC and DLco, and richness of the oral microbiome 
was reduced in PWH who had impaired DLco. We identified increased Veillonella, 
Page 16 of 58
 AJRCCM Articles in Press. Published on 04-November-2019 as 10.1164/rccm.201905-1016OC 
 Copyright © 2019 by the American Thoracic Society 
Streptococcus and Lactobacillus in PWH with abnormal lung function. Certain species of 
Veillonella and Streptococcus, such as Veillonella parvula, have the ability to induce the 
production pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-8, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α (63, 64). A 
strong correlation has been established between saliva Lactobacillus counts and poor 
dental health, with higher caries correlating with higher Lactobacillus counts (65, 66). 
Furthermore, a study indicated that some Lactobacillus strains could facilitate 
inflammatory responses of macrophages by inducing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as 
IL-8, TNF-α, IL-12p70, and IL-6 (67), although certain species and strains of Lactobacillus, 
such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus, can have an anti-inflammatory effect (68, 69). 
Metagenomic sequencing of microbial community with a resolution at strain’s level is 
needed in the future. In our study, we observed increases of plasma TNF-α, MIP-1β and 
endothelin-1 level associated with decreased lung function; and the level of these COPD-
associated pro-inflammatory cytokines were positively correlated with the abundance of 
key bacteria associated with lung dysfunction - Veillonella, Streptococcus and 
Lactobacillus, suggesting that the oral microbiome in PHW may contribute to lung 
dysfunction possibly by simulating inflammatory responses. When activated, 
macrophages express inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α and MIP-1β, which recruit 
and activate lymphocytes, leading to pulmonary damage (70, 71). Endothelin-1, a 
vasoconstrictor and a marker of vascular injury, is also released from activated 
macrophages. Endothelin-1 has a role in pathogenesis of pulmonary vasculopathy which 
may contribute to impaired diffusing capacity, particularly in PWH (42, 72). There may be 
different phenotypes of lung function abnormalities such as airway obstruction or impaired 
diffusing capacity in HIV COPD. DLco in general can be altered by emphysema, 
Page 17 of 58
 AJRCCM Articles in Press. Published on 04-November-2019 as 10.1164/rccm.201905-1016OC 
 Copyright © 2019 by the American Thoracic Society 
pulmonary vascular disease, and other processes. In HIV, we commonly see DLco 
impairment, even in never smokers, suggesting that there may be different mechanisms 
that contribute to its development (1, 47, 73). The oral microbiome and associated 
inflammation may act on different pathways to contribute to FEV1/FVC or DLco reduction. 
However, it should be noted that only three of nine detected biomarkers were significantly 
correlated with pulmonary function and key bacteria and that these associations may 
have been influenced by our sample size, suggesting that microbiome may impact 
pulmonary function through other pathways, for example, adaptive immunity. Overall, 
alterations in the oral microbiome may be at least partially responsible for changes seen 
in the lung function in PWH although it is not possible to prove causation in a cross-
sectional study. Altered oral microbiota may impact local and systemic immunity and 
inflammation that may further contribute to lung dysfunction (74, 75). In addition, oral 
microbes enter the lung via subclinical micro-aspiration and dispersion along bronchial 
mucosa to directly shape the lung microbiome community and indirectly influence the 
growth of lung bacteria. These oral microbes and their metabolic products could stimulate 
inflammation, recruiting white blood cells to the lung with release of inﬂammatory 
cytokines and chemokines and proteases (47) (Figure 6). 
Interestingly, these associations seemed to be unique to PWH. Among HIV-
uninfected individuals, we noted certain oral bacteria were associated with lung 
dysfunction in the HIV-uninfected group, but in analyses controlling for smoking and age, 
there was no overall difference between those with and without lung dysfunction. The 
interaction of HIV and lung function had a significant effect on beta-diversity of the oral 
microbiome. Possible explanations for these differences could include increased aberrant 
Page 18 of 58
 AJRCCM Articles in Press. Published on 04-November-2019 as 10.1164/rccm.201905-1016OC 
 Copyright © 2019 by the American Thoracic Society 
immune activation in response to oral microbes in PWH or structural differences in the 
oral cavity or lung environment that contribute to alterations in microbial communities or 
microbial function. Micro-aspiration may have occurred more frequently in the HIV-
infected individuals that could result in a greater bacterial exposure in the lung, further 
worsen the chronic inflammation responses. 
In addition to providing information on pathogenesis of oral microbes in HIV and 
COPD, our results suggest the utility of oral sampling as a marker of disease. Lung 
microbiome studies have been challenging given the difficulty and expense associated 
with bronchoscopy (76). Low biomass of bacteria in the lung makes sequencing difficult 
and increases risk of environmental contamination (77). Saliva is an easily collected 
sample with a higher biomass. Previous studies have shown potential overlap between 
oral and lung microbiome (9). Thus, the potential to use saliva as a biomarker of lung 
environment and lung function in PWH could greatly enhance the ability to investigate 
lung diseases and facilitate tracking changes over time in health and disease. 
Manipulation of the oral microbiome could be a novel therapeutic modality for altering 
lung function as prior work in the HIV-uninfected population suggests that dental 
interventions can improve respiratory symptoms (62).
There are several limitations to our study. First, this MACS cohort may not reflect 
the current HIV/AIDS epidemic in the US because it only included male participants and 
the majority were Caucasian, and most PWH were on appropriate ART. In addition, the 
study was cross-sectional and cannot determine relationships of the oral microbiome to 
development or progression of COPD in PWH. Second, we had limited data on oral health 
Page 19 of 58
 AJRCCM Articles in Press. Published on 04-November-2019 as 10.1164/rccm.201905-1016OC 
 Copyright © 2019 by the American Thoracic Society 
that was collected as part of the MACS study visit and could not investigate relationship 
of the oral microbiome to oral health. Additionally, the effect size (R^2) of examined 
variables to the microbiome was relatively small, indicating there might be other factors 
that contributed to the microbiome alterations that were not included in our study. We also 
had very few participants with severe COPD, which could limit differences detected in the 
microbiome. There may have been a smaller effect in the HIV-uninfected group that we 
had insufficient power to detect. Third, we did not have paired oral and lung microbiome 
samples. Although the similarity of lung and oral samples has previously been shown, 
there is not 100% overlap and the fact that we found alterations in the oral microbiome 
associated with HIV, smoking, and lung function that have not been reported in the lung 
suggest that there are important differences. These differences may occur even if the oral 
microbiome is largely responsible for populating the lung microbiome as bronchoalveolar 
lavage dilutes bacterial communities which may render the bacteria below the limit of 
detection, and aspiration of oral microbes is likely intermittent and could be missed during 
a single bronchoscopy. Finally, we performed 16S sequencing, and it is possible that 
whole genome sequencing or metatranscriptomics might have uncovered additional 
differences in these populations.
In summary, we discovered a relationship of overall oral microbial community 
structure to FEV1/FVC and DLco in PWH, as well as reduced richness of the oral 
microbiome in PWH with impaired DLco. Among PWH, alterations in the oral microbiome 
beta diversity were strongly associated with both airway obstruction and diffusion 
impairment, and richness of the oral microbiome was reduced in PWH who had impaired 
diffusing capacity. Veillonella, Streptococcus and Lactobacillus were increased in PWH 
Page 20 of 58
 AJRCCM Articles in Press. Published on 04-November-2019 as 10.1164/rccm.201905-1016OC 
 Copyright © 2019 by the American Thoracic Society 
who had abnormal lung function; suggesting that the oral microbiome may be playing a 
role in HIV COPD pathogenesis. Associations of microbes with lung function and 
peripheral TNF-α, MIP-1β, endothelin-1 levels suggest a potential role in stimulating 
inflammation and promoting lung damage. Longitudinal evaluation of the oral microbiome 
might also serve as a non-invasive marker for risk of lung function impairment. 
Therapeutic manipulation of the oral microbiome to impact lung function is also an 
appealing, though as yet untested, novel treatment modality.

Expended methods
Study participants
We recruited 168 male participants from the Pittsburgh site of the Multicenter AIDS 
cohort study (MACS) during a regular MACS visit. Seventy-five participants were HIV-
infected, while 93 were HIV-uninfected. All participants without a contraindication to 
pulmonary function testing were eligible. Exclusion criteria included individuals 
experiencing acute respiratory infection at the time of sampling, those with usage of 
antibiotics in the past 5 days, and usage of steroid in the previous 5 months. Demographic 
and clinical data were collected by standardized subject interview and medical record 
review. All participants provided written informed consent. The institutional review board 
of the University of Pittsburgh approved this study. 
DNA extraction
Bacterial DNA was directly extracted from 300 μl aliquot of the saliva samples and 
300 μl aliquot of the stool samples using DNeasy PowerSoil Kit (QIAGEN, Germantown, 
MD) following manufacturer’s instructions. Extracted DNA was eluted in 100 μl of Tris-
HCl, pH 8.5 and stored at 4 C. We used DNA-free sterile water per each batch of DNA 
extractions as negative control to examine possible contamination.
Page 48 of 58
 AJRCCM Articles in Press. Published on 04-November-2019 as 10.1164/rccm.201905-1016OC 
 Copyright © 2019 by the American Thoracic Society 
PCR amplification
Extracted gDNA was PCR amplified using the barcoded primers of Caporaso (1) 
targeting bacterial 16S rRNA V4 region. We used the Q5 HS High-Fidelity polymerase 
(New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) and 4 μl of DNA template of each sample in a total 
reaction volume of 25 μl. We utilized DNA-free sterile water and ZymoBIOMICS Microbial 
Community DNA Standard (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA) as negative and positive control, 
respectively. PCR products were purified with the AMPure XP beads (Beckman, Brea, 
CA) at a 0.8:1 ratio (beads:DNA) and then quantitated on Qubit fluorimeter using the 
Broad Range Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA). 
Sequencing library preparation
We pooled samples on ice by combining 40ng of each purified amplicon. For 
negative controls and poorly performing samples with low DNA concentration, we used 
20 μl of each sample. Three purifications of the combined sample pool were performed 
with: 1) MinElute PCR purification Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) , 2) a left-side:right-
side (0.80:0.61) double-spri AMPure XP beads purification (Beckman, Brea, CA), 3) 
Purelink PCR purification Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA). And we quantitated the 
pool in triplicate on the Qubit fluorimeter with High Sensitivity kit (Life Technologies, 
Carlsbad, CA). We prepared the sequencing pool as per Illumina’s recommendations. 
Sequencing pools were run at a final concentration of 8 pM and 15% PhiX control.

 AJRCCM Articles in Press. Published on 04-November-2019 as 10.1164/rccm.201905-1016OC 
 Copyright © 2019 by the American Thoracic Society 
Sequence processing
Reads were demultiplexed into individual sample fastq files onboard the sequencer 
using the standard Illumina software. Post-sequencing quality control (QC) including low 
complexity filtering, QV trimming, adapter trimming, primer trimming on raw 16S 
sequences from samples as well as negative controls was performed using an in-house 
pipeline of the University of Pittsburgh Center for Medicine and the Microbiome (CMM). 
Sequences with greater than 80% low complexity regions, or with QV lower than 33, or 
shorter than 125bp were excluded from further processing. This QC pipeline was 
dependent on standard open source software, including dust, fastx toolkit, and cutadapt 
(2–4). 
With reads passing filtering and trimming steps, we applied an in-house pipeline 
dependent on mothur for operational taxonomic unit (OTU) generation and the taxonomic 
assignment (5, 6). A 97% similarity threshold was used for OTU clustering. Each sample 
generated an average of 3475 16S reads. The taxa table was then filtered for samples 
that generated fewer than 600 reads. One negative extraction control had 375 reads. To 
avoid possible contamination, samples from this batch were removed from further 
analyses. After the sequences processing, we compared the total reads number 
(p=0.0013) and the beta diversity (Permanova, p=0.001) of negative controls to samples, 
and there was significant difference (Figure S1). 
        Our in-house pipeline for sequence processing and data analyses was available at 
Github, https://github.com/MedicineAndTheMicrobiome/AnalysisTools.

Results
Oral microbiome analyses
We also examined the effect of HIV/lung function interaction on beta-diversity. The 
interaction of HIV and DLco had a significant effect on beta-diversity of the oral 
microbiome (p=0.046). The interaction of HIV and FEV1/FVC had a nearly significant 
effect on beta-diversity of the oral microbiome (p=0.072). The interaction of HIV and FEV1 
had no significant effect on beta-diversity of the oral microbiome (p=0.10).
Gut microbiome analyses
In 94 participants with available stool samples, the gut microbial communities were 
dominated 
by 
Prevotella, 
Bacteroides, 
Faecalibacterium, 
Ruminococcaceae, 
Lachnospiraceae, Phascolarctobacterium and Alloprevotella, which accounted for 66% 
of the overall abundance of bacteria in stool. There were no differences in alpha diversity 
by HIV status (Figure S3A) and beta diversity only differed in univariate analysis 
(PERMANOVA, p=0.009, R2=0.02; Figure S3B). Overall, there was no significant 
difference in the gut microbial communities among current smokers, former smokers and 
non-smokers in PWH or HIV-uninfected individuals; however, alpha diversity of the gut 
microbiome in HIV-uninfected current smokers was higher than that in HIV-uninfected 
former smokers (t-test, p=0.03) and non-smokers (t-test, p=0.01) (Figure S3C). We did 
not identify a relationship of the gut microbiome to lung function parameters in either PWH 
or HIV-uninfected individuals (Figure S4).

 AJRCCM Articles in Press. Published on 04-November-2019 as 10.1164/rccm.201905-1016OC 
 Copyright © 2019 by the American Thoracic Society 
The interaction of HIV and lung function had no effect on gut microbiome (data not 
shown). Similar results were observed when using LLN as cut-offs for airway obstruction. 
Discussion
In contrast to oral microbiome, the gut microbiome did not differ significantly by 
HIV status, smoking status or lung function. Beta diversity of PWH was different from that 
of HIV-uninfected individuals only in univariate analysis, but this difference was lost when 
controlling for clinical factors such as race, age and smoking. Our results were 
inconsistent with previous work that observed gut microbiome alterations during HIV 
infection (7). This discrepancy and lack of associations with smoking or lung function 
parameters may be due to several factors: 1) we collected fewer stool samples than saliva 
samples due to participant preference; 2) there were fewer individuals with abnormal lung 
function, and we might not have sufficient power to detect an effect; 3) most of our 
participants were on appropriate ART; 4) differences in diet could have masked changes 
in the gut microbiome by HIV status, smoking status or lung function.
