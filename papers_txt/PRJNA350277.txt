ORIGINALITY-SIGNIFICANCE STATEMENT 
In this manuscript, we highlight the importance of inter-individual variability in the fermentation 
and colonization of wheat bran by the fecal microbiota of ten individuals in vitro. We show that 
the bacteria attached to wheat bran are composed of a specific subset of gut bacteria, comprising 
both primary degraders and organisms relying on cross-feeding to obtain their energy. Both 
functional groups were present in all donors, despite the large inter-individual differences. While 
the experimental approach to study the tested compounds is well-known, the strength of our 
study lies in the in-depth evaluation and interpretation and its relationship with inter-individual 
variability. Additionally, our findings are original because research so far has mainly focused on 
the benefits derived from fermentation of soluble dietary fiber. However, insoluble, partly non-
fermentable fibers might represent an interesting additional habitat for gut microbes to adhere to, 
grow or metabolically interact on. A further characterization of the microbiota associated with 
dietary particles in the gut will contribute to a better understanding of the colonization process of 
the human gut by micro-organisms, leading to a more comprehensive view of the complex 
microbiota-host symbiosis, eventually offering new possibilities for steering the microbial 
community in the gut. Wheat bran is a promising candidate in this regard. Many health benefits 
of wheat bran were already suggested, however, only two claims, one related to the fecal bulking 
properties and the other related to reduction of intestinal transit time, are approved by the 
European Food Safety Authority (EFSA). A detailed characterization of wheat bran, particularly 
the microbial community attached to the wheat bran residue, can aid in further substantiating the 
health promoting effects of wheat bran.

SUMMARY 
Gut microbiota research reveals a vital role for the luminal and mucosal gut microbiota in human 
health. Fewer studies, however, have characterized the microbiome associated with undigested, 
insoluble dietary particles in the gut. These particles can act as a food source for bacteria and 
offer a physical platform to which they can attach. In this study, the microbiome colonizing 
wheat bran particles was analyzed. In a batch experiment, wheat bran particles were separately 
incubated with the fecal microbiota derived from ten donors and washed after 48h to remove 
loosely attached bacteria. The response of the luminal community to wheat bran and inulin, 
acting as a well-characterized control, was largely donor-dependent, both functionally, and with 
respect to the microbiome composition. Depending on the donor, wheat bran and inulin 
fermentation yielded proportionally higher propionate or butyrate production. Clostridium cluster 
XIVa and, depending on the donor, Prevotella, Roseburia, Megamonas, Bifidobacterium and 
Bacteroides species were enriched on the wheat bran particles. These genera include species with 
the documented ability to serve as primary degraders of wheat bran components and other 
species depending on cross-feeding to obtain their energy. Both functional groups were present 
in all donors, despite the large inter-individual differences.

INTRODUCTION 
The gut ecosystem harbors a diverse and dense bacterial community, living in symbiosis with the 
human host, feeding on food residues escaping digestion in the small intestine and on host 
secretions (Bäckhed et al., 2005). The gut microbiota is extensively studied given its role in 
health and disease, with special attention to the relation between diet and health (Flint et al., 
2012a). Most research is conducted using fecal samples, which are considered to be 
representative of the luminal contents of the human large intestine (Flint et al., 2012a; den Besten 
et al., 2013). The mucosal environment receives increasing attention as a micro-environment 
where specific species can attach and thrive (Nava et al., 2011; Belzer and de Vos, 2012; Van 
den Abbeele et al., 2013b). Undigested food particles, in contrast, are poorly researched, 
although these could present an interesting niche for micro-organisms (Macfarlane et al., 1997). 
Bäckhed et al. (2005) suggested that nutrient platforms such as undigested food particles, shed 
epithelial cells and mucus fragments can act as a ‘dinner table’, where bacteria can attach to 
more efficiently extract oligo- and monosaccharides that can be used by these primary degraders 
or distributed to other members of the community. The idea of a specific microbiota, consisting 
of primary degraders and their cross-feeding partners, colonizing food particles is shared by 
many authors (Flint et al., 2008; Walker et al., 2008; Macfarlane et al., 2011; Flint et al., 2012a; 
White et al., 2014). Some support for this theory was provided in a study comparing the 
microbial community associated with the particulate matter in the fecal samples of seven donors 
with the liquid fraction using clone libraries and Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization (FISH) 
(Walker et al., 2008). Walker et al. (2008) demonstrated a reduced association of Bacteroidetes 
with food particles (18.5% compared with 28.5% luminally) and an increased colonization by 
Firmicutes (76.8% compared with 65.8% luminally). The Firmicutes Clostridium cluster IV

represented by relatives of Ruminococcus flavefaciens, R. bromii and R. albus and cluster XIVa 
represented by Roseburia and Eubacterium rectale mainly accounted for this specific particle 
association. Two other studies by Macfarlane et al. (1997,2006), conversely, failed to detect 
differences in microbial community composition based on culturing isolates and FISH. These 
studies, however, did show that adherent and non-adherent bacterial populations are not only 
characterized by a distinct physical niche but also by distinct functional niches (Macfarlane et al., 
1997; Macfarlane and Macfarlane, 2006). The adherent community fermented arabinogalactan at 
a higher rate compared to the non-adherent population which was more efficiently fermenting 
oligosaccharides. Furthermore, differences in fermentation products were noted with 
proportionally more acetate being produced by the adherent community versus a relative increase 
in butyrate production by the non-adherent species (Macfarlane and Macfarlane, 2006). 
Considering the unaffected community composition, this indicates a very flexible microbial 
community able to tweak the polysaccharide utilization pathways upon substrate attachment 
(Sonnenburg et al., 2004). However, taking into account the inconsistency with the results of 
Walker et al. (2008), the differences in substrate preference and metabolic output profiles could 
also imply underlying differences in composition of the attached microbial community 
responsible for the substrate breakdown and fermentation.  
In order to verify the existence of a food particle specific microbial community, wheat bran is 
used in this study as a defined model substrate, since the heterogeneous nature of particulate 
matter in fecal samples might complicate the interpretation. It is already shown in the context of 
the rumen ecosystem, where the presence of a plant biomass adherent microbial community is 
long-recognized (Shinkai and Kobayashi, 2007), that the type of biomass largely determines the 
attached community (Larue et al., 2005).

The use of wheat bran as a model substrate can be justified by the position of wheat as a staple 
food in the Western diet. Wheat processing generates a highly abundant wheat bran side stream 
which is increasingly being used in whole grain food products to which several physiological 
benefits are attributed (Marlett et al., 2002; Slavin, 2003; Stevenson et al., 2012; FAO, 2015). 
Moreover, wheat bran consists of a unique combination of fermentable and non-fermentable 
fibers, entangled in a porous insoluble network that could serve as an ideal ‘dinner table’ for 
micro-organisms. Fermentable compounds such as starch, proteins and less substituted 
arabinoxylans constitute the ‘dinner’ for the microbes. Cellulose and highly branched 
arabinoxylans are less- or even non-fermentable fibers that could act as a physical contact 
surface or ‘table’ for gut microbes to adhere, grow or metabolically interact on.  
In this study, the microbial community attached to the non-fermentable bran residue after 48h 
was assessed in vitro in a batch experiment and compared with the luminal microbial community 
using next generation amplicon sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene. In addition, the effect of 
wheat bran on the fermentation activity of the gut microbial community was compared with a 
carbohydrate-low control medium and inulin acting as a reference prebiotic. The microbial 
community derived from ten subjects was used, taking into account the large inter-individual 
variability in the human microbiome.

RESULTS 
The wheat bran residue harbors a specific wheat bran associated microbiome 
In the introduction, it was hypothesized that wheat bran particles, consisting of a unique 
combination of insoluble fermentable and non-fermentable fibers, might represent an interesting 
niche for gut micro-organisms to colonize and might, therefore, harbor a specific microbial 
community. This hypothesis was addressed by separately incubating wheat bran with the fecal 
inoculum derived from ten different human donors for 48h. The chemical composition of the 
wheat bran residue was analyzed and starch is efficiently removed (reduced from 14.7% to 1.2% 
DW) from the substrate upon in vitro digestion and subsequent fermentation by fecal bacteria. 
The remaining bran particles mainly consisted of dietary fiber, comprising cellulose (16.5% DW) 
and arabinoxylans (31.6% DW) with increasing degree of substitution (A/X ratio of 1.1 
compared to before A/X 0.6). The microbiota attached to the wheat bran residue after a washing 
procedure to remove loosely attached bacteria, further termed bran-attached microbiota was 
compared to the microbial community in the liquid phase, further termed luminal microbiota. A 
principle Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) of the microbiota composition at species level, as obtained 
with next generation 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, revealed a distinct luminal and bran-
attached microbial community, further confirmed by Unweighted Pair-Grouped Method using 
arithmetic Averages (UPGMA) clustering and Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance 
(PERMANOVA) (p=0.001) (Supplementary data, Figure S1-S2).

DISCUSSION 
Separate incubation of wheat bran with the fecal microbiota from ten different individuals 
revealed that the insoluble wheat bran residue, mainly consisting of cellulose and complex 
arabinoxylans with a degree of arabinose substitution of 1.1, hosts a distinct microbial 
community, compared to the luminal environment. The main genus level shifts included the 
enrichment of some Firmicutes members such as Clostridium cluster XIVa and Megamonas next 
to Prevotella on the bran, whereas Bacteroides, Escherichia/Shigella, Faecalibacterium, 
Flavonifractor, Oscillibacter and Sutterella made up a smaller part of the community colonizing 
the bran. While genus level shifts were characterized by donor variability, the enrichment of 
Clostridium cluster XIVa on the bran was consistent in all donors, representing 12% up to 47% 
of the bran-attached microbial community. These findings are in agreement with previous reports 
of preferential colonization of wheat bran by members from Clostridium cluster XIVa by fecal 
samples from four donors (Leitch et al., 2007). The main Clostridium cluster XIVa species that 
were enriched on the bran were Eubacterium rectale, Clostridium xylanolyticum and OTU 9 with 
sequence similarity to Hungatella hathewayi. In some donors Ruminococcus and Roseburia 
species were also successfully colonizing the bran. The species level shifts found in our study are 
consistent with the findings of Leitch et al. (2007), where the Clostridium cluster XIVa 
comprised Eubacterium rectale, Roseburia faecis and some sequences distantly related to 
Clostridium hathewayi. The specific bran colonization by members from Clostridium cluster 
XIVa was concomitant with a proportionally reduced abundance of the genus Bacteroides on 
bran (0.8-27%) as opposed to the lumen (21-63%) for eight out of ten donors. This confirms 
previous observations from Leitch et al. (2007). It has been put forward that the Bacteroides 
genus, whilst characterized by a typical ‘glycobiome’ with genes encoding glycosyl hydrolases

(GH) and polysaccharide lyases, may be more specialized in the breakdown of soluble 
carbohydrates, whereas insoluble carbohydrates may be more efficiently fermented by 
Firmicutes (Salyers et al., 1981; Mirande et al., 2010; Van Wey et al., 2011). However, this 
hypothesis is based on a study with only one representative of both phyla (Mirande et al., 2010) 
and little is known regarding the primary degradation of insoluble complex substrates, such as 
wheat bran, by the human gut microbiota (Flint et al., 2008; Mirande et al., 2010). In the rumen 
ecosystem fibrolytic activity can be linked to attachment to plant biomass through a cellulosome 
system (Shinkai and Kobayashi, 2007; White et al., 2014). At present Ruminococcus 
champanellensis is the sole identified cellulolytic gut species displaying a cellulosome enzyme 
system, mediating attachment to insoluble cellulose (Morais et al., 2016). In our study, 
Ruminococcus species were present at a relative abundance of approximately 1% in the fecal 
sample of all donors, but were only slightly enriched on the bran for a few donors, suggesting 
that i) the expected solubilization activity of Ruminococcus species is not proportional to their 
relative abundance in the community or ii) other yet unidentified species and/or mechanisms play 
a role in wheat bran solubilization. The latter is more likely as wheat bran degradation by pure 
cultures of amongst others Eubacterium rectale, Roseburia intestinalis and Roseburia faecis is 
shown by Duncan et al. (2016).  
It is not established to what extent the solubilized components remain associated with the 
insoluble substrate. Therefore, the capacity to breakdown and utilize solubilized wheat bran 
constituents, such as arabinoxylan backbones, might contribute to the colonization of wheat bran. 
Enzyme mediated attachment to soluble substrates is well documented. Xylan Utilization 
Systems (Xus systems) are membrane associated enzyme complexes involved in extracellular

binding of soluble xylan and transport of xylan fragments, discovered in xylanolytic 
Bacteroidetes (Dodd et al., 2011; McNulty et al., 2013; Rogowski et al., 2016). 
Our study revealed the enrichment of Bacteroides cellulosilyticus and Bacteroides ovatus on the 
bran. Bacteroides cellulosilyticus possesses the highest amount of genes encoding carbohydrate 
active enzymes with xylans being the preferred substrate (McNulty et al., 2013; White et al., 
2014). Activity against insoluble cellulose is shown (Flint et al., 2008). Bacteroides ovatus is one 
of the most studied xylanolytic bacteria, shown to possess Xylan utilization systems (Xus) and 
able to grow on wheat bran arabinoxylans (White et al., 2014; Rogowski et al., 2016). Yet, these 
two species were not consistently enriched in the bran-attached community of all donors. This 
may be the result of competition between species sharing the same xylan utilization capacities, as 
this is not solely confined to the Bacteroides genus (Dodd et al., 2010). The Prevotella genus for 
instance contains several species that are efficient xylan degraders (Dodd et al., 2011). This is 
supported by our finding of a significant enrichment of Prevotella copri in donors 9 (from 1% to 
51.8%) and 10 (from 1.65% to 30.5%). Using the Polysaccharide-Utilization Loci DataBase 
(PULDB), 7 PULs containing genes encoding GH involved in arabinoxylan breakdown were 
found (Terrapon et al., 2015). Other glycan degrading specialists can be found in the 
Bifidobacterium genus (van den Broek et al., 2008). Our study revealed an enrichment of 
Bifidobacterium OTU 16, in donors 1,3,4,8 and 9. Yet, this is probably the result of cross-feeding 
on mono- or oligosaccharides as most bifidobacterial glycan degrading enzymes are intracellular 
and growth is more efficient on xylo-oligosaccharides (XOS) compared to arabinoxylans 
(Broekaert et al., 2011; Flint et al., 2012b; Rogowski et al., 2016).  
Our findings also revealed some representatives of the Firmicutes with more limited 
carbohydrate utilization capacities, such as Megamonas funiformis, to be enriched on the bran.

Megamonas funiformis can benefit from wheat bran through cross-feeding since phenotypic 
characterization shows acid production on arabinose and xylose and a BioCyc search indicates 
the absence of xylan degrading pathways and enzymes (Sakon et al., 2008). This ability to 
indirectly gain energy from the wheat bran substrate does not involve attachment to it, indicating 
that other mechanisms facilitate adhesion (McNulty et al., 2013; White et al., 2014). Our finding 
of Eubacterium rectale and Roseburia spp., preferentially colonizing the wheat bran residue 
while Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Anaerostipes spp. prefer the lumen, parallel those of the 
mucosal microbiota and is consistent with their flagellar presence, respectively absence 
suggesting motility might play a role (Nava et al., 2011; Flint et al., 2012a,; Van den Abbeele et 
al., 2013b). Additionally, Eubacterium rectale possesses a type IV pilus system, encoded by a 
tad (tight adherent) operon (Xu et al., 2012). The role of pili in mucus adhesion is demonstrated 
in lactobacilli species (Roos and Jonsson, 2002; Kankainen et al., 2009; Johansson et al., 2011; 
Lebeer et al., 2012), and in bifidobacterial species the expression of putative pilus-gene clusters 
is inducible by different types of glycans, depending on the species, suggesting interaction of pili 
with glycans (Foroni et al., 2011). In the rumen ecosystem the role of pili in the attachment of 
Ruminococcus albus to cellulose is established (Rakotoarivonina et al., 2002). Other mechanisms 
aiding in adhesion of Ruminococcus albus and R. flavefaciens to cellulose in the rumen include 
an exopolysaccharide glycocalyx, a type of Extracellular Polymeric Substances (EPS) (Morrison 
and Miron, 2000; Mosoni and Gaillard-Martinie, 2001; White et al., 2014). EPS production by 
the gut microbiota is poorly studied, and limited to lactobacilli and bifidobacteria (Ruas-Madiedo 
et al., 2006). Overall, the extent to which bacterial adhesins, EPS production and flagellar 
activity play a role in bran attachment needs to be further substantiated.

The addition of wheat bran or inulin as a substrate also shaped the luminal microbial community 
in this study. The observed shifts in microbiota composition were largely donor-dependent and 
included stimulation of Eubacterium rectale, Bacteroides spp., Ruminococcus spp., 
Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Prevotella copri and Clostridium cluster XIVa spp. by inulin and 
Eubacterium rectale, Bacteroides spp., Clostridium cluster XIVa spp., Escherichia/Shigella spp. 
and Sutterella spp. by wheat bran. Many others have already demonstrated the potential of these 
substrates to induce changes in the microbiota composition (Duncan et al., 2003; Ramirez-Farias 
et al., 2009; Roberfroid et al., 2010; Sonnenburg et al., 2010; Walker et al., 2011; Flint et al., 
2012a; Duncan et al., 2016). Yet differences in tested substrate (e.g. degree of polymerization 
and substitution), experimental setup and analytical methodology often result in highly variable 
observations with both inulin and wheat bran stimulating Bacteroides spp., Prevotella spp., 
Lachnospiraceae, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Ruminococcus spp., Eubacterium rectale, other 
members from Clostridium cluster IV and XIVa and bifidobacteria and lactobacilli (Gibson et 
al., 1995; Kleessen et al., 2001; Apajalahti et al., 2002; Duncan et al., 2003; Bouhnik et al., 2004; 
Langlands et al., 2004; Makras et al., 2005; Gourgue-Jeannot et al., 2006; Van der Meulen et al., 
2006; Grootaert et al., 2007; Hughes et al., 2007; Kolida et al., 2007; Vardakou et al., 2007; 
Ramirez-Farias et al., 2009; Sanchez et al., 2009; Costabile et al., 2010; Broekaert et al., 2011; 
Damen et al., 2011; Van den Abbeele et al., 2011b; Van Den Abbeele et al., 2011a; Walton et al., 
2012; Van den Abbeele et al., 2013a; Duncan et al., 2016). The capacity to use a substrate will be 
a predominant factor explaining the observed changes in relative species abundance upon inulin 
or wheat bran treatment. Bacteroides ovatus for instance, is equipped with the required 
enzymatic systems for arabinoxylan breakdown and is stimulated by wheat bran in donor 5 and 6 
(White et al., 2014; Rogowski et al., 2016). However, many other factors determine an 
ecosystem’s response to a given substrate amendment. A species’ initial abundance in the 
Page 18 of 84
Wiley-Blackwell and Society for Applied Microbiology
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
19 
 
community, its interactions both positive (cross-feeding) and negative (competition due to 
functional redundancy) with other community members and environmental modulation all 
account for the variability in effects of these substrates as reported in literature.  
The variability and even unpredictability of the community response upon substrate amendment 
was also reflected by the observed shifts in functionality. As expected and previously shown, 
addition of inulin and wheat bran increases the production of SCFA for all donors (Bourquin et 
al., 1992; De Preter et al., 2010). Stimulation of propionate (donors 5,6,8,9,10) or butyrate 
production (donors 2,4,7) was primarily determined by donor-origin, explaining the highly 
inconclusive body of literature concerning the specific butyrogenic effect of inulin and wheat 
bran or the presumed propionate promoting effect of wheat bran arabinoxylans (Apajalahti et al., 
2002; Van de Wiele et al., 2004; Hughes et al., 2007; van de Wiele et al., 2007; Grootaert et al., 
2009; Vanhaecke et al., 2009; Damen et al., 2011; Van den Abbeele et al., 2011b; Van Den 
Abbeele et al., 2011a; Damen et al., 2012; Macfarlane and Macfarlane, 2012; Walton et al., 
2012; Van den Abbeele et al., 2013a; Duncan et al., 2016). Similarities in donor microbial 
community composition, as determined by clustering, corresponded to the observed inter-
individual differences in response. Relating functionality to community composition is, however, 
complex. Especially with respect to glycan breakdown and carbohydrate fermentation, the high 
level of functional redundancy results in a huge inter-individual variability. It is therefore not 
surprising that using Sparse partial least squares regression no clear associations could be 
retrieved between certain species and functionality within the current experiment, considering 
only ten donors. 
To conclude, we have demonstrated that wheat bran can act as a dietary platform for preferential 
colonization by specific bacteria. Revisiting the ‘dinner table’ hypothesis, it is interesting to see 
Page 19 of 84
Wiley-Blackwell and Society for Applied Microbiology
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
20 
 
that core functionalities represented by different bacterial groups were preserved in all donors. 
The key players and mechanisms involved in primary degradation of insoluble wheat bran are 
still unresolved. Subsequent primary degradation of soluble wheat bran components can be 
carried out by Prevotella and Bacteroides spp., possessing extended enzyme systems. The 
increased presence of Megamonas, Bifidobacterium and Roseburia spp. indicates that primary 
degraders and cross-feeders are united in a trophic network on the bran (Flint et al., 2012b; 
White et al., 2014). This sort of cooperative strategy was also suggested to be in place for mucin, 
resistant starch and cellulose utilization (Macfarlane et al., 2011; Belzer and de Vos, 2012; Ze et 
al., 2012; Michel, 2015). Finally, the large inter-individual differences in response to wheat bran 
or inulin supplementation, as observed in this experiment, warrants attention (Walker et al., 
2011; Macfarlane and Macfarlane, 2012). As a result of functional redundancy, many of the 
above-mentioned functions are being carried out by different species depending on an 
individual’s microbiota composition. The lack of predictable outcomes suggests a more 
individualized approach for dietary modulation is appropriate.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES 
Experimental set-up  
To examine the inter-individual variability in the fermentation of wheat bran, fecal samples were 
collected from ten healthy individuals, without a history of antibiotic use six months prior to the 
study. Apart from subject 8, who is vegetarian, all participants consumed a mixed Western diet. 
The five male (2,3,5,6,9) and five female (1,4,7,8,10) subjects were of the age 23-37. Research 
incubation work with fecal microbiota from human origin was approved by the ethical committee 
of the Ghent University hospital under registration number B670201214538. 
Fecal samples were collected in sterile airtight containers comprising an AnaeroGenTM sachet 
(Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, Hampshire, UK) to maintain anoxic conditions until processing. 
Within an hour, a 20% (w/v) fecal slurry was prepared according to De Boever et al. (2000) by 
suspending 20 g of fecal material in 100 mL 0.1 M phosphate buffer at pH 7, containing 1 g L-1 
sodium thioglycolate as reducing agent (C2H3O2SNa) (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, US). After 
homogenization in a stomacher (LabBlender 400, Seward Ltd, Worthing, West Sussex, UK) for 
10 min, the fecal slurry was transferred to falcon tubes and centrifuged for 2 min at 500 x g to 
remove the particulate material (De Boever et al., 2000). 250 µL of the fecal slurry was 
centrifuged for 10 min at 5000 x g and, after supernatant removal, the pellet was stored for one 
month at -20°C for subsequent DNA extraction. 
Before adding inulin (Fibruline®,Cosucra, Warcoing, Belgium) and micronized (particle size 
diameter of 150 µm) commercial wheat bran (Dossche Mills, Deinze, Belgium), with chemical 
composition displayed in Table 1, to the fecal slurry, an in vitro batch digestion was carried out 
to mimic digestion during passage through the stomach and small intestine. The digestion 
protocol was adapted from Minekus et al. (2014). Briefly, wheat bran samples (45 g) and inulin 
Page 21 of 84
Wiley-Blackwell and Society for Applied Microbiology
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.

were incubated at 37°C with simulated saliva fluid for 15 min (1:2 w/v), simulated gastric fluid 
for 2 h (1:1 v/v) and simulated pancreatic juice for 2h (1:1 w/v). The simulated gastro-intestinal 
fluids consisted of an electrolyte solution to which the respective enzymes were added. In 
addition to the simulated saliva fluid described by Minekus et al. (2014), 0.050 g L-1 mucin from 
porcine stomach Type II (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, US) was added. The composition of the 
pancreatic juice was adapted by adding 3 g L-1 pancreatin from porcine pancreas (Sigma Aldrich, 
St. Louis, MO, US) and 8 g L-1 Oxgall dehydrated fresh bile (BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ, US) to the 
autoclaved electrolyte solution. Insoluble undigested material was centrifuged and lyophilized to 
preserve until chemical analysis or batch incubation in hungate tubes (Glasgeratebau Ochs 
Gmbh, Bovenden-Lenglern, Finland) with fecal slurries prepared from the ten donors. Inulin, a 
well characterized prebiotic, was included as a control. Prebiotics were supplemented in a 1% 
concentration in a carbohydrate-low medium containing 3 g L-1 yeast extract (Oxoid Ltd., 
Basingstoke, Hampshire, UK), 1 g L-1 peptone (Oxoid Ltd., Basingstoke, Hampshire, UK) and 1 
g L-1 mucin from porcine stomach Type II (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, US), dissolved in 0.1 
M phosphate buffer at pH 6.8. As a negative control, carbohydrate-low medium without addition 
of product was taken along. The tubes were flushed with N2 during 30 cycles to obtain anaerobic 
conditions prior to inoculation. The cycle was stopped at overpressure and before the start of the 
experiment the tubes were set at atmospheric pressure. Inoculation at a 1:5 dilution of the 20% 
(w/v) fecal slurry resulted in a final concentration of 4% (w/v) fecal inoculum in the hungate 
tubes. Consequently, the hungate tubes were incubated at 37°C and 150 rpm on an orbital shaker. 
The course of fermentation was followed up by measuring the pH and gas pressure after 6, 24 
and 48h to verify stable batch conditions. The starting pH was 6.8 and remained constant in the 
control. Some acidification, linked to the production of short chain fatty acids, is observed after 
addition of wheat bran (6.6 +- 0.36) and inulin (6.13+-0.31) after 48h, however pH remains

al. (2014). Total Ammonium Nitrogen (TAN) was determined as an indicator for protein

repeated three times.

Microbial community analysis 
Samples from the fecal slurry of the ten donors, as well as samples after 48h of incubation from 
the luminal liquid phase in the control, inulin and bran condition and bran samples after washing 
from all ten donors were selected for an in depth microbial community analysis, applying 
Illumina next generation 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. 
DNA extraction was performed as reported by Geirnaert et al. (2015). As starting material, the 
pellet obtained after centrifuging 250 µL of luminal sample at 5000 x g for 10 min or 0.1 g bran 
aliquots were used. 1 mL Tris/HCl (100 mM pH 8) lysis buffer (Vilchez-Vargas et al., 2013) and 
200 mg glass beads (0.11 mm Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) were added and cells were 
mechanically lysed by multidirectional beating for two times 40 s at 1600 rpm in a FastPrep®-96 
instrument (MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, US). Glass beads were removed by centrifugation 
for 5 min at maximum speed, DNA was purified by a phenol-chloroform extraction and 
precipitated by adding 1 volume ice-cold isopropyl alcohol and 1:10 volume 3 M sodium acetate 
for at least 1 h at -20°C, followed by centrifugation for 30 min at maximum speed. The 
supernatant was discarded and the DNA pellet was dried and suspended in 100 µL 1x TE buffer 
(10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA). The DNA quality was verified by electrophoresis on a 1.5% (w/v) 
agarose gel. DNA was quantified by a fluorescence assay using the QuantiFluor® dsDNA kit 
(Promega, Madison, WI, US) and Glomax®-Multi+ system (Promega, Madison, WI, US) and 
normalised to contain 1 ng µL-1 DNA. Samples were sent out to the company LGC Genomics 
(Teddington, Middlesex, UK) for library preparation and sequencing on an Illumina Miseq 
platform. In a first step the V3-V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR using 
primers derived from Klindworth et al. (2013), with a slight modification to the reverse primer 
by introducing another degenerated position (K) to make it more universal (Klindworth et al.,

Bioinformatics analysis of amplicon data  
The mothur software package (v.1.33.3) and guidelines were used to process the amplicon data 
generated by LGC Genomics (Kozich et al., 2013). The detailed information can be consulted in 
the supplementary data. In short, after assembling forward and reverse reads, contigs with a 
length between 441 and 467 bases were aligned to the mothur formatted silva_seed release 119 
alignment database, trimmed between positions 6388 and 25316, to be compatible with the 341F-
785R primers (Quast et al., 2013). After removing non-aligning sequences as well as sequences 
containing homopolymer stretches of more than 12 bases, sequences are preclustered allowing up 
to 4 differences. UCHIME was applied to remove chimera (Edgar et al., 2011). Subsequently, 
sequences were classified, by means of a naive Bayesian classifier, against the RDP 16S rRNA 
gene training set, version 14 with an 80% cutoff for the pseudobootstrap confidence score. Only 
bacterial sequences were retained and sequences derived from the mock community samples 
were precluded after calculating the sequencing error rates. This resulted in an average error rate 
of 0.93± 0.02% and a number of 83-89-126 spurious OTU’s out of 9571-16800-11513 sequences 
for the triplicate mock samples. A total of 1,378,852 withheld sequences were binned into 
Operational Taxonomic Units (OTU’s) within each order identified by the preceding 
classification step. An OTU is defined in this manuscript as a collection of sequences with a 
length between 430 and 465 nucleotides that are found to be more than 97% similar to one 
another in the V3-V4 region of their 16S rRNA gene after applying hierarchical clustering 
(Schloss et al., 2009; Schloss and Westcott, 2011; Wang et al., 2012; Chen et al., 2013). 14,976 
OTU’s are identified in the 50 samples. Finally, taxonomy assignment was obtained according to 
the RDP version 14 and silva.nr_v119 database (Wang et al., 2007; Quast et al., 2013; Cole et 
Page 27 of 84
Wiley-Blackwell and Society for Applied Microbiology
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
28 
 
al., 2014). Figures in this manuscript display the RDP taxonomy. Clostridium cluster XIVa in the 
figures should be interpreted as ‘Unclassified Genus belonging to Clostridium cluster XIVa’. 
Taxa that are part of the Clostidrium cluster XIVa, but that could be classified otherwise at the 
genus level are not included in the Clostridium cluster XIVa. The shared file, containing the 
number of reads observed for each OTU in each sample, was loaded into R version 3.2.2 (2015-
08-14) (R Core Team, 2016). Singletons, i.e., reads occurring only once in all samples were 
considered noise and were removed (McMurdie and Holmes, 2014). From the 14,976 reads, 
2245 were retained. For the most abundant OTU’s the sequences retrieved from the 3% 
dissimilarity level fasta file, obtained in mothur, were classified through the RDP web interface 
using the RDP SeqMatch tool restricting the database search to type strains with only near-full-
length good quality sequences and blasted in NCBI against the 16 S rRNA gene sequences, 
selecting only type material, with optimization of the BLAST algorithm for highly similar 
sequences (accession date: December 2016) (Altschul et al., 1990; Wang et al., 2007; Cole et al., 
2014). Although a certain level of uncertainty is introduced by classification to the species level 
based on short 300 bp reads, the best hit returned by both databases is used to refer to interesting 
OTU’s in the results section of this paper. In case inconsistencies were seen between the RDP 
SeqMatch tool and NCBI BLAST, no species level classification is mentioned. A more detailed 
overview of the RDP Seqmatch and NCBI BLAST results for the 30 most abundant OTU’s (best 
hit as well as the next best hit) can be found in the supplementary information (Supplementary 
data, Table S2).

Statistical analysis 
All statistical analysis were performed in R, version 3.3.1 (2016-06-21) (R Core Team, 2016). 
All formal hypothesis test were conducted on the 5% significance level (α = 0.05).  
To visualize differences in microbial community composition between donors and products 
(inulin, wheat bran and control), ordination and clustering techniques were applied. For these 
purposes, the shared file was further processed to remove OTU’s with too low abundance 
according to the arbitrary cutoffs described by McMurdie and Holmes (2014). An OTU should 
be observed in 5% of the samples and read counts should exceed 0.5 times the number of 
samples (McMurdie and Holmes, 2014). Rarefaction curves were constructed to assure that the 
samples were sequenced in sufficient depth (Supplementary data, Figure S9) (Oksanen et al., 
2016). To deal with differences in sampling depth, proportional data transformed on the common 
scale to the lowest number of reads was used (McMurdie and Holmes, 2014). Principle 
Coordinate Analysis (PCoA; package stats4_3.3.1) (Gower, 1966; Cailliez, 1983; Becker et al., 
1988; Cox, 2001; Ramette, 2007) was conducted based on the abundance based jaccard 
dissimilarity matrix (package vegan_2.4-0) (Anderson et al., 2006; Borcard et al., 2011; Oksanen 
et al., 2016) and visualized with ggplot2_2.1.0 (Wickham, 2009). This procedure was repeated 
on species and genus level focusing on both the comparison between luminal and bran samples 
on the one hand and the comparison of products in the luminal samples on the other hand. On the 
genus level, weighed averages of genera abundances were a posteriori added to the ordination 
plot using the wascores function in vegan (Oksanen et al., 2016). To confirm the trends, observed 
data was clustered by means of an Unweighted Pair-Grouped Method using arithmetic Averages 
(UPGMA) clustering method (cluster_2.0.4) (Maechler et al., 2016). The significance of the 
observed group separation between luminal and bran samples in the PCoA was assessed with a 
Page 29 of 84
Wiley-Blackwell and Society for Applied Microbiology
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
30 
 
Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) using distance matrixes 
(package vegan_2.4-0) (Ramette, 2007; Oksanen et al., 2016). Prior to this formal hypothesis 
testing, the assumption of similar multivariate dispersions was evaluated. The influence of the 
inter-individual variability when comparing the effects of inulin and wheat bran on the control 
was determined by applying a distance based redundancy analysis (db RDA) (Vardakou et al.) 
using the abundance based jaccard distance as a response variable (vegan) (Ramette, 2007; 
Oksanen et al., 2016). The factor donor is used as a constraint with the effect of the product 
(control, inulin or wheat bran) being partialled out. Interpretation of the results is preceded by a 
permutation test of the RDA results to confirm that a linear relationship exists between the 
response data and the exploratory variables. The constrained fraction of the variance, explained 
by the exploratory variables is adjusted by applying Ezekiel’s formula (Borcard et al., 2011). 
In order to find statistically significant differences in species abundance between the different 
treatments (inulin, wheat bran and control), the DESeq package was applied as suggested by 
McMurdie and Holmes (2014) (Love et al., 2014a; Love et al., 2014b; McMurdie and Holmes, 
2014). The factors donor and product were used in the design formula. Statistical differences 
between lumen and bran environment were determined using a Wilcoxon Signed Rank Test on 
the proportional data.  
Statistical hypothesis testing to assess the effect of the products on the functional parameters was 
performed by using the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test, followed by Pairwise Wilcoxon Rank Sum 
Tests with Holm correction for multiple testing.  
In order to relate the microbial community composition with produced metabolites, a Multiple 
Factor Analysis (MFA) was performed correlating the Hellinger transformed proportional 
community data and the molar acetate, propionate, butyrate, valerate, branched SCFA and

Wiley-Blackwell and Society for Applied Microbiology

ammonium concentrations. A permutation test of the RV coefficients was consequently used to 
test the null hypothesis that functional data and community composition are not significantly 
linked (Le et al., 2008; Borcard et al., 2011). In a next step smart feature selection was applied to 
look for specific species associations with the produced metabolites by using sparse partial least 
squares regression, available in the R-package mixOmics (Le Cao et al., 2016). The initial model 
was built in regression mode with the molar acetate, propionate, butyrate, summed branched 
short chain fatty acids, valerate and ammonium concentration as response (Y) variables in 
function of the DESeq transformed variance stabilized read counts (X). 2 Dimensions, 3 Y 
variables and 50 X variables were selected after tuning based on a Leave One Out (LOO) 
validation (Le Cao et al., 2008; Le Cao et al., 2016).
