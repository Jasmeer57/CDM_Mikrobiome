Abstract
Objectives  The involvement of the gut microbiota 
in the pathogenesis of calcium nephrolithiasis has 
been hypothesised since the discovery of the oxalate-
degrading activity of Oxalobacter formigenes, but never 
comprehensively studied with metagenomics. The aim 
of this case–control study was to compare the faecal 
microbiota composition and functionality between 
recurrent idiopathic calcium stone formers (SFs) and 
controls.
Design  Faecal samples were collected from 52 SFs 
and 48 controls (mean age 48±11). The microbiota 
composition was analysed through 16S rRNA microbial 
profiling approach. Ten samples (five SFs, five controls) 
were also analysed with deep shotgun metagenomics 
sequencing, with focus on oxalate-degrading microbial 
metabolic pathways. Dietary habits, assessed through 
a food-frequency questionnaire, and 24-hour urinary 
excretion of prolithogenic and antilithogenic factors, 
including calcium and oxalate, were compared between 
SFs and controls, and considered as covariates in the 
comparison of microbiota profiles.
Results  SFs exhibited lower faecal microbial diversity 
than controls (Chao1 index 1460±363vs 1658±297, 
fully adjusted p=0.02 with stepwise backward 
regression analysis). At multivariate analyses, three 
taxa (Faecalibacterium, Enterobacter, Dorea) were 
significantly less represented in faecal samples of SFs. 
The Oxalobacter abundance was not different between 
groups. Faecal samples from SFs exhibited a significantly 
lower bacterial representation of genes involved in 
oxalate degradation, with inverse correlation with 24-
hour oxalate excretion (r=−0.87, p=0.002). The oxalate-
degrading genes were represented in several bacterial 
species, whose cumulative abundance was inversely 
correlated with oxaluria (r=−0.85, p=0.02).
Conclusions  Idiopathic calcium SFs exhibited altered 
gut microbiota composition and functionality that could 
contribute to nephrolithiasis physiopathology.
Introduction
Calcium nephrolithiasis (CN) is a chronic disease 
with a high prevalence in populations of Cauca-
sian and Arab ethnicity, characterised by recurrent 
episodes of renal colic with stones of calcium oxalate 
composition.1 The mechanisms of stone formation 
can be identified in only few cases, where congen-
ital abnormalities of the urinary tract or defined 
disorders of calcium and oxalate metabolism are 
present.2 Otherwise, the kidney stone disease (KSD) 
is called idiopathic, and its main risk factors are 
represented by poor fluid intake and dietary imbal-
ances, including excessive salt and animal protein 
consumption.3–5

These factors facilitate calcium oxalate salt precipitation in the 
urinary microenvironment,6 resulting from an altered balance 
between lithogenesis promoters (ie, calcium, oxalate, phosphate, 
uric acid) and inhibitors (potassium, magnesium and citrate).7 
Hypercalciuria and hyperoxaluria are the most frequent meta-
bolic conditions associated with CN.6 7
Oxalate excretion results from endogenous catabolism of 
hyproxyproline, uracil, orotic and ascorbic acid.8–10 Dietary 
oxalate bioavailability is also involved.10 Low-calcium diets 
generally prevent calcium oxalate chelation in the intestinal 
lumen, promoting oxalate absorption and predisposing to hyper-
oxaluria.11 Conversely, the presence of oxalate-degrading func-
tionality in the gut microbiota may limit oxalate absorption and 
reduce oxalate excretion.10 12 13
In vitro assays have demonstrated that the Gram-negative gut 
commensal Oxalobacter formigenes is able to degrade oxalate, 
through expression of two enzymes, oxalyl-CoA decarbox-
ylase and formyl-CoA transferase.14 15 Oxalate represents in 
fact the only energy source for O. formigenes metabolism. 
Such micro-organism is commonly identified in the human gut 
microbiota, particularly in healthy subjects without a history of 
nephrolithiasis.16
Culturomic analyses have shown that O. formigenes was 
significantly less represented in stool samples of stone formers 
(SFs) than controls.17 However, no association was found 
between O. formigenes occurrence and urinary oxalate excre-
tion in SFs under free diet.18 The administration of functional 
foods containing O. formigenes or other bacteria, engineered 
with oxalate-degrading functionality, showed a decrease of 
oxalate excretion in SFs with hyperoxaluria.19 20 However, the 
only randomised placebo-controlled clinical trial performed in 
SFs21 and a cross-over study in healthy volunteers22 showed no 
influence of probiotics on oxalate excretion.
The emergence of high-throughput bacterial DNA sequencing 
techniques, that is, 16S rRNA microbial profiling and shotgun 
metagenomics,23 has allowed to better understand the human 
gut microbiota composition and unveil its physiopathological 
role in a large number of diseases.24In vitro studies have demon-
strated that the oxalate-degrading capacity is not limited to O. 
formigenes, but may be present in a large number of species 
using oxalate as a secondary substrate for growth.25 Thus, 
the gut microbiota may have a distinct composition and func-
tionality in KSD, influencing an increased oxalate load to the 
kidneys.26
The aim of our case–control study was to establish the possible 
differences in gut microbiota composition and functionality 
between recurrent calcium oxalate SFs and stone-free controls, 
using next-generation metagenomics sequencing techniques.
Methods
Study participants and ethical standards
At the Kidney Stone Clinic of Parma University  Hospital, 
we enrolled 55 recurrent idiopathic calcium SFs (33  men, 
22  women; mean age 48±11, range 24–70). The subjects 
reported at least two episodes of renal colic in their personal 
history and stones of calcium oxalate composition ≥80%, docu-
mented by passed stone analysis or inferred by the presence of 
radio-opaque stones in abdominal X-ray images. Subjects with 
stone composition other than calcic or radio-opaque, secondary 
causes of CN, congenital abnormalities of the urinary tract, 
recurrent urinary tract infections or history of major urological 
surgery were excluded.
We also enrolled 53 healthy volunteers (32 men, 21 women; 
mean age 47±13, range 22–74) as controls. They were 
included based on the absence of episodes of renal colic in their 
personal history and of subclinical retained stones at abdominal 
ultrasound.
Excluded from both groups were subjects under antibiotic 
treatment in the 30 days before the evaluation, with gastro-
intestinal or systemic diseases known to affect gut microbiota 
composition, chronic kidney disease with glomerular filtration 
rate  <40 mL/min or blood creatinine  >1.4 mg/dL, following 
restricted dietary regimens or taking prebiotics/probiotics.
All the participants gave their written informed consent for 
participation in the study. The study protocol was approved by 
the local ethics committee. All the procedures followed the Good 
Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki principles.
The sample size was calculated considering the preliminary 
results by Stern and colleagues, who reported a 25% difference 
in the average relative abundance of some key taxa (Bacteroides 
and Prevotella) between SFs and controls.26 According to the χ2 
statistics, a sample size of 41 subjects is sufficient to detect such a 
difference, with two-sided α=0.05 and β=0.20. This sample size 
is in line with previous metagenomics studies performed with a 
similar design.27
Study procedures
All participants collected a 24-hour urine sample for determina-
tion of the urinary profile of lithogenic risk. This is a panel of 
19 examinations involving the determination of calcium, magne-
sium, potassium, sodium, sulfate, chloride, phosphorus, oxalate, 
citrate, uric acid, urea, creatinine, ammonium, pH, volume and 
supersaturation indexes for calcium oxalate, calcium phosphate, 
uric acid and struvite, providing a detailed overview of the 
lithogenic salt precipitation. Its prescription is recommended by 
international consensus in the diagnostic work-up of recurrent 
calcium nephrolithiasis.2 The procedures of urine sample collec-
tion and the used laboratory techniques are detailed elsewhere.28 
Namely, 24-hour urinary oxalate excretion (UOE) was deter-
mined by ion chromatography. The urine samples were collected 
under free diet, and participants were instructed not to modify 
their dietary habits, to avoid Hawthorne effect, in compliance 
with consensus recommendations.2
Each participant also collected a 2-gram stool sample by spon-
taneous evacuation. Samples were delivered at our Stone Clinic 
within 2 hours from collection, immediately frozen at −22°C 
and submitted to laboratory analyses, as detailed below, within 
2 weeks from collection.
To assess long-term nutritional habits that could influence gut 
microbiota composition, a food-frequency questionnaire (FFQ) 
validated in the Italian population (European Prospective Inves-
tigation into Cancer and Nutrition, EPIC) was administered to

16S rRNA microbial profiling analyses
The bacterial DNA was extracted from stool samples, and 
the partial 16S rRNA gene sequences were amplified with the 
QIAamp DNA Stool Mini kit according to the manufacturer’s 
instructions (Qiagen, Strasse, Germany). The primer pairs Probio 
Uni and/Probio_Rev, targeting the V3 region of the 16S rRNA 
gene sequence, were used for amplification.30 Illumina adapter 
overhang nucleotide sequences were added to the 16S rRNA 
gene-specific sequences. The 16S metagenomic Sequencing 
Library Preparation Protocol was used to prepare the 16S rRNA 
gene amplicons. A Verity Thermocycler (Applied Biosystems) 
was used for amplifications with PCR. A 2200 TapeStation 
Instrument (Agilent Technologies, USA) was used to verify the 
integrity of PCR amplicons by electrophoresis.
To remove primer dimers, the amplified 16S rRNA gene 
sequences were then purified by magnetic purification step with 
the Agencourt AMPure XP DNA purification beads (Beckman 
Coulter Genomics GmbH, Bernried, Germany). DNA concen-
tration of the amplified sequence library was estimated through 
fluorimetric Qubit quantification system (Life Technologies). 
DNA amplicon solutions were diluted to 4 nM, and 5 µL of each 
solution was mixed to build the pooled final library. An Illu-
mina MiSeq sequencer with MiSeq Reagent Kit V.3 chemicals 
was used for sequencing.
The obtained fastq files were then processed with QIIME 
following a protocol validated by the literature.31 Paired-end 
reads were merged to obtain sequences with a length between 
140 and 400 bp and mean quality score >25. Quality control 
allowed truncation of a sequence at the first base if a low-quality 
rolling 10 bp window was found, and eliminated sequences with 
mismatched forward and/or reverse primers. The reads were 
pooled in operational taxonomic units (OTUs) with the uclust 
software, using a 99% identity cut-off.32 All OTUs encompassing 
less than 10 sequences were removed in a subsequent filtering 
step to reduce noise. These data were used to determine the 
Chao1 index of biodiversity and to perform a beta-diversity 
analysis of interindividual variability using the Principal Coor-
dinate Analysis (PCoA) method based on weighted UniFrac and 
Bray-Curtis dissimilarity at genus level. The QIIME software 
suite31 and the related 16S database SILVA,33 V.128, were used 
for taxonomic classification of OTUs. The relative abundance of 
every taxon was calculated in all samples.
The 16S rRNA microbial profiling analysis of the 100 stool 
samples (52 SFs, 48 controls) produced a total number of 9 
933 784 reads. The quality and chimaera filtering resulted in 
8 236 905 reads, with a 76 268 average per sample (see online 
supplementary table 1).
Shotgun metagenomics analysis of faecal samples
DNA extracted from 10 faecal samples selected to include 
five controls and five SF subjects, representative of the average 
composition of controls and SF individuals, was employed to 
perform deep shotgun metagenomics sequencing. Bacterial DNA 
was extracted from stool samples as detailed above. It was subse-
quently fragmented to 550–650 bp by means of a BioRuptor 
machine (Diagenodo, Belgium). Preparation of samples was 
performed in compliance with the TruSeq Nano DNA Sample 
Preparation Guide. An Illumina NextSeq sequencer with 
NextSeq 500/550 High Output Reagent Cartridge V.2 300 cycles 
chemicals was used.
The obtained fastq files were filtered for reads with 
length <80 bp, quality <25 and presence of human DNA. Bases 
were also removed from the end of the reads until the average 
quality in a window of 5 bp was >25 and only paired data were 
further analysed. A custom metagenomic platform for analysis 
of metagenomics data, METAnnotatorX, was used to perform 
a reads-based homology search of the metagenomics datasets 
against a custom database encompassing all the known homo-
logues of the oxalate-degradation genes formyl-CoA transferase

In the preliminary analysis, clinical, nutritional, laboratory 
urinary variables and faecal microbiota alpha-diversity (Chao1 
index) were compared between SFs and controls using linear or 
multinomial logistic regression analysis or the χ2 test, as appro-
priate. Alpha  diversity was also compared between SFs and 
controls with a stepwise backward regression analysis model, 
accounting for all available covariates.
Interindividual variability (beta  diversity) between SFs and 
controls was evaluated with permutational analysis of variance 
(PERMANOVA) and Adonis test.
The average relative abundance of all detected taxa, including 
those with purported oxalate-degrading properties,25 was 
compared between SFs and controls using analysis of variance 
(ANOVA). For those taxa with different abundance between 
the two groups, multivariate regression analysis was applied. 
Age, sex and body mass index were considered as covariates 
(model 1), together with BSS and all the variables with statis-
tically significant differences at the preliminary analyses (model 
2). In a further model, weekly number of bowel movements, 
alpha diversity and the relative abundance of Prevotella, Rumi-
nococcus and Methanobrevibacter were also considered (model 
3) since previous studies demonstrated that all these variables 
may influence microbiota composition or may be related with 
stool moisture, a major factor influencing the metagenomics 
results.35 36 Finally, a comprehensive stepwise backward regres-
sion analysis was performed (model 4).
The correlation between the relative abundance of the most 
represented taxa and 24-hour UOE was assessed in both SFs and 
controls with multiple regression analysis (models 1, 2 and 3) 
and stepwise backward regression analysis (model 4), following 
the same procedure detailed above for covariate selection.
The microbial representation of genes involved in oxalate 
degradation was compared between the SFs and controls 
selected for shotgun metagenomics analysis using PERMANOVA 
and co-occurrence analysis. For the shotgun-sequenced samples, 
Pearson analysis was used to verify the correlation between 
24-hour UOE and microbial representation of genes belonging 
to oxalate degradation pathway, and the correlation between 
UOE and relative abundance of taxa harbouring oxalate-de-
grading functionalities.
Statistical analyses were performed with the SAS V.9.2 (SAS 
Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA) and the SPSS V.23 (IBM) 
software, considering p values <0.05 as statistically significant.
Results
General characteristics of SFs and controls
All the 55 SFs and the 53 controls who participated in the study 
provided a stool sample for metagenomics analyses. However, 
eight participants (three SFs, five controls) were excluded due to 
scarceness of the stool sample or alterations in preservation. The 
final analysis was carried out on 52 SFs (31 men, 21 women) and 
48 controls (28 men, 20 women).
At linear or multinomial logistic regression models, SFs did 
not differ from controls for age, anthropometry and bowel 
movements, but had a higher 24-hour  excretion of calcium 
(median 238, IQR 183–354, vs 163, IQR 121–231, mg/24 hours, 
p=0.003) and oxalate (29±11 vs 25±8 mg/24 hours, p=0.03) 
(table 1).
The average nutritional intakes of SFs and controls, estimated 
through the EPIC FFQ questionnaire, are reported in table 2. 
Total energy, macronutrient and salt intake was not different 
between the two groups, but SFs had a lower intake of calcium 
than controls (median 728.9, IQR 473.7–874.4, vs 806.5, IQR 
627.6–1000.2, mg/day, p=0.04 with logistic regression analysis).
Biodiversity of the faecal microbiota
The rarefaction curves constructed through evaluation of 
the Chao1 biodiversity index for 10 subsamplings of the 100 
data sets are shown in figure 1. At the highest sequencing depth, 
the Chao1 biodiversity index was significantly lower in SFs than 
in controls (number of OTUs 1460±363 vs 1658±297, age-ad-
justed and sex-adjusted p value <0.001 with logistic regression 
analysis) (figure  1). The lower biodiversity in faecal samples 
from SFs confirmed also in a backward regression analysis model 
considering all the variables listed in tables 1 and 2 as possible 
covariates (β±SE 0.0004±0.0001; p=0.02).
PCoA based on weighted UniFrac matrix and Bray-Curtis 
dissimilarity at genus level showed that the microbiota compo-
sition of SFs and controls was different even in the absence 
of evident clusterisation at visual inspection (p=0.002 with 
PERMANOVA; p=0.004 with Adonis test) (figure 2).

Taxonomic analysis of faecal microbiota composition
Taxonomic assignment of the OTUs predicted for all the 100 
samples revealed the composition of their bacterial population 
down to genus level (online supplementary figures 1 and 2). 
Inspection of the profiles, retrieved at phylum level, evidenced 
a statistically significant difference (p<0.001 with ANOVA) 
between SFs and controls in the average abundance of Bacte-
roidetes and Firmicutes (table 3). Namely, SFs showed a higher 
average representation of Bacteroidetes (57.7% vs 44.2%) and 
a lower average representation of Firmicutes (39.1% vs 51.3%) 
and Tenericutes (table 3 and online supplementary figure 1).
At species level, an average number of 112 taxa with relative 
abundance  >0.001% could be identified in samples from SFs, 
while 123 taxa could be identified in controls. ANOVA of all the 
profiled taxa showed that the average representation of some 
taxa, at different taxonomic levels, was significantly different 
between SFs and controls (table 3).
The average relative abundance of the main identified taxa 
was also compared between SFs and controls with multivar-
iate regression analysis (models 1, 2 and 3), and stepwise back-
ward regression analysis (model 4) accounting for all possible 
covariates, as shown in table 4. Three taxa showed a significant 
(p<0.05) lower representation in SFs independently of covari-
ates: Dorea, Enterobacter and Faecalibacterium.
Identification of oxalate-degrading bacteria
Oxalobacter formigenes was detected in all the analysed faecal 
samples, with very low representation (<0.001% in all samples).

Functional analysis of faecal microbiota through shotgun 
metagenomics
The faecal microbiota of SFs displayed a reduced representation 
of genes involved in oxalate degradation, that is, formyl-CoA 
transferase and oxalyl-CoA decarboxylase, than controls 
(0.0021% vs 0.0041% of the total genome, p=0.007 with 
PERMANOVA), as shown in figure 3.
Pearson correlation analysis also demonstrated a nega-
tive correlation between the gut microbiota representation of 
oxalate-degrading genes and 24-hour UOE in the faecal and 
urinary samples of the 10 subjects (SFs and controls) selected 
for shotgun metagenomics analyses (r=−0.87, p=0.002) 
(figure 4A).
A custom metagenomic platform for analysis of metagenomics 
data, METAnnotatorX, allowed the identification in the faecal 
microbiomes of the taxa encompassing oxalate-degrading genes 
in their genomes (online supplementary figures 3 and 4). The 
highest representation of these genes was detected in some 
Archaea and bacteria, either with known (O. formigenes) or 
previously unknown oxalate-degrading properties (Escherichia 
coli, Eggerthella spp, Roseburia hominis, Bacteroides massil-
iensis, Clostridium citroniae). The sum of the average relative 
abundances of the taxa listed in online supplementary figure 4, 
showing oxalate-degrading features, was significantly higher in 
all the 48 controls than in all the 52 SFs (p<0.001 with ANOVA). 
Finally, the number of taxa with oxalate-degrading properties 
identified in the 10 samples analysed with shotgun metage-
nomics sequencing was significantly and negatively correlated 
with 24-hour  UOE (r=−0.85, p=0.02 with Pearson analysis) 
(figure 4B).
Discussion
This study shows that a group of subjects with recurrent idio-
pathic CN had different gut microbiota composition and func-
tionality than healthy controls. The faecal microbiota of SFs 
exhibited reduced diversity, as well as lower representation of 
taxa predicted to be involved in dietary oxalate degradation, and 
reduced expression of genes belonging to oxalate degradation 
metabolic pathways. The relative abundance of five taxa was 
also correlated with 24-hour UOE. All these differences were 
independent of dietary habits and bowel movements.
These results contribute to go beyond the simplistic concept 
that the ‘gut–kidney axis’ in nephrolithiasis is merely limited 
to O. formigenes.16–18 Preliminary data by Stern and colleagues

profile than controls, even at phylum level.26 However, in their 
study, the composition of stones passed by patients was not 
homogeneous, including both calcium oxalate and uric acid SFs, 
and the accuracy of metagenomics analyses was limited to high 
taxonomic levels.
In the present study, deep shotgun metagenomics sequencing 
allowed to identify other bacterial species that may exhibit 
significant and previously unknown oxalate-degrading proper-
ties. The in vivo oxalate degradation activity may be lower for 
these bacteria, compared with the optimal oxalate-degrading 
performance reported for O. formigenes,16 25 but their higher 
representation in faecal microbiota may make their metabolic 
activity more significant. Although the oxalate-degrading 
properties of the faecal microbiota of SFs and controls were 
not assessed directly, the detected strong negative correlation 
between the relative abundance of these previously unknown 
oxalate-degrading bacteria and 24-hour urinary oxalate excre-
tion suggests an influence of gut microbiota composition in the 
process of renal lithogenesis through modulation of intestinal 
oxalate bioavailability. This is in line with data showing a strong 
involvement of the gastrointestinal system in the KSD.10
These results could help to understand why most studies 
have demonstrated that probiotic products containing O. formi-
genes and lactobacilli with oxalate-degrading activity were 
not able to influence oxalate excretion and the nephrolithiasis 
course.18 21 22 37 In the gut microbiota, the oxalate-degrading 
activity may in fact be shared by a large number of taxa, influ-
encing each other in a complex metabolic network, and not rely 
solely on a single player or a limited number of species. Thus, 
introducing an oxalate-degrading species in the gut environment 
by administering a probiotic may not be sufficient to produce 
a significant overall endoluminal oxalate degradation. Interest-
ingly, data from environmental microbiology analyses suggest 
that the expression of genes related to oxalate metabolism is 
rather common in bacteria, but not easily identifiable with stan-
dard techniques.38
Among those taxa significantly under-represented in the faecal 
microbiota of SFs, Faecalibacterium has a documented activity 
of synthesis of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), mainly butyrate. 
In general, SCFA-producing taxa are responsible for modula-
tion of inflammation and protection against oxidative stress.39 
The depletion of Faecalibacterium could thus be involved in 
the nephrolithiasis-associated chronic kidney injury, which has 
been recently demonstrated in epidemiological and clinical 
studies.40 Furthermore, depletion of SCFA-producing taxa has 
been recently demonstrated in the gut microbiota of patients 
with chronic kidney disease.41 Although nephrolithiasis is not 
an inflammatory disease, the involvement of Faecalibacterium 
in modulating renal function and lithogenesis needs further 
investigation.
Our data also showed that dietary habits were apparently 
not involved in the nephrolithiasis-associated abnormalities of 
gut microbiota composition, with the only possible exception 
of calcium intake. This may contrast with the current paradigm 
of nephrolithiasis physiopathology and treatment, assigning a 
central role to nutrition.2–6 However, dietary habits were not 
the main outcome of the present study, which could have been 
underpowered to detect nutritional differences between SFs

low-potassium and high-animal protein intake are the most

hyperoxaluria and hypercalciuria, being associated with increased 
oxalate bioavailability and increased bone demineralisation.3
However, investigations specifically comparing the dietary 
habits of SFs and controls have demonstrated only limited differ-
ences.43 Genetic factors could explain this discrepancy,44 and the 
present study allows to hypothesise that a distinct gut micro-
biota signature could also have a role in the onset of KSD. Future 
studies, with either dietary or probiotic treatment, should verify 
this hypothesis in healthy subjects and SFs.
Some limitations should be considered when interpreting 
our results. First, only the relative abundance of single taxa (ie, 
the percentage of their representation on the overall number 
of bacteria) and not their absolute number was considered for 
statistical analyses. The relative abundance approach may not 
reflect substantial interindividual differences in faecal microbial 
loads, producing possible bias.35 Additionally, stool moisture, 
one of the major determinants of faecal microbiota composition 
and faecal bacterial load,36 was assessed only indirectly, through 
the average BSS score.
Then, the low number of faecal samples that were submitted 
to shotgun analyses suggests caution in generalising the concept 
that SFs have less oxalate-degrading functionalities in their gut 
microbiota. Some determinants of microbiota composition, such 
as lifetime cumulative antibiotic exposure, chronic medications, 
number of members of the household, exposure to pets and 
possibly vitamin D deficiency,45 which is highly prevalent in SFs 
and in the general population,46 47 were not assessed. However, 
all the participants were in good health and had a low burden 
of chronic diseases and medications. The EPIC FFQ, though 
validated in the Italian population, could have not reflected 
the complexity of dietary habits that may influence microbiota 
composition. Finally, the observational nature of the study does 
not allow to establish precise cause–effect relationships between 
differences in faecal microbiota composition and presence of 
KSD.
Despite of these limitations, the present study provides novel 
insights about the possible involvement of gut microbiota in the 
physiopathology of CN and may open new scenarios about the 
role of probiotics and prebiotics as modulators of KSD. Future 
population-based prospective studies and intervention studies 
should verify whether the gut microbiota dynamics can influence 
KSD course.48
Conclusions
A group of idiopathic calcium SFs had a distinct gut microbiota 
composition, in terms of overall biodiversity, representation of 
single taxa and oxalate-degrading genes, compared with healthy 
controls, independently of dietary habits and bowel movements. 
These findings support the hypothesis of the presence of a gut–
kidney axis in the modulation of kidney stone formation.
