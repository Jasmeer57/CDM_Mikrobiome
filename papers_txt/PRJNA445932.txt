Abstract 
Objectives: Gut bacteriome profiling studies in type 1 diabetes (T1D) to date are mostly limited to 
populations of Europe, with two studies from China and one study each from Mexico and the USA. 
We therefore sought to characterize the stool bacteriome in children after onset of T1D along with 
age- and place-matched control subjects from four geographically distant African and Asian 
countries. 
Methods: Samples were collected from 73 children and adolescents shortly after T1D onset 
(Azerbaijan 19, Jordan 20, Nigeria 14, Sudan 20) and 104 matched control subjects of similar age and 
locale. Genotyping of major T1D susceptibility genes was performed using saliva or blood samples. 
The bacteriome was profiled by next-generation sequencing of 16S rDNA.  Negative binomial 
regression was used to model associations, with adjustment for the matched structure of the study. 
Results: A significant positive association with T1D was noted for the genus Escherichia (class 
Gammaproteobacteria, phylum Proteobacteria), whereas Eubacterium and Roseburia, two genera of 
class Clostridia, phylum Firmicutes, were inversely associated with T1D. We also confirmed a 
previously observed inverse association with Clostridium clusters IV or XIVa. No associations were 
noted for richness, evenness, or enterotypes. 
Conclusions: Based on our results, some type of distortion of the gut bacteriome appears to be a 
global feature of T1D, and our findings for four distant populations add new candidates to the 
existing list of bacteria. It remains to be established whether the observed associations are markers 
or causative factors. 
 
Keywords: 
16S rDNA sequencing; bacteriome imbalance; type 1 diabetes; Asia; Africa

Introduction 
The aetiology of type 1 diabetes (T1D) has a prominent environmental component, which has been 
documented by a rapid rise in disease incidence, both globally (reviewed e.g. in [1]) and continuously 
in many genetically stable populations [2-4]. It is assumed that this phenomenon is due to a change 
in yet-unknown environmental exposure, among which prominent candidate factors include 
inapparent and asymptomatic infections (reviewed e.g. [5]).  
 
Numerous studies on individual candidate infectious agents have been performed in settings of pre-
diabetic cohorts or T1D case-control datasets, with the aim of identifying factors that initiate islet 
autoimmunity or accelerate the pace towards overt disease. The advent of next-generation 
sequencing has offered such research an entirely new dimension via parallel investigation of all 
viruses or bacteria in a sample, usually a stool sample. 
 
The microbiome, the set of all microbes in a given sample, consists of the virome, bacteriome, and 
mycobiome. To date, two studies have been published on intestinal viromes and (pre-) T1D: one is 
our earlier study of the DIPP cohort followed up in Tampere and Oulu [6]; the other is a recent 
double-the-size study investigating children from the DIABIMMUNE study recruited in Finland and 
Estonia reporting differences in phage diversity and in the frequency of circovirus-like sequences [7]. 
More data on the bacterial profiles of stool related to (pre-) T1D are available (reviewed e.g. in [8], 
and additional works have recently been published [9, 10]). However, no two of the 16 bacteriome 
papers published to date have agreed on a bacterium or a well-delineated taxonomic group that is 
associated with T1D. Importantly, the vast majority of the studied subjects originate from Europe and 
the USA, with only three rather modestly sized studies investigating other populations - one study 
from Mexico with 21 cases and 8 controls [11] and two from China: with 15 cases and 15 controls [9], 
and 12 cases and 10 controls [12]. 
 
  
4 
 
The scientific community is currently awaiting the results of the international TEDDY study on 
environmental factors in T1D pathogenesis [13], yet even this enormously large study includes no 
individuals from populations outside Europe and the USA. Admittedly, although a certain portion of 
the data from the TEDDY study will be derived from African Americans, their genetic roots are 
geographically limited to a restricted set of several populations of West Africa [14]. Indeed, 
bacteriome and virome studies thus far have not covered the vast majority of populations of non-
Europoid origin, even though it has been demonstrated that not only bacteriomes but also viromes 
vary dramatically between distant populations differing in lifestyle [15].  
 
The infrastructure for case identification, characterization, and matching and for sample collection 
and shipment is available for many populations that differ in genetic composition, climate, dietary 
habits, lifestyle, infection exposure, and other parameters. Overall, efforts to identify microbes 
contributing to T1D development would be strongly aided by detection of a consistent microbial 
association across such populations (or a previously identified candidate from Europids). 
 
The aims of the study were to assess the association of T1D with stool bacteriome profiles in four 
geographically distant and populations of Africa and Asia.

Subjects and Methods 
Subjects 
Samples from subjects with acute-onset insulin-dependent diabetes (thus presumably T1D) were 
collected. The age limits were 3-19 years, and the collection should have taken place shortly after 
metabolic stabilization, optimally within 3-14 days after diagnosis of diabetes. Diagnosis of T1D was 
made according to World Health Organization (WHO) glycaemic criteria, proneness to ketosis, 
dependence on insulin and absence of pre-existing conditions that might have caused diabetes. 
Controls were recruited among schoolmates or friends of the patients living in the same community 
and of similar age to the case (within 10%) but not from the same nuclear family or household. One 
control subject was a minimum, but two were collected whenever possible. To match for seasonal 
patterns in virus occurrence, sampling was performed within one month of the case sampling. 
Exclusion criteria were marked obesity, a disease known to alter gut flora (chronic inflammatory 
bowel disease, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) positivity, active tuberculosis, acute fever), and 
history of antibiotics in the prodromal period of one month. 
 
A total of 73 children and adolescents with diabetes were recruited (19 from Azerbaijan, 20 from 
Jordan, 14 from Nigeria, 18 from Sudan; age at diabetes onset 11.7 years, interquartile range 7.8 -
13.7), as were 103 matched control subjects of similar place of residence and age (11.3 years, 
interquartile range 8.1 - 13.7). The study was approved by local ethics committees or review boards, 
and all subjects or their guardians expressed their consent to participate. The characteristics of the 
patients and control subjects are shown in Table 1. 
 
Stool samples - collection and transport 
Stool samples were collected using self-administered kits at the hospital during the initial 
hospitalization, at the subject’s home, or at outpatient clinics. Subjects were instructed to defecate 
into plastic bags with absorbent pads that were inserted into toilets (Toilet Bowel Liner, cat. no. ACL 
  
6 
 
9538534, Cleanis, Paris, France); the absorbent material is inert and acts to eliminate urine that 
might otherwise interfere with downstream analyses. A pea-sized piece of stool was then collected 
into a clean plastic tube using a spoon attached to the lid, and the tube was inserted into a protective 
plastic container. The tube was marked using an enclosed pen marker and frozen in a home freezer 
or immediately brought to the clinic. At the clinic, the samples were stored at -20°C or deeper until 
shipment. 
 
The samples from each country were sent in one package using a courier service. Shipment was 
performed on dry ice (from Nigeria, Jordan and Azerbaijan). Upon arrival, we verified that the 
amount of remaining dry ice was sufficient, and the samples were frozen at -80°C until processing. As 
transport on dry ice was not permitted from Khartoum (Sudan), initially frozen samples were sent in 
an insulated container with frozen pads, and the container was kept at refrigerator temperature 
wherever possible during transport; these samples were therefore delivered refrigerated but 
thawed. An internal temperature monitor was not used. The transit time of this shipment from 
Khartoum to Prague was approximately 50 hours.  
 
Genomic DNA and genotyping 
The source of genomic DNA was either anticoagulated blood or a saliva sample collected into a self-
administered kit (DNA Genotek, Ottawa, Canada). Samples were kept frozen along with the stool 
samples until shipment.  
 
DNA was used for genotyping of the HLA-DQB1 and -DQA1 genes, and genotyping three single-
nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) known to significantly alter the risk of T1D: rs689 in the INS gene (a 
substitution in the promoter region of the insulin gene, also known as the -23 HphI polymorphism); 
rs2476601 in the PTPN22 gene (a substitution of Arg for Trp at amino acid 620); and rs1990760 in the 
IFIH1 gene (a substitution of Ala for Thr at amino acid 946). DNA was extracted using the QiaAmp 
  
7 
 
DNA Blood kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) or Oragene DNA saliva collection and extraction kits (DNA 
Genotek, Ottawa, Canada). HLA-DQB1 and -DQA1 genotyping was performed at an intermediate 
resolution, with detailed resolution of diabetes-associated alleles, by in-house polymerase chain 
reaction (PCR) using sequence-specific primers as described previously [16]. The three SNPs were 
genotyped using the TaqMan SNP allelic discrimination technique (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, 
MA USA). 
 
Bacteriome profiling 
Total DNA for amplicon sequencing was extracted from unprocessed stool samples using the 
PowerSoil DNA isolation kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and microbiome profiles were determined by 
next-generation sequencing of the variable region 4 of the 16S bacterial ribosomal gene [17]. 
Samples were sequenced in duplicate, differing in their dual index combinations. Amplification was 
performed with AccuPrime Pfx SuperMix (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) and the following thermal 
protocol: initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 minutes, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 
15 seconds, primer annealing at 55°C for 30 seconds and extension at 68°C for 1 minute, all with slow 
amplification ramp rates to reduce chimera formation [18]. After purification with Ampure magnetic 
beads (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA), the products were equalized according to the results from 
quantification using the KAPA Library quantification kit (Kapa Biosystems, Wilmington, MA). A mock 
community, a mixture of bacterial DNA of a known content, was processed and sequenced as a 
control along with the samples. Sequencing was performed using a MiSeq machine with a 2x250bp 
version 2 sequencing kit (both Illumina, San Diego, CA). 
 
The bacteriome profiling sequencing data were then trimmed and filtered for quality in the Mothur 
suite [19] and processed in the Qiime suite [20]. Chimeras were removed by usearch [21]. 
Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were constructed based on similarity of 97% or higher, and 
representatives of each OTU were selected by choosing the most abundant sequence. Sequences 
  
8 
 
were identified and taxonomically classified using the Silva database [22], with subsequent manual 
curation of taxonomic identification of the 100 most frequent OTUs.  
 
Statistical analysis 
Downstream analyses were performed in the R environment [23] with the phyloseq module [24]. 
High degree of identity between duplicate sequencing of each sample was verified using principal 
component analysis, and duplicates were then merged.  
 
The relative abundances of taxonomic units in cases versus controls were compared at the level of 
phylum, class, genus, species and OTU. The OTU composition was first filtered to avoid spurious 
signals due to stochastic effects: OTUs were retained only if they were supported by more than 50 
reads in at least 2.5 % of samples; this step removed 5.5% of reads. After this filtering step, the 
median of reads per samples was 23,874 (interquartile range 20,372 - 27,024).  
 
No rarefaction was employed in the analytic process, as rarefaction has been advocated against by 
recent literature [25]. Instead, differential abundance was assessed using the DESeq2 tool [26] 
embedded through the phyloseq-to-deseq function within the Phyloseq package. The negative 
binomial multiple regression implemented in this tool also allows for utilization of the matched data 
of the study. Because some of the cases and controls from Nigeria were matched suboptimally, we 
fitted two variants of models: one that accounted for the structure of Nigerian matching pairs and 
trios and another that considered the Nigerian samples as a single case-control matching group. P 
values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method implemented 
within the DESeq2 package, and P < 0.05 was considered significant.  
 
Significant OTUs were inspected for consistency of effect across countries of origin (in particular, the 
Sudanese versus non-Sudanese samples due to the difference in transport conditions). Association 
  
9 
 
signals that lacked consistency in direction and magnitude among at least three of the four 
nationalities were removed, as were signals that were supported by less than an average of 100 
reads per sample (i.e., 0.4% of the median count of reads per sample). Prevalence plots were drawn 
for visual inspection, in which abundances were transformed to a relative scale normalized to 10,000 
reads.  
 
Alpha diversity measures were determined from the original unfiltered dataset by counting observed 
taxa (OTU richness) and by Chao1, ACE, Shannon, Simpson and Fisher indices; diversity was 
compared between cases and controls. Beta diversity was assessed by examining the results of 
principle component analysis using multiple distance methods. As no appreciable difference between 
cases and controls was noted upon visual inspection, no formal testing of clustering was performed. 
Furthermore, we attempted to reduce the complexity of the bacteriome profiles by applying Dirichlet 
multinomial mixture models [27]. This analysis simplifies the complex multi-dimensional composition 
of bacteriomes into a mixture of a limited number of components: these components then consist of 
varying proportions of individual taxa. We utilized the abundance of these Dirichlet components as 
predictors in a logistic model with two levels: one of the matching case-control group and another of 
the nationality of the subject. In a further attempt to simplify complex bacteriome profiles, we sorted 
each sample into one of the three enterotypes based on the leading components of their 
bacteriomes; the enterotype was then tested as a predictor of the case-control status in a model 
similar to that mentioned above.  
 
Deposition of sequence data 
Sequence data obtained from bacteriome profiling were deposited along with results of real-time 
PCR testing at the NCBI Sequence Reads Archive (SRA) under accession PRJNA445932.

Results 
Taxa associated with the risk of T1D 
We determined the bacterial 16S rDNA profiles of cases with T1D and age-matched controls. The 
profiles were then explored at various taxon levels for association signals using negative binomial 
regression models adjusted for the matching group. Table 2 shows the significant results of this 
analysis at the genus level, the finest meaningful resolution obtainable from 16S rDNA V4 region 
sequencing. 
 
A positive association with T1D was noted for the genus Escherichia. The mean fold difference in its 
proportion in the model between cases and controls was 1.8 (P value corrected for multiple tested 
taxa = 0.050). A further modelling with simple conditional logistic regression identified a 41% 
increase in T1D risk associated with each 10 percent point increase in relative Escherichia abundance. 
Moreover, the positive association for Escherichia propagated upwards into the levels of class and 
phylum: Gammaproteobacteria (class) and Proteobacteria (phylum) were 1.88-fold and 1.71-fold 
more abundant in cases (Pcorr = 0.024 and Pcorr = 0.054), respectively. 
 
In contrast, an inverse association with T1D was noted for two genera: Eubacterium, with a 0.64-fold 
mean difference between cases and controls (Pcorr = 0.041), and Roseburia, with a 0.55-fold 
difference in the model (Pcorr = 0.018); see Figure 1. Both association signals were also significant in 
simple conditional logistic regression models, and both propagated downwards into the level of 
species, being observed in Roseburia inulinivorans, Eubacterium hallii and Eubacterium rectale. 
 
Suggestive associations of several further taxonomic units were discarded because they did not fulfil 
the pre-set analytic criteria: mostly because the association was not consistent in at least three of the 
four populations. Further association signals were removed because the mean read count supporting 
  
11 
 
the taxon was lower than the pre-set limit (100 reads, i.e., approximately 0.4% of the mean relative 
read counts per sample).  
 
In subsequent post hoc analysis, we tested candidate genera suggested by previous literature for an 
association between bacteriome profiles and T1D, regardless of whether the taxa fulfilled our pre-set 
criteria of minimum read counts. Of these, Haemophilus showed a strongly significant inverse 
association with T1D in two of our four sample sets. Due to the uncertainty of such a putative 
association, we report the parameters in Supplementary Table 1. Further post hoc testing indicated 
a significant inverse association between T1D and taxa of Clostridium clusters IV or XIVa. A simple 
conditional logistic regression model yielded an odds ratio (OR) of 0.59 per doubling in proportions of 
these taxa within the 16S rDNA profile (uncorrected P = 0.004). 
 
Composition of diversity and of reduced dimensionality models among the four studied populations 
The overall composition of the bacteriomes by country is shown in Figure 2. Alpha diversity measures 
did not significantly differ between cases and controls. Beta diversity plots did not show any 
appreciable clustering of cases versus controls. Distance plots by country of origin revealed that the 
Sudanese samples grouped differently from the remainder of the sample set, which in addition to 
genuine differences due to nutritional or climatic variables, might be related to the extra freeze-thaw 
cycle during international transport on cold packs. Because the downstream models were all 
adjusted for country of origin and because all Sudanese samples arrived together with the case and 
control samples having been subjected to exactly the same conditions, we decided to retain these 
data in the analyses.  
 
We then constructed a Dirichlet classification model that performed best with three components 
(Supplementary Figure 1). Thus, the individual sample profiles were best modelled as mixtures of 
three theoretical bacteriomes, each comprising a given percentage of taxa. The leading genera 
  
12 
 
contributing to this classification scheme were Prevotella, Faecalibacterium, Blautia, Bifidobacterium, 
and Enterococcus, followed by several genera belonging to the class Clostridia and the genus 
Bacteroides (Supplementary Table 2). The abundance of these three virtual components within 
individual samples was not associated with diabetes in models adjusted for nationality and case-
control matching group or for nationality alone. Furthermore, the Dirichlet model fit did not improve 
upon the addition of a term for case-control status. Therefore, diabetes status was not predictable by 
modelling the bacteriome profile from reduced dimensionality components. 
 
No association with case-control status was found for enterotypes assigned to each sample, an 
assignment based on the Dirichlet component to which the sample best conformed (Supplementary 
Figure 2). Enterotype distribution differed significantly by country (Supplementary Figure 3C). As 
documented in Supplementary Figures 2 and 3C, enterotype 1 - among others - was characterized by 
a higher prevalence of Enterococcus, Romboutsia and Bifidobacterium and almost absence of 
Prevotella and a dearth of Faecalibacterium. The Sudanese samples fell exclusively into this rather 
heterogeneous enterotype, whereas the Azerbaijan samples largely lacked this enterotype. 
Enterotype 2 was characterized by an increased prevalence of Clostridiales and Bacteroides; it was 
most prevalent in Jordan. Finally, enterotype 3 was characterized by a predominance of Prevotella 
and a dearth of Blautia, Enterococcus and Romboutsia, with other taxa present in various 
proportions. Enterotype 3 was abundant among samples from Azerbaijan and Nigeria, whereas it was 
rare in Jordan and absent in Sudan. None of the enterotypes was associated with T1D. 
 
T1D genetic association testing 
As serum for beta cell autoantibody testing was not available, we could not directly confirm the type 
of diabetes among the cases. Instead, we carried out genetic association analysis to assess whether 
the association signals conformed to the presumed autoimmune origin of diabetes. The patients 
were significantly more likely to carry HLA-DQ8, -DQ2 or both alleles (P = 3x10-4 in a conditional 
  
13 
 
logistic regression model) or the 946Thr allele of IFIH1 rs1990760 (P = 0.015), whereas no significant 
negative association was apparent for HLA-DQB1*0602 positivity or for the insulin gene SNP rs689 
(Table 1). As the minor allele of PTPN22 rs2476601 was carried only by 3 individuals, formal 
association testing was not performed for this SNP.

Discussion 
There is a paucity of studies investigating the association of gut bacteriome profiles with T1D in 
children and adolescents. Here, we present results from four geographically distant African and Asian 
populations. We observed a significant association with T1D for three bacterial genera (Escherichia, 
Eubacterium and Roseburia). Furthermore, post hoc analysis confirmed additional previously 
observed association signals for Haemophilus and Clostridium clusters IV and XIVa. Within the 
background of already published data, the findings of this study reinforce the notion that there is no 
well-delineated bacterial taxon that can serve as a general bacteriome marker for T1D across 
populations or even that can be suspected of having a true pathogenic influence in the process. 
 
Associated taxa 
Escherichia was almost twofold more abundant in T1D patients than in controls. This genus was 
present in all four nationalities, without a noticeable correlation with other taxa. Escherichia is the 
best known representative of Enterobacteriaceae, class Gammaproteobacteria, and is predominantly 
hosted by mammals, more often carnivores than herbivores or omnivores [28]. Its presence in 
animals is linked to their physical proximity to humans, which - along with its favourable growth 
characteristics - makes it an indicator organism of human faecal pollution. The genus is ubiquitous in 
adults, and its colonization rates in neonates and young infants are high. Although Escherichia has 
not been associated with islet autoimmunity or T1D, it has been associated with type 2 diabetes [29] 
and body weight, albeit with inconclusive results [30, 31].  
 
Eubacterium and Roseburia, two genera within Firmicutes, class Clostridia, were found to be 
inversely associated with T1D. In a previous study by Murri et al [32], Eubacterium (or more 
specifically, a group comprising E. rectale) was shown to be more prevalent in stools of healthy 
children compared to children with diabetes; the difference was significant, with difference in copy 
counts per gram of faeces of over one decadic order of magnitude. However, the results were 
  
15 
 
obtained using SybrGreen PCR quantification, and not 16S rDNA profiling, and the total bacterial load 
per gram of stool was not reported. It is therefore impossible to directly compare those results to 
ours, which were obtained using 16S rDNA relative profiles that generally show much more modest 
distortions.  
 
The potential mechanism of the inverse association between Eubacterium and Roseburia may involve 
the production of butyrate, which has beneficial effects upon gut permeability and repair. Both gut 
strains include bacteria with an excellent capacity for butyrate production and acetate consumption 
[33]; moreover, both are saccharolytic, and their abundance has been shown to respond to the level 
of fermentable carbohydrates in food [34]. Interestingly, abundance of E. hallii increases after 
successful therapeutic transfer of microbiota from lean to obese individuals [35], and its 
administration to insulin-resistant mice can enhance insulin sensitivity [36].  
 
Thus, evidence to date seems to favour the role of the three above genera as markers of 
hyperglycaemia or diabetes of any type, rather than as a causative factor for islet autoimmunity 
persisting beyond T1D onset. 
 
Enterotypes are not associated with T1D 
We attempted to reduce the dimensionality of the bacterial profile space by Dirichlet classification 
modelling and then further by assigning an 'enterotype' to each sample based on the leading 
component. In accord with what had been suggested previously [37-39], such simplification was not 
a viable alternative to analysis of individual taxa, as it clearly could not substitute for the rich 
dimensionality of the bacteriome space. In fact, visual inspection of heat maps characterizing 
individual bacteriome profiles revealed that simplification into three classes is imprecise because the 
most abundant leading genera of each type were not followed by a consistent pattern of less 
  
16 
 
prevalent ones (Supplementary Figure 2). Consequently, T1D associations could not be detected for 
Dirichlet components or for enterotypes.  
 
Previous literature from Europid populations 
Only a limited number of studies have explored bacterial profiles in newly diagnosed or established 
T1D patients, and similar to our work, most of these studies collected only one sample per subject. 
The largest study of this type is from Denver, CO, in which stool bacterial profiles of 35 children and 
young persons with diabetes (up to six months after diagnosis) were compared to those of 
unmatched controls [40]. Those results showed that genera Alistipes, Staphylococcus and 
Thalassospira as well as a group of unclassified Bacteroidetes were differentially abundant in T1D 
patients compared to controls. Although none of these genera was found to be associated in our 
primary analysis, upon post hoc testing, we did observe an isolated positive association between 
Staphylococcus and T1D, which was restricted to the Sudanese sample set (Supplementary Table 1). 
As the original observation in the Denver case-control group yielded an inverse association and our 
association is seemingly driven only by several low-positive Sudanese cases, the result should be 
interpreted with utmost caution. 
 
With regard to the number of subjects, the second largest to date was the joint analysis of DIPP and 
VirDiab sample sets with 28 very young case subjects of international origin (predominantly Finnish) 
and 27 age-matched controls who were almost exclusively Finnish [41]. This study provided an 
extremely interesting view of the evolving gut microbiome before and after its critical maturation 
point at the age of three years. A complex pattern of differences among the microbiota was found in 
that study: the chief finding was that in younger children, Clostridium clusters IV and XIVa displayed 
an inverse association with T1D, whereas this association dissipated in children over the age cut-off 
of 2.9 years. Interestingly, we also observed an inverse association of these clusters with T1D, though 
our subjects were considerably older. This post hoc test result should also be interpreted with 
  
17 
 
caution because the clusters are classified using a scheme from a recent work by Rajilic-Stojanovic 
[42] whose categorization may be different from the previous scheme used by de Goffau et al.  
 
The only study systematically collecting repeated stool samples along the progression towards T1D 
involves the DIABIMMUNE cohort in Finland and Estonia [43]. In this study, the authors followed up 
on 11 patients with islet autoimmunity, of whom three progressed to T1D, in an effort to identify 
factors associated with progression. The use of longitudinal serial samples was an important strength 
of this study, as it enabled the authors to demonstrate a marked flattening in alpha diversity trends 
after islet autoimmunity in subjects who later progressed to T1D; this was in contrast to both islet 
autoantibody-negative subjects and non-progressors among seroconvertors. The authors interpret 
this drop in diversity as a marker of dysbiosis and ascribe it to relative overabundance of Blautia, 
Rikenellaceae, Ruminococcus and Streptococcus. However, in our study, none of the mentioned taxa 
showed significant tendencies towards associations with T1D, and no differences were noted in 
indices of richness or evenness.  
 
Two other studies have also investigated bacterial composition of stools, though utilizing methods 
other than 16S rDNA profiling or metagenomic sequencing. The first is a from Spain, in which 16 
established cases of T1D and 16 controls were tested [32] using a combination of denaturing gradient 
gel electrophoresis, band cloning with sequencing, and 13 phylum- or genus-specific real-time PCR. 
The findings demonstrated a difference in the microbial composition of stool between children with 
diabetes and controls, defining seven significant genera or genus-like groups. As already mentioned, 
comparison of their results to our 16S rDNA profiling is impossible due to technical reasons. The 
second study is from Turkey, and the authors compared a collection of stools from 35 newly 
diagnosed cases versus 35 controls using quantitative culture methods [44], finding a decrease in 
Bifidobacteria and an increase in non-E. coli Enterobacteriaceae and Candida in T1D compared to 
controls. A potential weakness is that the samples were not analysed on site but were sent from 
  
18 
 
Turkey to Luxembourg for quantitative culture; moreover, the authors did not elaborate on 
comparisons of transit times or temperature conditions among samples individually collected over 
the study period of eight months. 
 
Previous non-Europid studies 
Three studies from non-Europid populations have also been published. In a modestly sized matched 
case-control study from China [9], Qi et al found lower richness in faecal samples from 15 diabetic 
children compared to 15 control and defined an association of T1D with five genera: Blautia, 
Haemophilus, Lachnospira, Dialister and Acidaminococcus. In our dataset, Blautia, a prevalent 
anaerobe previously linked to various medical conditions, did not show any tendency for an 
association with T1D in any of our four sample subsets. Due to its low abundance, the genus 
Haemophilus (family Pasteurellaceae, order Pasteurellales, class Gammaproteobacteria) did not pass 
the filter in our primary analysis. In subsequent unplanned post hoc testing, we observed a relatively 
strong association in two of our four subsets (Supplementary Table 1), but the dearth of this taxon 
still raises doubt regarding generalizability. Neither Lachnospira nor Dialister were associated with 
T1D in our dataset. Lastly, Acidaminococcus was very rare in the study by Qi et al, who observed it 
only in controls, with an extremely low mean read count of nine (i.e., presumed ~135 reads in the 
entire study, as recalculated from the figures in the paper; raw data have not be deposited). 
Analysing taxa of very low abundance is generally fraught with difficulties, especially when 
electrophoresis gel extraction is used within the sequencing pipeline (as it was in the above 
mentioned study) because a very high risk of cross-contamination occurs among samples analysed in 
parallel.  
 
In a recent study in 12 Han Chinese adult patients with T1D and 10 controls, Huang et al [12] 
identified a dramatic difference among the stool bacteriomes - patients' profiles were clearly 
dominated by phylum Bacteroidetes whereas stools of control subjects were rich in Firmicutes. 
  
19 
 
Differences were observed also at lower taxonomic levels. Interestingly, the authors identified 
associations of bacterial phyla also with gender, BMI, and serum lipid levels. This extent and 
magnitude of bacteriome association is exceptional among so far published studies: the previous 
study in Han Chinese, cited above, observed no differences at the phylum level [9]. A direct 
comparison is however impossible, as no link to a data repository has been provided.  
 
From the State of Sonora, Mexico [11], another non-Europid study compared bacteriome profiles 
from 29 new or established T1D cases to 8 controls. The subjects were described as Mestizos of 
Sonoran ancestry. The authors evaluated phyla and genera with relative abundances over 1%, finding 
more Bacteroides but fewer Prevotella, Megamonas and Acidaminococcus bacteria in cases versus 
controls. In our dataset, no tendency towards association was found for any of these taxa, with 
Bacteroides or Prevotella being abundant genera and Megamonas relatively rare. 
 
Possible interpretations of differences in association signals 
Comparing our results to what has been published previously from Europids and non-European 
populations reveals the enormous heterogeneity in findings. Three scenarios might explain this lack 
of consistency. First, a microbial agent associated with T1D may not exist. In such a case, most 
associations observed thus far - including ours - may be due to improperly handled confounders, 
insufficient sample collection schemes, still-underdeveloped methods of molecular profiling and/or 
imperfections in presently available strategies for statistical analysis. Within this context, 
independent reanalyses of previous studies might be helpful but may be complicated by the general 
lack of sequencing data in public databases. Second, T1D may be associated with a microbial function 
or metabolite, rather than with a taxon. Such a function may be assumed by different taxa depending 
on the individual clinical status and on the epidemiological, climatic, and nutritional characteristics of 
the underlying population. In such a case, microbial profiling would be useless, and only expensive 
and complicated methods of bacterial metagenomics might be useful. Third, studies to date may 
  
20 
 
have attempted to identify microbial signals from an unsuitable source: the large intestine and stool 
microbiome may be irrelevant for assessing the microbial contents of more important, yet poorly 
accessible, parts of the gut, e.g., the duodenum or small intestine. 
 
Strengths of the study 
Our analysis is based on a synergistic evaluation of matched sample sets from four geographically, 
climatically, nutritionally and culturally different populations on two continents. The distance 
separating these populations is exemplified by differences in the composition of their stool 
bacteriome profiles, which well illustrates that the bacteriome is indeed influenced by the above-
mentioned differences [45]. Any taxon consistently associated with T1D across these distant 
populations has a considerable likelihood of representing a genuine underlying biological 
phenomenon.  
 
The strength of the study design is also in the matching strategy. We attempted to match cases to 
age-related controls, which should help to eliminate spurious signals due to the evolution of the 
bacteriome during childhood and young adulthood as well as local differences in dietary habits. 
Nonetheless, to avoid overmatching, we did not recruit controls residing in the same household, as 
bacteriomes are known to be influenced by close human relationships [45]. 
 
Finally, our analytical approach was rather conservative: only statistically significant findings 
supported by the same direction of association in three of the four studied populations in a negative 
binomial regression model and further confirmed by simple conditional logistic regression with taxon 
proportion as a predictor are reported. The validity of our conclusions may also be reinforced by the 
fact that the present study investigated the highest number of T1D cases published thus far. 
 
Limitations and their remedies 
  
21 
 
The first limitation is linked to the transport of Sudanese samples on cooling packs rather than on dry 
ice. However, data published from controlled experiments suggest only minimal shifts in bacteriome 
profiles upon prolonged times after collection before the sample is frozen [46]. A situation similar to 
ours was anecdotally described by Knip et Siljander, who showed distortion in genera Granulicatella 
and Clostridium after unintentional thawing of samples during transport [8]. In our study, immediate 
treatment of collected samples with a preservative solution was not a viable option, as our in-house 
tests indicated that proper mixing is not achievable outside a well equipped laboratory, which is in 
accordance with what has been observed previously [47]. Bearing in mind that the bacterial 
populations from Sudan might be mildly shifted, we paid special attention to consistency in the 
direction of association during our statistical analyses. 
 
Second, difficulties were noted in obtaining matched controls from Nigeria. Our study commenced 
shortly after the Nigerian outbreak of Ebola virus had been contained [48]. This potentially 
catastrophic outbreak received a wide publicity, and consequently the willingness of lay persons to 
participate in our study was limited: the participants perceived that the study protocol alluded to an 
infectious origin of diabetes, with associated negative connotations. Hence, the criteria for 
recruitment and matching had to be relaxed. Furthermore, several individuals withdrew, leaving 
unmatched counterparts. We therefore recruited several Nigerian patients who did not have a recent 
diagnosis of diabetes, and we then needed to reorganize the matching groups to allow for 
withdrawals. We also performed the statistical analyses using the Nigerian sample set as one large 
matching group, with no appreciable changes in the direction or magnitude of the results. 
 
Third, no serum was available for autoantibody testing because of difficulties in securing its 
centrifugal separation and storage. The diagnosis of T1D therefore relied upon dependency on insulin 
and proneness to ketosis - paediatric diabetes in Africa is almost always insulin dependent and 
autoimmune [49]. We attempted to verify the gross composition of the case group by assessing 
  
22 
 
genetic markers of T1D, namely polymorphisms in HLA-DQ, the insulin gene, IFIH1 and PTPN22. The 
strong observed genetic association with HLA-DQ and the borderline finding for IFIH1 indicated that 
the majority of the case subjects indeed had type 1 disease. The reasons for the absence of an 
inverse association with the insulin gene and HLA-DQB1*06:02 merit further attention. The ongoing 
HLA typing project in Africa and Asia with samples collected under the auspices of the Life for a Child 
insulin provision programme has hinted that such an attenuation of HLA-DRB1*15:01-DQB1*06:02-
linked protection may be a genuine phenomenon in certain non-Europid populations (published as 
an abstract [50]). 
 
Finally, the current study design cannot resolve whether the observed associations of taxa with T1D 
are a cause or a consequence of newly manifested diabetes. Bacteriome alterations may indeed be 
an effect of increased glycaemia, of its excursions, or even of dietary changes following diagnosis. 
Conducting longitudinal prospective studies would be unconceivable at present and unethical in 
some of the diabetes centres participating in this study, as it would not address the major 
shortcomings in clinical care and would unnecessarily burden patients.  
 
Conclusions 
By examining four independent case-control datasets from distant populations of Africa and Asia, we 
herein demonstrate an association with T1D for three bacterial genera in stool bacteriomes, and 
confirm a previously observed association with Clostridium clusters IV or XIVa. In the context of 
previously published works, this may indicate that although primary or secondary imbalance in the 
bacteriome is a general feature of T1D, its type and extent vary.
