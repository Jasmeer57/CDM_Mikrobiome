Abstract 
20 
The biological succession that occurs in the gut of infants inhabiting Western 
21 
countries during the first year of life is broadly predictable in terms of the increasing 
22 
complexity of microbiota composition. Less information is available about 
23 
microbiotas in Asian countries where environmental, nutritional and cultural 
24 
influences may differentially affect the composition and development of the microbial 
25 
community. We compared the fecal microbiotas of Indonesian (n = 204) and New 
26 
Zealand (NZ; n = 74) infants aged 6-7 months and 12 months. Comparisons were 
27 
made by analysis of 16S rRNA gene sequences, and associated derivation of 
28 
community diversity metrics, relative abundances of bacterial families, detection of 
29 
enterotypes, 
and 
co-occurrence 
correlation 
networks. 
Abundances 
of 
30 
Bifidobacterium longum subspecies infantis and longum were made by quantitative 
31 
PCR. All observations supported the view that the Indonesian and NZ infant 
32 
microbiotas developed in complexity over time, but the changes were much greater 
33 
in NZ infants. B. longum subsp. infantis dominated the microbiota of Indonesian 
34 
children whereas subsp. longum was dominant in NZ children. Network analysis 
35 
showed that the niche model (trophic adaptation results in preferential colonization) 
36 
of microbiota assemblage was supported in Indonesian infants, whereas the neutral 
37 
(stochastic) model was supported by the development of the microbiota of NZ infants. 
38 
The results of the study show that the development of the fecal microbiota is not the 
39 
same for infants in all countries and points to the necessity of obtaining a better 
40 
understanding of the factors that control the colonization of the gut in early life.

Importance.  
44 
This study addresses the microbiology of a natural ecosystem (the infant bowel) 
45 
associated with children in a rural setting in Indonesia and an urban environment in 
46 
New Zealand. Analysis of DNA sequences generated from the microbial community 
47 
(microbiota) in the feces of the infants during the first year of life showed marked 
48 
differences in the composition and complexity of the bacterial collections. The 
49 
differences were most likely due to differences in the prevalence and duration of 
50 
breastfeeding of infants in the two countries. These kinds of studies are essential in 
51 
developing concepts of microbial ecology that relate to the influence of nutrition and 
52 
environment on the development of the gut microbiota and in determining the long-
53 
term effects of microbiological events in early life on human health and well-being.

Introduction 
56 
 
57 
The transition of an infant from a mainly milk diet to the introduction of solid foods 
58 
(complementary feeding period) is accompanied by transformation of the 
59 
composition of the gut microbiota (1-8). It is recommended in Western countries that 
60 
complementary feeding starts at around 6 months of age, and that infants are eating 
61 
mainly “family” foods by one year (9-11). In addition to diet, environmental (water, 
62 
sanitation, hygiene, air pollution) and lifestyle factors differ among countries and their 
63 
populations. These differences may influence the gut microbiota during its 
64 
development towards a microbial community more similar to that of adults (12). In 
65 
general, in studies of Western infants, members of the genus Bifidobacterium that 
66 
utilize lactose and, for some species, Human Milk Oligosaccharides (HMOs), for 
67 
growth are abundant in the fecal microbiota of exclusively milk-fed infants. Bacterial 
68 
species capable of degrading and fermenting plant polysaccharides and their 
69 
components (Bacteroidaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae) are dominant 
70 
by the end of the complementary feeding period (1-8).  
71 
 
Knowledge of the development of the gut microbiota in early life is important 
72 
because deviations from the norm (dysbiosis) may be associated with adverse 
73 
conditions later in life (13, 14, 15). Defining the normal situation for infants in different 
74 
countries therefore has value as concepts of the involvement of the gut microbiota in 
75 
the etiology of specific diseases and conditions develop (16). 
76 
 
Descriptions of the fecal microbiota commonly rely on culture-independent 
77 
analysis that uses 16S rRNA gene sequences amplified by PCR from bulk DNA 
78 
extracted from feces. Analysis of these sequences confidently provides taxonomic 
79 
information about microbiota composition with respect to bacterial families and

83

85

89 
 
The purpose of our study was to compare the fecal microbiota composition of 
90 
infants in Indonesia and New Zealand (NZ) during the complementary feeding period, 
91 
using 16S rRNA gene sequences obtained by next-generation-sequencing, and 
92 
qPCR targeting bifidobacterial species and subspecies. The Indonesian infants were 
93 
from rural villages, whereas the NZ children were urban and predominantly NZ 
94 
European (23-25). We found that microbiota composition changed radically in NZ 
95 
children during the first 12 months of life, whereas bifidobacteria, particularly 
96 
Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis, remained at high abundance in Indonesian 
97 
infants during this time.

Co-occurrence networks in the fecal microbiota of Indonesian and NZ infants. 
155 
Construction of microbial (correlation) networks from sequencing data may assist in 
156 
understanding community structure. The networks show individual microbes (ASVs 
157 
or features) as nodes (hub species) and species co-occurrence or mutual exclusion 
158 
as feature-feature pairs (edges) where an edge may imply a biological or 
159 
biochemical relationship between features. Microbes that benefit one-another may 
160 
positively correlate, whereas microbes that compete for the same niche may 
161 
negatively correlate (28, 29).  
162 
Constructing correlation networks from our data underlined the different 
163 
development of microbiotas of Indonesian infants relative to NZ infants. Indonesian 
164 
networks displayed an increase in node numbers between 6 and 12 months of age 
165 
(28 versus 46) and a 1.5 fold increase in edge numbers (48 versus 74). There was 
166 
also an increase in the proportion of positive correlations at 12 months (67% at 6 
167 
months; 81% at 12 months) (Figure 6 A, B). In contrast, the NZ 12 month network 
168 
was much more complex with a greater number of nodes observed at 12 months 
169

170

171

172

Discussion. 
193 
Comparison of the development of the fecal microbiota of Indonesian and NZ infants 
194 
during the first year of life showed clear differences in the composition and 
195 
complexity of the bacterial communities. Enterotypes dominated by B. longum/breve 
196 
(in fact B. longum subspecies infantis as shown by qPCR) were common in 
197 
Indonesian infants at both 6 and 12 months of age, whereas a third enterotype was 
198 
common in the NZ infant microbiota at 12 months. This enterotype was not 
199 
dominated by B. longum/breve.

The processes that underpin the assembly of communities in natural systems 
201 
are of much interest to ecologists. Two contrasting models to explain the 
202 
development of diversity in communities have been proposed: the niche selection 
203 
and neutral theories of ecology (41-44). The dynamics of the niche selection model, 
204 
it is proposed, are dominated by resource selection where species of particular 
205 
trophic adaptations are preferential colonizers of the habitat. Resource sharing of the 
206 
habitat’s resources might result in interactions of mutual benefit that will result in the 
207 
development of a stable community (45-47). In contrast, the dynamics of the neutral 
208 
model are stochastic and imply that species that assemble are functionally 
209 
equivalent, so competition will be a feature of community assembly.  
210 
The gut microbiota of Indonesian children favors the niche model because co-
211 
occurrence networks at 6 and 12 months have similar numbers of nodes and most of 
212 
the correlations between nodes were positive (co-presence edges). This suggests a 
213 
relatively stable environment with a direct reliance on environmental resources by 
214 
bacteria with special trophic adaptations that permit co-habitation (for example 
215 
differential utilization of HMOs for growth; 22, 39, 45). In contrast, NZ microbiotas at 
216 
12 months were competitive (high proportion of mutual exclusion edges) within a 
217 
much more complex microbiota (more nodes). This suggests that acquisition of 
218 
bacterial species was more random (neutral theory of assemblage) and involved an 
219 
assortment of competing bacteria with similar trophic requirements (for example 
220 
utilization of dietary fibre).  
221 
 
Niche selection by HMOs is highly likely to explain the situation in Indonesian 
222 
infants. HMOs and their components are used by B. longum subspecies infantis, 
223 
following transport into the cell, for growth. B. longum subspecies infantis has a 
224

225

226 
HMO components (22). The extent of breast milk feeding is different in Indonesia 
227 
and NZ. While initial breast milk feeding uptake is high in both countries (~100%), 
228 
rates fall to a national average of 65% of infants by 6 months after birth in NZ and 
229

230

231

232

233

234 
which is substantially lower by 6 months of age in both countries because 
235 
complementary feeding needs to start by this age.  
236 
The composition of breast milk has been reported to differ between mothers in 
237 
different countries, and even between mothers in different locations in the same 
238 
country (50-56). Comparisons of HMO content and other biochemistry of Indonesian 
239 
and NZ breast milks have not been made so may therefore be a further topic worth 
240 
investigating. Additionally, volumes of milk consumed daily by infants in different 
241 
settings would provide useful information in considering the relationship between 
242 
microbiota and infant nutrition.

Evidence from studies of the fecal microbiotas of twins, and of co-habiting 
244 
adults and children, indicate the potential importance of shared environments and 
245 
similar diets in the assembly and maintenance of the microbiota of the human body 
246 
(57-59). Shared environments provided by communal living, such as in rural 
247 
Indonesia, might result in greater ease of dispersal (horizontal transmission) of gut

249 
Dispersal theory (60-63) has been advocated to explain the differing diversity, as 
250 
well as greater similarity, of microbiotas in non-industrialized populations (for 
251 
example, rural Papua-New Guinea; 12) relative to Western microbiotas. Easier 
252 
dispersal of gut microbes in a rural village setting might favor the acquisition of B. 
253 
longum subsp. infantis whereas Western methods of sanitation, water treatment, and 
254 
hygiene may be inimical to this organism. Although B. longum subsp. infantis was 
255 
detectable in the microbiotas of some NZ infants, it did not dominate the bacterial 
256 
community as was the case for Indonesian children. Thus B. longum subsp. infantis 
257 
is not an extinct lineage in NZ microbiotas but is most likely to have been influenced 
258 
by lack of enrichment by HMOs in the infant gut due to breastfeeding practices. At 
259 
least in the case of adults, studies of fecal microbiotas in industrialized countries 
260 
have not shown major differences between nationalities (such as Japan, Italy, Spain, 
261 
Denmark, France, USA), indicating that genetic factors probably have only minor 
262 
influences on microbiota composition (26).  
263 
Overall, the transition from infant to more adult-like microbiota during the first 
264 
year of life was more pronounced in NZ compared to Indonesian infants. 
265 
Bifidobacteria are common in infants from both countries when aged 6-7 months. 
266 
However, NZ infants have a complex microbiota, dominated by an enterotype 
267 
different than that predominating in Indonesian infants at 12 months of age. The 
268 
outcomes of the study show that the development of the fecal microbiota is not the 
269 
same for infants in all countries and points to the necessity of obtaining a better 
270 
understanding of the factors that control the colonization of the gut in early life. Such 
271 
knowledge is important because early life influences due to the microbiota may have

Fecal DNA extraction, 16S rRNA gene sequencing, and sequence analysis. 
307 
DNA was extracted from 250 mg feces according to the kit protocol provided by the 
308
