Abstract 
34 
Centenarians, or individuals who have lived more than a century, represent the ultimate 
35 
model of successful longevity associated with decreased susceptibility to ageing-associated 
36 
illness and chronic inflammation1–3. The gut microbiota is considered to be a critical 
37 
determinant of human health and longevity4–8. Here we show that centenarians (average 107 
38 
yo) have a distinct gut microbiome enriched in microbes capable of generating unique 
39 
secondary bile acids, including iso-, 3-oxo-, and isoallo-lithocholic acid (LCA), as compared to 
40 
elderly (85-89 yo) and young (21-55 yo) controls. Among these bile acids, the biosynthetic 
41 
pathway for isoalloLCA had not been described previously. By screening 68 bacterial isolates 
42 
from a centenarian’s faecal microbiota, we identified Parabacteroides merdae and 
43 
Odoribacteraceae strains as effective producers of isoalloLCA. Furthermore, we generated and 
44 
tested mutant strains of P. merdae to show that the enzymes 5a-reductase (5AR) and 3b-
45 
hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (3bHSDH) were responsible for isoalloLCA production. This 
46 
secondary bile acid derivative exerted the most potent antimicrobial effects among the tested 
47 
bile acid compounds against gram-positive (but not gram-negative) multidrug-resistant 
48 
pathogens, including Clostridioides difficile and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium. 
49 
These findings suggest that specific bile acid metabolism may be involved in reducing the risk 
50 
of pathobiont infection, thereby potentially contributing to longevity. 
51 
 
52 
Main 
53 
The microbiome has long been recognized as a key player in determining the health status of ageing 
54 
individuals through its role in controlling digestive functions, bone density, neuronal activity, 
55 
immunity, and resistance to pathogen infection9–13. Microbial consortia in elderly individuals often 
56 
show increased interindividual variability and reduced diversity, and are thus being linked to 
57 
immunosenescence, chronic systemic inflammation, and frailty6,14. An integrated understanding of 
58 
the dynamic balance and functions of microbial members with respect to ageing is essential for 
59 
establishing a strategy toward rational manipulation of the microbiota for restoring and/or 
60 
maintaining tissue homeostasis and overall health. 
61 
Centenarians (aged 100 years and older) are known to be less susceptible to age-related 
62 
diseases including hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and cancer3,15. Moreover, centenarians have likely 
63 
survived periods of hunger and several bouts with infectious diseases such as influenza, tuberculosis, 
64 
shigellosis, and salmonellosis16. It has been postulated that there are centenarian-specific members of 
65 
the gut microbiota which, rather than representing a mere consequence of ageing, might actively 
66 
contribute to maintaining homeostasis, resilience, and healthful ageing4–6,8. In this study, we aimed 
67 

 
 3
to identify symbiotic, beneficial bacteria in the gut microbiota of centenarians that may contribute to 
68 
resistance to pathogen infection and other environmental stresses. 
69 
 
70 
Microbiome signature of centenarians 
71 
 
We recruited a cohort consisting of three age groups: centenarian (n = 160), elderly (n = 112), 
72 
and young (n = 44). All centenarians were recruited as part of the Japan Semi-supercentenarian 
73 
Study15, with most living in nursing homes (85.0%) and the remainder at home (9.4%) or in hospitals 
74 
(5.6%) (Table S1). Centenarians generally reported reduced activities of daily living (ADL) and 
75 
mini-mental state examination (MMSE) scores, along with reduced red blood cell counts and serum 
76 
albumin (Extended Data Fig. 1a-c and Table S1). Consistent with the paradigm that ageing is 
77 
accompanied by chronic inflammation secondary to decreased barrier integrity and 
78 
immunosenescence9,12,13, a subset of centenarians showed signs of low-grade inflammation as 
79 
evidenced by elevated serum C-reactive protein and faecal lipocalin (Extended Data Fig. 1c, d). 
80 
Nevertheless, the majority of centenarians were free of chronic diseases such as obesity, diabetes, 
81 
hypertension, and cancer, and the prevalence of these diseases was not significantly increased as 
82 
compared to the elderly group (Extended Data Fig. 1e, f and Table S1). We collected faecal samples 
83 
from the three groups to characterize the microbiome by both 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) amplicon 
84 
and whole metagenome shotgun sequencing. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray-
85 
Curtis distance revealed significant differences in microbiota composition between centenarians and 
86 
both control groups (PERMANOVA FDR P < 0.05, Fig. 1a). At the phylum level, we observed a 
87 
significant enrichment of Proteobacteria and Synergistetes, a moderate enrichment of 
88 
Verrucomicrobia, and a depletion of Actinobacteria in centenarians as compared to controls (Fig. 1c 
89 
and Extended Data Fig. 2), partially in agreement with previous centenarian studies including that 
90 
of the Sardinian cohort8. Such expansions of Proteobacteria are a frequent finding in patients with 
91 
inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)17; however, in contrast to the reduced microbial a-diversity 
92 
commonly observed in IBD patients, centenarians had on average a higher Shannon diversity index 
93 
compared to young controls (Fig. 1b). Moreover, the microbiota composition of centenarians was 
94 
distinct from that of IBD patients, as evidenced by differential clustering in PCoA analyses 
95 
(Extended Data Fig. 3a). 
96 
Several taxa displayed differential relative abundances in centenarians versus control groups 
97 
(Fig. 1d-f and Extended Data Fig. 3b-d), which we categorized into three signatures based on 
98 
trajectory with age: (i) The first signature included taxa whose abundance was increased or decreased 
99 
with age (Fig. 1d). For example, Eubacterium siraeum and undefined Firmicutes species (msp_161, 
100 
213) were most abundant in centenarians, followed by the elderly and then the young controls, 
101 

 
 4
whereas Blautia wexlerae displayed the opposite trend, being most abundant in young controls, 
102 
followed by the elderly and finally the centenarians. Alistipes shahii was comparably enriched in both 
103 
the elderly and centenarian groups as compared to young controls. These findings are in alignment 
104 
with previous studies that suggest the relative abundances of these taxa reflect adaptation to ageing, 
105 
and may be related to physical activity, environment, and diet4–6,8. (ii) The second signature included 
106 
taxa whose abundance was similar in centenarians and young controls, but distinct from the elderly 
107 
(Fig. 1e). These species might reflect the maintenance of youth or possess reverse-ageing effects. 
108 
Notably, Ruminococcus gnavus and Eggerthella lenta were part of this signature, as they were 
109 
comparably abundant in both centenarians and young controls. Interestingly, these species have been 
110 
implicated in bile acid metabolism, and likely participate in the biosynthesis of iso-bile acids in the 
111 
host gut18. (iii) The third signature included centenarian-specific taxa whose abundance was 
112 
significantly different between centenarians and both the elderly and young control groups, but not 
113 
between these two control groups (Fig. 1f). Here, Alistipes, Parabacteroides, Bacteroides, and 
114 
Clostridium species, as well as Methanobrevibacter, a predominant archaeon in the human gut, were 
115 
specifically enriched in centenarians as compared to the other groups. One of the most abundant 
116 
species in centenarians was Clostridium scindens, which is known to possess the relatively rare 7a-
117 
dehydroxylation capacity needed to convert primary into secondary bile acids19,20. In contrast, key 
118 
butyrate producers such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Eubacterium rectale, and Roseburia 
119 
intestinalis were selectively depleted in centenarians (Fig. 1f). Some of these observations are in 
120 
agreement with the Sardinian study, in which centenarians exhibited a decreased relative abundance 
121 
of F. prausnitzii and E. rectale, and an increase in M. smithii8. 
122 
We also collected stool from the lineal descendants and siblings of centenarians and analysed 
123 
them by 16S rRNA sequencing (n = 22 from 14 centenarians, 48-95 yo, Extended Data Fig. 3 and 
124 
Table S1). Some bacterial species, such as Phascolarctobacterium faecium and Alistipes putredinis, 
125 
were more abundant in centenarians and their family members as compared to the other groups 
126 
(Extended Data Fig. 3d). Enrichment of these taxa in centenarians and their lineal descendants may 
127 
be due to host genetics, lifestyle, and diet; for example, consumption of cruciferous vegetables has 
128 
been reported to favour expansion of the aforementioned taxa21. 
129 
 
130 
Centenarians have a unique bile acid profile 
131 
We next assessed the faecal metabolite profile of centenarians as compared to elderly and 
132 
young controls. We first analysed faecal short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and found decreased levels 
133 
of both propionic and butyric acid in centenarians (Extended Data Fig. 4a). In contrast, branched-
134 
SCFAs like isobutyric and isovaleric acid, as well as ammonium, were elevated in centenarians 
135 

 
 5
(Extended Data Fig. 4a-b). These metabolic alterations are consistent with previous observations4,5,8 
136 
and may be attributable to the simultaneous depletion of SCFA-producers, such as R. intestinalis and 
137 
F. prausnitzii22 (Fig. 1f), and enrichment of protein-fermenting organisms, such as A. putredinis23 
138 
(Fig. 1f). This increase in amino acid-utilizing bacteria is likely a consequence of the reduced upper 
139 
intestinal proteolytic capacity commonly observed in centenarians. Moreover, faecal pH was 
140 
significantly higher in centenarians than controls (Extended Data Fig. 4c), which may be due in part 
141 
to the lower SCFA concentrations and reduced gastric juice production characteristic of ageing. 
142 
Given the enrichment of species potentially capable of metabolizing bile acids in centenarians, 
143 
we next focused on faecal bile acid distribution. Primary bile acids are synthesized from cholesterol 
144 
in the liver, conjugated to either glycine or taurine, and secreted into bile24,25. These primary bile 
145 
acids are then deconjugated and biotransformed into a variety of secondary bile acids by the gut 
146 
microbiota19,25. The predominant biotransformation is the 7a-dehydroxylation of primary bile acids 
147 
[cholic acid (CA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA)], thereby converting them into secondary bile 
148 
acids [deoxycholic acid (DCA) and lithocholic acid (LCA)]. Microbiota-mediated bile acid 
149 
metabolism consists of multiple redox reactions catalysed by enzymes encoded by bile-acid-inducible 
150 
(bai) operon genes: BaiB, BaiCD, BaiA2, BaiE, BaiF, and BaiH19,26 (Extended Data Fig. 5a). In 
151 
addition to the 7a-dehydroxylation, bile acids can undergo oxidation and epimerization to generate 
152 
oxo- (keto-), iso- (3b-hydroxy), allo- (5a-H-), as well as cis- and trans-forms25 (Extended Data Fig. 
153 
5a). Metagenomic analysis of our cohorts identified an increase in the relative abundance of bai 
154 
operon gene homologues in centenarians (Fig. 1g), though this trend was not apparent in the Sardinian 
155 
cohort (Extended Data Fig. 6). 
156 
To characterize the bile acid profile of centenarians, we implemented a highly sensitive, 
157 
targeted liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method. In pilot studies, 
158 
we found that 95 of 132 examined bile acids were minor components (<0.5 µmol/g) of centenarians’ 
159 
faeces. We thus selected the remaining 37 relevant bile acid compounds for follow-up quantitative 
160 
analysis (Fig. 2a and Table S2). Although total bile acid load in filtered, weight-normalized stool 
161 
suspensions was not significantly different between groups, centenarians showed a unique 
162 
distribution of faecal bile acids (Fig. 2a-c and Extended Data Fig. 7). For example, centenarians 
163 
exhibited lower levels of primary bile acids with increased levels of CDCA metabolites (Fig. 2d-f). 
164 
In particular, the levels of isoLCA, 3-oxoLCA, and isoalloLCA were significantly elevated in 
165 
centenarians, whereas they were comparably low in the elderly and young control groups, suggesting 
166 
that this enrichment is not simply a byproduct of ageing (Fig. 2a, f). Furthermore, the concentrations 
167 
of isoLCA, 3-oxoLCA, and isoalloLCA were positively associated with faecal pH (Extended Data 
168 
Fig. 4d), potentially implying that their enrichment in centenarians may reflect changes in diet and 
169 

 
 6
digestive function, as well as consequent changes in the intestinal luminal metabolome. Such an 
170 
intestinal milieu may promote the expansion of certain bacterial species and/or the expression of 
171 
enzymes involved in the production of isoLCA, 3-oxoLCA, and isoalloLCA. Alternatively, intestinal 
172 
colonization by isoLCA-, 3-oxoLCA-, and isoalloLCA-producing bacteria may causally affect other 
173 
members of the gut microbiota and their metabolic processes.

Identification of isoLCA-, 3-oxoLCA-, and isoalloLCA-producing bacterial strains 
176 
We set out to identify bacterial strains and enzymes responsible for the biosynthesis of 
177 
isoLCA, 3-oxoLCA, and isoalloLCA in centenarians’ microbiota. A previous report demonstrated 
178 
that 3-oxoDCA can be generated from 3-oxo-D4-DCA (also termed 3-oxo-4,5-dehydro-DCA) by 
179 
hydrogenation across the C4-C5 double bond such that the C5 hydrogen is in the b position 
180 
(Extended Data Fig. 5b). This reaction is mediated by a 3-oxo-5b-steroid 4-dehydrogenase (also 
181 
termed 5b-reductase, 5BR) encoded by the BaiCD gene, which is reported to be carried by a small 
182 
number of Clostridium species including C. scindens and C. hylemonae26,27. It has additionally been 
183 
reported that E. lenta and R. gnavas can generate 3-oxoDCA from DCA, and isoDCA from 3-
184 
oxoDCA, by the actions of 3a-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase (3aHSDH) and 3bHSDH, 
185 
respectively18. Thus, we hypothesized that 3-oxoLCA and isoLCA are produced in a manner similar 
186 
to 3-oxoDCA and isoDCA, via the actions of 5BR, 3aHSDH, and 3bHSDH (Fig. 2g and Extended 
187 
Data Fig. 5a). On the other hand, the biosynthetic pathway leading to isoallo-bile acid generation 
188 
had not been previously determined. We predicted that isoalloLCA might be generated from 3-oxo-
189 
D4-LCA by the sequential action of a 5a-reductase (5AR) homologue and 3bHSDH, through a 3-
190 
oxoalloLCA intermediate. 5AR is known to mediate the conversion of testosterone into 5a-
191 
dihydrotestosterone by hydrogenating across the C4-C5 double bond, thereby forcing the A and B 
192 
steroid rings into a planar (trans) conformation28 (Extended Data Fig. 5c). We reasoned that 3-
193 
oxoalloLCA (a trans-bile acid) might arise from 3-oxo-D4-LCA via an analogous pathway. We also 
194 
predicted that the subsequent transformation of 3-oxoalloLCA to isoalloLCA might use a 3bHSDH, 
195 
mirroring the previously characterized conversion of 3-oxoDCA to isoDCA18 (Fig. 2g and Extended 
196 
Data Fig. 5b). 
197 
In order to validate our pathway predictions and identify isoLCA-, 3-oxoLCA-, and 
198 
isoalloLCA-producing bacterial strains, we followed up on a supercentenarian (CE91, over 110 yo) 
199 
who displayed no major abnormalities in a blood test and showed high levels of faecal iso-, 3-oxo-, 
200 
and isoallo-LCA (Fig. 2a). We cultured faecal samples from CE91 in vitro in a variety of media and 
201 
analysed bacterial colonies by 16S rRNA gene sequencing to elaborate a consortium of 68 unique 
202 

 
 7
strains, which roughly recapitulated the microbiota structure of CE91 (Fig. 3a and Table S3). We 
203 
then incubated individual isolates at pH 7 or pH 9 with either CDCA, LCA, or 3-oxo-D4-LCA as 
204 
starting substrates. Culture supernatants were collected after 48 hr and bile acids were quantified by 
205 
LC-MS/MS (Fig. 3b, c and Extended Data Fig. 8). Incubation with CDCA did not result in 
206 
production of iso-, 3-oxo-, or isoallo-LCAs in any of the cultures, though C. scindens strains 59-60 
207 
(St59-60) and C. hylemonae St63 were able to produce LCA, albeit at low levels (Extended Data 
208 
Fig. 8a, b), in line with previous reports27. When cultured with LCA, Gordonibacter pamelaeae St32 
209 
and E. lenta St33-35 were found to produce 3-oxoLCA and isoLCA (Fig. 3b and Extended Data 
210 
Fig. 8c, d), implying their carriage of 3aHSDH and 3bHSDH as predicted in a previous study18. In 
211 
addition, Raoulibacter timonensis St30-31 and Lachnospiraceae spp. St57 were also capable of 
212 
transforming LCA into 3-oxoLCA, suggesting that these species possess 3aHSDH, similar to E. lenta. 
213 
When 3-oxo-D4-LCA was used as a substrate, 3-oxoLCA accumulated to high levels in the 
214 
supernatants of Hungatella hathewayi St54-55 and Lachnospiraceae spp. St62 cultures (Fig. 3c and 
215 
Extended Data Fig. 8e, f), suggesting that these strains possess 5BR. Similarly, isoLCA was 
216 
generated from 3-oxo-D4-LCA at high levels in Clostridium innocuum St51 and Lachnospiraceae spp. 
217 
St58 cultures (Fig. 3c and Extended Data Fig. 8e, f), suggesting carriage of 5BR and 3bHSDH. It is 
218 
noteworthy that Parabacteroides distasonis St4-5 converted LCA to 3-oxoLCA and further to 3-oxo-
219 
D4-LCA (Fig. 3b and Extended Data Fig. 8c, d), as well as 3-oxo-D4-LCA to isoLCA and LCA (Fig. 
220 
3c and Extended Data Fig. 8e, f), suggesting that these strains possess 3aHSDH, 3bHSDH, and 
221 
5BR. Collectively, at least 12 among 68 strains were capable of robustly generating 3-oxoLCA, and 
222 
8 were able to generate isoLCA from either LCA or 3-oxo-D4-LCA. 
223 
Strikingly, after incubation with 3-oxo-D4-LCA, a marked accumulation of isoalloLCA was 
224 
observed in the cultures of Parabacteroides merdae St3, Odoribacter laneus St19, Odoribacteraceae 
225 
spp. St21-24, and to a lesser degree in those of Bacteroides dorei St6-7 (Fig. 3c and Extended Data 
226 
Fig. 8e, f), suggesting that these strains harbour both 5AR and 3bHSDH activities. Additionally, 
227 
Parabacteroides goldsteinii St1-2, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron St9, B. uniformis St10-13, Alistipes 
228 
finegoldii St15-16, A. onderdonkii St17-18, and O. laneus St20 cultures all displayed a substantial 
229 
accumulation of presumed intermediate 3-oxoalloLCA, but little to no isoalloLCA (Fig. 3c and 
230 
Extended Data Fig. 8e, f), likely due to carriage of 5AR but lack or insufficient activity of 3bHSDH 
231 
in these culture conditions. Therefore, a total of 20 Bacteroidales strains (St1-24 excluding St4, 5, 8, 
232 
and 14) were found to be capable of transforming 3-oxo-D4-LCA into 3-oxoalloLCA, 8 of which were 
233 
able to robustly generate isoalloLCA. Of note, incubation at pH 9 (representative of centenarians’ gut 
234 
environment) enhanced the accumulation of 3-oxoalloLCA, isoalloLCA, isoLCA, and 3-oxoLCA as 
235 

 
 8
compared to that at pH 7 (Extended Data Fig. 8), suggesting that alkaline pH stress may promote 
236 
the activity or expression of enzymes involved in the production of these bile acids. 
237 
 
238 
5AR- and 3bHSDH-mediated transformation of 3-oxo-D4-LCA to isoalloLCA 
239 
To further validate our predicted biosynthetic pathway, and in particular the hypotheses that 
240 
3-oxo-D4-LCA conversion to 3-oxoLCA and isoLCA is mediated by 5BR and 3a-/3b-HSDH, and 
241 
that isoalloLCA generation is mediated by 5AR and 3bHSDH, we sequenced the genomes of all 68 
242 
isolates by integrating Miseq and PacBio platforms (Table S3). Querying these genome sequences 
243 
revealed carriage of 3aHSDH genes by Eggerthella strains (Fig. 3e), in line with a previous report18. 
244 
P. distasonis St4, 5 were also found to have putative 3aHSDH genes, consistent with the above in 
245 
vitro evaluation of bile acid metabolism. Additionally, sequences orthologous to human 5AR (steroid 
246 
5 alpha-reductase 1, SRD5A1) were identified in 21 Bacteroidales strains with >30% amino acid 
247 
sequence similarity (magenta in Fig. 3d-e, Extended Data Fig. 9, and Extended Data Fig. 10a). We 
248 
next assessed genes directly adjacent to the predicted 5AR loci. In all 21 strains, we found clusters of 
249 
genes functionally related to bile acid metabolism, including sequences annotated as NADH:flavin 
250 
oxidoreductase, which we predicted to be 5BR (blue in Fig. 3d-e, Extended Data Fig. 9, and 
251 
Extended Data Fig. 10b). We also identified sequences annotated as short-chain dehydrogenase 
252 
(SDR), which we predicted to be 3bHSDH. These SDR sequences comprised two groups: group I 
253 
sequences (green) showed high similarity (>40%) to P. merdae St3 3bHSDH, whereas group II 
254 
sequences (purple) were closely related to one another but not to P. merdae St3 3bHSDH (Fig. 3d-f, 
255 
Extended Data Fig. 9, and Extended Data Fig. 10c). In addition, there were sequences presumably 
256 
encoding bile acid transporters near the gene clusters (Extended Data Fig. 9). We found that carriage 
257 
of putative 5AR and 3bHSDH genes was clearly related to 3-oxoalloLCA and/or isoalloLCA 
258 
production from 3-oxo-D4-LCA, except in the case of St8 (Fig. 3c, e). 
259 
To further elucidate the relevant biosynthetic pathways, we deepened our in vitro screen of 
260 
the 24 Bacteroidales isolates’ bile acid transformation capabilities by incubating each with either 3-
261 
oxoalloLCA, 3-oxoLCA, or isoLCA (Extended Data Fig. 11a-c). The observed patterns of bile acid 
262 
transformation were largely consistent with our predicted pathway, although there was substantial 
263 
substrate specificity and strain-to-strain variation in transformation efficiency. For instance, P. merdae 
264 
St3, P. distasonis St4-5, and B. dorei St7, and Odoribacteraceae St21 all had strong 3bHSDH 
265 
activities, reflected by simultaneous high isoalloLCA production from 3-oxoalloLCA (Extended 
266 
Data Fig. 11a) and isoLCA production from 3-oxoLCA (Extended Data Fig. 11b), whereas several 
267 
other strains such as B. dorei St6 and B. uniformis St10-13 showed less efficient biotransformation 
268 

 
 9
despite carriage of putative 3bHSDH genes (Extended Data Fig. 11a, b). The strength of 5BR 
269 
activity also differed among the strains: P. distasonis St4-5 and B. dorei St7 effectively transformed 
270 
3-oxoLCA to 3-oxo-D4-LCA, while other strains showed moderate to weak activities (Extended Data 
271 
Fig. 11b). Porphyromonas somerae St14 lacked putative 5AR and 3bHSDH genes but was able to 
272 
generate isoalloLCA from 3-oxoalloLCA nonetheless (Extended Data Fig. 11a), suggesting that it 
273 
carries a strain-specific gene with 3bHSDH activity. To examine whether isolates carrying different 
274 
genes were capable of cooperatively metabolizing bile acids, we cocultured E. lenta St34 (a 3aHSDH 
275 
and 3bHSDH encoder) or P. distasonis St4 (a 3aHSDH, 3bHSDH, and 5BR encoder) with P. merdae 
276 
St3 or Odoribacteraceae St21 (5BR, 5AR, and 3bHSDH encoders) in the presence of LCA. All 
277 
combinations resulted in cooperative production of isoalloLCA, with E. lenta St34 and P. merdae St3 
278 
coculture giving the highest yield (Extended Data Fig. 11d). Collectively, although there were 
279 
strain-dependent differences in enzymatic activity, substrate specificity, and gene location, the 
280 
Bacteroidales gene clusters identified above likely contribute to the cooperative production of bile 
281 
acids and may be responsible, at least in part, for the unique faecal bile acid profile observed in 
282 
centenarians. 
283 
To further confirm the roles of 5AR and 3bHSDH in microbiota-mediated bile acid 
284 
metabolism, we set out to generate mutant strains of P. merdae and Odoribacteraceae. Although a 
285 
lack of genetic tools hampered the introduction of targeted mutations in Odoribacteraceae strains, 
286 
we were able to successfully generate three P. merdae St3 mutants lacking genes encoding putative 
287 
5AR (PM3806), 3bHSDH (PM3804), or 5BR (PM3805) via conjugation29 (Extended Data Fig. 12). 
288 
As expected, when incubated with 3-oxo-D4-LCA, P. merdaeD5AR failed to produce either 3-
289 
oxoalloLCA or isoalloLCA, whereas P. merdaeD3bHSDH was able to generate 3-oxoalloLCA but 
290 
not isoalloLCA (Fig. 3f). Consistently, when incubated with 3-oxoalloLCA, P. merdaeD5AR 
291 
generated isoalloLCA in a manner similar to the wild-type parental strain, whereas P. 
292 
merdaeD3bHSDH did not (Fig. 3f). P. merdaeD3bHSDH additionally failed to convert 3-oxoLCA 
293 
into isoLCA, confirming that 3bHSDH can utilize both trans- and cis- bile acids as substrates. P. 
294 
merdaeD5BR produced isoalloLCA from 3-oxo-D4-LCA or 3-oxoalloLCA but showed a defect in 
295 
transforming 3-oxoLCA into 3-oxo-D4-LCA (Fig. 3f). Together, these results corroborate the 
296 
involvement of 5AR, 3bHSDH, and 5BR in the production of isoalloLCA, 3-oxoLCA, and isoLCA 
297 
by the human gut microbiota.

Bactericidal effects of isoalloLCA against gram-positive pathogens 
300 

 
 10 
Secondary bile acids are known to play important roles in several biological contexts, such as 
301 
modulation of host metabolic and immune responses (including the induction of regulatory T 
302 
cells)25,30–35 and prevention of intestinal pathogen expansion36–39. In particular, DCA, LCA, and 
303 
isoLCA have been implicated in inhibiting the growth of Clostridioides difficile36,40, which is 
304 
currently classified as one of the most urgent antibiotic resistance threats41. Thus, we next investigated 
305 
whether 3-oxoLCA and isoalloLCA share this capacity to inhibit C. difficile growth. We incubated 
306 
C. difficile 630 with various concentrations of isoLCA, 3-oxoLCA, isoalloLCA, 3-oxoalloLCA, LCA, 
307 
DCA, or vehicle control and used optical density measurements to track growth over time in vitro. 
308 
Strikingly, isoalloLCA potently inhibited the growth of C. difficile 630. The minimal inhibitory 
309 
concentration required to prevent ≥90% growth (MIC90) in WCA medium was 2.0 µM, far below 
310 
that of the other bile acids tested (Fig. 4a, b and Extended Data Fig. 13a, b). Potent growth inhibition 
311 
by isoalloLCA was also observed in toxigenic C. difficile VPI10463 and vancomycin-resistant 
312 
Enterococcus faecium (VRE) (Fig. 4a, b and Extended Data Fig. 13a, b). Scanning and transmission 
313 
electron microscopy revealed that isoalloLCA was bactericidal, producing morphologic and 
314 
ultrastructural alterations including collapse, swelling, and multiple cross walls in C. difficile 630 and 
315 
VRE (Fig. 4c). These patterns of damage are reminiscent of those induced by b-lactam antibiotics42. 
316 
Co-culturing with Odoribacteraceae St21 in conjunction with 3-oxo-D4-LCA supplementation 
317 
resulted in significant C. difficile 630 and VRE growth inhibition, similar to that observed with 
318 
isoalloLCA treatment (Fig. 4d). In contrast, bacteriostatic effects were not observed when co-
319 
culturing was performed with C. innocuum St51 (an isoLCA producer) or P. distasonis St4 (an 
320 
isoLCA and LCA producer). 
321 
We then examined the effect of isoalloLCA on other gram-positive pathogens, including 
322 
methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), Streptococcus dysgalactiae subsp. equisimilis 
323 
(SDSE), Clostridium perfringens, Streptococcus pyogenes, Streptococcus sanguinis, and Bacillus 
324 
cereus, as well as on gram-negative pathogens, including Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, 
325 
Salmonella enterica, Proteus vulgaris, and Proteus mirabilis. S. aureus is a prominent skin pathogen, 
326 
though it often colonizes the intestine and is known to be resistant to most bile acids43. IsoalloLCA 
327 
strongly inhibited growth of all gram-positive pathogens tested, including S. aureus, with MIC90 
328 
values ranging from 0.5 to 3 µM in WCA and from 3 to 6.25 µM in BHI medium (Fig. 4a, b and 
329 
Extended Data Fig. 13a, b). In contrast, all members of our gram-negative pathogen panel were 
330 
resistant to isoalloLCA, even at the highest concentration tested (50 µM) (Fig. 4a, b and Extended 
331 
Data Fig. 13a, c). Taken together, these results suggest that isoalloLCA exerts strong 
332 
bactericidal/bacteriostatic effects specifically on gram-positive pathogens, suggesting that it may 
333 
interfere with the bacterial cell wall. 
334

335 
Effects of isoalloLCA on the commensal gut microbiota 
336 
Gut metabolites are encountered not only by enteric pathogens, but also by commensals, and 
337 
as such we proceeded to investigate how isoalloLCA affects common members of the human 
338 
microbiota. A total of 42 prevalent gut microbiota members, consisting of both gram-positive and 
339 
gram-negative species, were selected from our culture collection, and each was incubated with 
340 
increasing concentrations of isoalloLCA in WCA and BHI media. IsoalloLCA did not appreciably 
341 
affect the growth of most gram-negative commensals such as Bacteroides (Fig. 4e and Extended 
342 
Data Fig. 14a). In contrast, it substantially interfered with the growth of gram-positive commensals 
343 
(Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 14a). However, MIC90 values for commensal strains were 
344 
generally higher than those for pathogens, and scanning electron microscopy revealed that 
345 
commensals’ cell wall structures [C. sporogenes, C. indolis, and C. HGF2 (innocuum)] were 
346 
preserved when incubated with 2.5 µM isoalloLCA (1.25x MIC90 for C. difficile) (Extended Data 
347 
Fig. 14b). In particular, Lactobacillus strains were highly resistant to the inhibitory effects of 
348 
isoalloLCA (Fig. 4e). Moreover, culturing commensal strains in peptone- and amino acid-rich BHI 
349 
conferred increased resistance to isoalloLCA as compared to culturing in WCA medium, whereas 
350 
pathogens generally remained sensitive irrespective of media (Fig. 4a, e and Extended Data Fig. 15). 
351 
These results indicate that although the concentration at which isoalloLCA exerts bactericidal effects 
352 
on gram-positive bacteria varies substantially depending on environmental conditions, pathogens 
353 
consistently remain more sensitive than commensals. 
354 
To further evaluate the effects of isoalloLCA within the context of a complex, normal gut 
355 
flora, we incubated human faecal microbiota from young, healthy volunteers with isoalloLCA, 3-
356 
oxoLCA, LCA, or vehicle control, and analysed the shift in bacterial composition by 16S rRNA gene 
357 
sequencing. Although a-diversity was not significantly affected, isoalloLCA induced broad changes 
358 
in microbial community structure that were evident at the phylum level (Fig. 4f, g). We observed a 
359 
pronounced reduction in gram-positive species like Clostridium, Faecalibacterium, Bifidobacterium, 
360 
and Streptococcus, along with a corresponding increase in gram-negative species like Bacteroides 
361 
and Alistipes, following incubation with isoalloLCA as compared to other bile acid compounds (Fig. 
362 
4g). These results are consistent with the increased relative abundance of Bacteroides and Alistipes 
363 
seen in centenarians’ gut microbiota, and suggest that isoalloLCA can directly impact the structure 
364 
of intestinal microbial communities. 
365 
Finally, having confirmed that 5AR, 5BR, and 3bHSDH play critical roles in the production 
366 
of unique secondary bile acid derivatives, we returned to the metagenome sequence data of Japanese 
367 
centenarians to identify additional species that carry these genes. 5AR, 5BR, and 3bHSDH gene 
368 

 
 12 
clusters were identified in 35 species (all Bacteroidales) (Extended Data Fig. 16a). We evaluated 
369 
the abundance of these species in relation to faecal concentrations of isoLCA, 3-oxoLCA, and 
370 
isoalloLCA. We observed a significant positive correlation between isoalloLCA levels and several 
371 
Alistipes sp., Bacteroides cellulosilitycus, B. intestinalis, and P. goldsteinii; these species were also 
372 
positively associated with isoLCA and 3-oxoLCA concentrations (Extended Data Fig. 16b). In 
373 
contrast, Bacteroides vulgatus and Bacteroides ovatus were significantly negatively associated with 
374 
the three tested secondary bile acids. O. laneus showed significant or moderate positive correlations 
375 
with 3-oxoLCA, isoLCA, and isoalloLCA, and we did not observe significant associations between 
376 
P. merdae and these bile acids (Extended Data Fig. 16b). These results suggest that expression and 
377 
activity of the identified genes may be regulated via complex species-specific and intestinal milieu-
378 
dependent mechanisms and likely involve interbacterial- and bacteria-host interactions in vivo.

380 
Discussion 
381 
In the present study, we identified centenarian-specific gut microbiota signatures and defined 
382 
bacterial species and genes/pathways that promote the generation of isoLCA, 3-oxoLCA, and 
383 
isoalloLCA. It has been reported that isoalloLCA induces Treg cells and that 3-oxoLCA and isoLCA 
384 
suppresses T helper 17 (TH17) cells, and as such the accumulation of these bile acids may protect 
385 
against overexuberant immune responses and inflammation (ref.30 and Jun Huh and A. Sloan Devlin, 
386 
personal communication). Consistent with this notion, centenarians who participated in this and 
387 
previous studies1–3 were largely unafflicted by chronic diseases, such as metabolic disease and cancer, 
388 
which are associated with aberrant activation of immune system and immunosenescence12. In 
389 
addition to the previously reported anti-inflammatory properties of isoalloLCA, we found that it also 
390 
exerts a very strong antibacterial effect against gram-positive pathogens. Several reports have 
391 
demonstrated that bile acids contribute to protection against enteropathogenic infection36,44,45. To our 
392 
knowledge, isoalloLCA is one of the most potent antimicrobial agents selective against gram-positive 
393 
microbes, including multidrug-resistant pathogens. Although more research is needed to elucidate the 
394 
molecular mechanism by which isoalloLCA disrupts bacterial cell wall structure, our findings suggest 
395 
that isoalloLCA may be a potential factor contributing to longevity by promoting colonization 
396 
resistance against gram-positive pathogens. Regardless of whether the increase in isoalloLCA-, 
397 
isoLCA-, and 3-oxoLCA-producing microbes is a consequence of ageing or a contributor to longevity, 
398 
these bile acids could be used as biomarkers to monitor health conditions and predict life expectancy. 
399 
Moreover, we could exploit the unique bile acid-metabolizing capabilities of the bacterial strains 
400 
identified in this study to rationally manipulate the bile acid pool and ultimately ameliorate infectious 
401 

 
 13 
diseases caused by gram-positive pathogens including antibiotic-resistant C. difficile, VRE, and 
402 
MRSA.

Metagenomic sequencing and 16S rRNA gene pyrosequencing of human stool samples 
419 
Faecal samples were suspended in an equal volume of PBS containing 20% glycerol and 10 mM EDTA and stored 
420 
at -80 ºC until use. After thawing, 100 µL of faecal suspension was gently mixed and incubated in 800 µL TE10 
421 
(10mM Tris-HCl, 10 mM EDTA) buffer containing RNase A (final concentration of 100 µg/mL, Invitrogen) and 
422 
lysozyme (final concentration of 15 mg/mL, Sigma) for 1 hr at 37 ºC. Purified achromopeptidase (final 
423 
concentration of 2,000 U/mL, Wako) was added and further incubated for 30 min at 37 ºC. SDS (final concentration 
424 
of 1%) and proteinase K (final concentration of 1 mg/mL, Roche) was further added to the mixture and incubated 
425 
for 1 hr at 55 ºC. High molecular weight DNA was extracted with phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1 at 
426 
pH 7.9), precipitated with isopropanol (equal volume to the aqueous phase), washed with 1 mL of 70% ethanol, and 
427 
gently resuspended in 30 µL of TE buffer. 
428 
The 16S rRNA sequencing was performed using MiSeq according to the Illumina protocol. PCR was 
429 
performed using 27Fmod 5’-AGRGTTTGATYMTGGCTCAG-3’and 338R 5’-TGCTGCCTCCCGTAGGAGT-3’ 
430 
to the V1‒V2 region of the 16S rRNA gene. Amplicons generated from each sample (~330bp) were purified using 
431 
AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter). DNA was quantified using a Quant-iT Picogreen dsDNA assay kit 
432 
(Invitrogen) and Infinite M Plex plate reader (Tecan), then stored at 4 ºC. The pooled amplicon library was 
433 
sequenced using a MiSeq Reagent Kit v2 (500 cycles) and Miseq sequencer (Illumina, 2 x 250bp paired-end reads). 
434 
Two paired-end reads were merged using the fastq-join program based on overlapping sequences. Reads with an 
435 
average quality value of <25 and inexact matches to both universal primers were filtered out. Both primer sequences 
436 
were trimmed off and 3,000 quality filter-passed reads were rearranged in descending order according to the quality 
437 
value and then clustered into OTUs with a 97% pairwise-identity cutoff using the UCLUST program v5.2.3246. 
438 
Taxonomic assignment of each OTU was made via searching by similarity against the Ribosomal Database Project 
439 
(RDP) and the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) genome database using the GLSEARCH 
440 
program. 
441 
Metagenomic sequencing libraries were prepared from 2 ng of input DNA using the Nextera XT DNA 
442 
Library Preparation kit (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. Libraries were pooled 
443 

 
 15 
by equal volume and insert sizes and concentrations for each pooled library were determined using an Agilent 
444 
Bioanalyzer DNA 1000 kit (Agilent Technologies). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with 
445 
151bp paired-end reads to yield ~10 million paired-end reads per sample. Data was analysed using the Broad Picard 
446 
Pipeline, which includes de-multiplexing and data aggregation (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard). The quality 
447 
control for the metagenomic data was conducted using Trim Galore! to detect and remove sequencing adapters 
448 
(minimum overlap of 5bp) and KneadData v0.7.2 to remove human DNA contamination and trim low-quality 
449 
sequences (HEADCROP:15, SLIDINGWINDOW:1:20), retaining reads that were at least 50bp long. Metagenomic 
450 
reads were assembled individually for each sample into contigs using MEGAHIT47, followed by an open reading 
451 
frame prediction with Prodigal48 and retaining predicted genes that had both a start and a stop codon. A non-
452 
redundant gene catalogue was constructed by clustering predicted genes based on sequence similarity at 95% 
453 
identity and 90% coverage of the shorter sequence using CD-HIT49,50. Reads were mapped to the gene catalogue 
454 
with BWA requiring a unique, strong mapping with at least 95% sequence identity over the length of the read51, 
455 
counted (count matrix) and normalized to transcripts per kilobase million (TPM matrix) using in-house scripts. 
456 
Count matrix served as an input for binning genes into metagenomic species pan-genomes (core and accessory 
457 
genes) using MSPminer with default settings52. We represented the abundance of every metagenomic species (MSP) 
458 
in a sample as a median TPM for 30 top representative core genes reported by MSPminer. Assembled genes were 
459 
annotated at species, genus, and phylum levels with NCBI RefSeq (version May 2018) as described previously53. 
460 
To annotate phylogenetically MSPs that had no match to any species from NCBI RefSeq we used Phylophlan with 
461 
default settings54. a-diversities were calculated using Shannon index and b-diversity was calculated using Bray-
462 
Curtis dissimilarity based on relative abundances at species levels (Vegan package in R). The non-redundant gene 
463 
catalogue was queried using USEARCH ublast53 with proteins in the bai operon of C. scindens26 or proteins in the 
464 
bacterial isolates reported here as 5AR, 5BR, 3bHSDH I, or 3bHSDH II to identify and annotate homologous 
465 
proteins with at least 40% identity and 80% coverage to the query sequence. An identical processing pipeline has 
466 
been applied to the dataset describing the gut microbiome in Sardinian centenarians8. 
467 
In the subsequent analysis, we only used samples with at least 4 million reads after the quality control step. 
468 
Additionally, we discarded samples that were collected while the subject was undergoing any antibiotic treatment. 
469 
To test differential abundance of species or phyla and differences in the Shannon diversity index, we employed 
470 
linear random effects modelling (centenarians vs. young or elderly controls) or fixed effects modelling (elderly vs. 
471 
young controls), as implemented in the lmer and lm functions in R. Furthermore, for analysis of species differential 
472 
abundance, we restricted the analysis to MSPs present in at least 10% of samples, zeros were replaced by half of 
473 
the smallest non-zero measurement on a per-feature basis and log10 transformation was applied on the relative 
474 
abundances for normality. Linear modelling included fixed effect covariates: sex (male or female) and cohort 
475 
information (centenarian, elderly, or young); random effect included subject information to account for more than 
476 
one sample among a few centenarians. The permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA analysis 
477 
as implemented in adonis function in the R package Vegan was applied to the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity to identify 
478 
the correlation between age group (centenarian, elderly, young) and sex information and the composition of the gut 
479 
microbiome as a whole.

Bacterial whole-genome sequencing 
573 
The extracted genomic DNA of 68 isolated strains was sheared to yield DNA fragments. The genome sequences 
574 
were determined by the whole-genome shotgun strategy using PacBio Sequel and Illumina MiSeq sequencers. The 
575 
library of the Illumina Miseq 2 x 300bp paired-end sequencing was prepared using TruSeq DNA PCR-Free kit 
576 
(target length = 550bp) and all the MiSeq reads were trimmed and filtered with a >20 quality value (QV) using 
577 
FASTX-toolkit (hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toolkit). The library of the PacBio Sequel sequencing was prepared using 
578 
SMRTbell template prep kit 2.0 (target length = 10 - 15kbp) without DNA shearing. After removal of internal control 
579 
and adaptor trimming by Sequel, the error correction of the trimmed reads was performed using Canu (v1.8) with 
580 
additional options (corOutCoverage = 10,000, corMinCoverage = 0, corMhapSensitivity = high). De novo hybrid 
581 
assembly of the filter-passed MiSeq reads and the corrected Sequel reads were performed using Unicycler (v0.4.8), 
582 
which contained checks for overlapping and circularization to generate circular contigs. The gene prediction and 
583 
annotation of the generated contigs were performed using the Rapid Annotations based on Subsystem Technology 
584 
(RAST) server56 and Prokka software tool57. Default parameters were used unless otherwise specified.

Mutant generation 
587 
The deletion mutants (D5AR, D5BR, and D3bHSDH) of P. merdae St3 were generated by conjugation-mediated 
588 
plasmid transfection and selection of double-crossover resolvents with a rhamnose-inducible ssBfe1 cassette29. 
589 
Approximately 2kb sequences flanking the coding region were amplified by PCR (PCR primers used in this study 
590 
are listed in Table S4) and assembled into the PstI and SalI sites of the suicide vector pLGB30 using HiFi DNA 
591 
Assembly (NEB) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. 1 µL aliquots of each reaction were transformed into electro-
592 
competent E. coli MFDpir58. Transformants were conjugated with P. merdae St3 as follows. The donor (E. coli 
593 
MFDpir) and recipient (P. merdae St3) strains were cultured in LB and BHI media, respectively, to an OD600 of 0.5 
594 
and mixed at a ratio of 1:1. The mixture was dropped onto a BHI agar plate and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C 
595 
for 16 hr. Transconjugants were selected on BHI agar plates containing tetracycline (6 µg/mL). Subsequently, to 
596 
select for loss of plasmid from the genome by a second crossover, transconjugants were plated on M9 agar 
597 
supplemented with 0.25% (wt/vol) glucose, 50 mg/L L-cysteine, 5 mg/L haemin, 2.5 µg/L vitamin K1, 2 mg/L 
598 
FeSO4·7H2O, 5 µg/L vitamin B12, and 10 mM rhamnose. Successful deletions were confirmed by PCR and Sanger 
599 
sequencing.

Statistical Analysis 
672 
Pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to evaluate differences in the relative abundance of bai operon 
673 
homologues in centenarians compared to elderly- and young-controls. Spearman’s rank correlation was used to 
674 
evaluate trends between the relative abundance of Bacteroidales species encoding 5AR, 5BR, 3bHSDH I, or 
675 
3bHSDH II genes and the abundance of the secondary bile acids in stool samples. Overall nominal P-values were 
676 
adjusted for multiple testing using Benjamini-Hochberg correction and associations at FDR P < 0.05 (unless stated 
677 
differently) were considered as significant. Statistical analyses below were performed using GraphPad Prism 
678 
software (GraphPad Software, Inc.). One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test (parametric) and Kruskal-Wallis with 
679 
Dunn’s test (nonparametric) was used for multiple comparisons. Wilcoxon signed-rank test post hoc test with 
680 
Bonferroni correction (nonparametric) was used to compare group means for meta 16S rRNA analysis. Mann-
681 
Whitney test (two-tailed) with Welch’s correction (nonparametric) was used for all comparisons between two groups 
682 
in the co-culture inhibition experiments.
