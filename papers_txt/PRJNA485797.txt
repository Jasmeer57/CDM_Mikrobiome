ABSTRACT 
23 
The gut microbiome is emerging as an important factor in regulating mental health yet it remains 
24 
unclear what the target should be for psychiatric treatment. We aimed to elucidate the complement of 
25 
the gut-microbiome community for individuals with bipolar disorder relative to controls; and test for 
26 
relationships with burden of disease measures. We compared the stool microbiome from individuals 
27 
with bipolar disorder (n=115) and control subjects (n=64) using 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene 
28 
sequence analysis. Analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) revealed global community case-control 
29 
differences (AMOVA p=0.047). Operational Taxonomical Unit (OTU) level analysis revealed significantly 
30 
decreased fractional representation (p<0.001) of Faecalibacterium after adjustment for age, sex, BMI 
31 
and false discovery rate (FDR) correction at the p<0.05 level. Within individuals with bipolar disorder, 
32 
the fractional representation of Faecalibacterium associated with better self-reported health outcomes 
33 
based on the Short Form Health Survey (SF12); the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ9); the Pittsburg 
34 
Sleep Quality Index (PSQI); the Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD7); and the Altman Mania Rating 
35 
Scale (ASRM), independent of covariates. This study provides the first detailed analysis of the gut 
36 
microbiome relationships with multiple psychiatric domains from a bipolar population. The data support 
37 
the hypothesis that targeting the microbiome may be an effective treatment paradigm for bipolar 
38 
disorder.

INTRODUCTION 
44 
Accumulated evidence over the past decade supports a bi-directional relationship between the 
45 
modifiable gut microbiome, mood and cognitive dysfunction. Schizophrenic and bipolar subjects show 
46 
increased markers of bacterial tanslocation from the intestinal lumen (compared to controls), which 
47 
may underlie increased inflammation (1) known to be a component of these disorders. In depressed 
48 
subjects compared to controls, a survey of the stool microbiota showed increased Bacteroidetes, 
49 
Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria and reduced Firmicutes Faecalibacterium, the latter of which 
50 
negatively associated with severity of depression (2, 3). Intervention studies in humans suggest a benefit 
51 
of microbiome manipulation for clinical psychiatric outcomes. Supplementation of L. helviticus and 
52 
Bifobacterium longum, which reduce anxiety-like behavior in rats, reduced psychological distress in 
53 
humans (4). Patients with gastrointestinal disorder, which is highly comorbid with psychiatric conditions, 
54 
who consumed fermented milk that contained B.bifidum, showed improvement in gastrointestinal and 
55 
psychological symptoms (5). In a small, randomized control trial of a multi-species pro-biotic in 
56 
depressed adults, the pro-biotic treatment significantly reduced cognitive activity toward depressed 
57 
mood when compared to placebo (6). These studies suggest that targeted manipulation of the 
58 
microbiome can be beneficial for mood state in a psychiatric population. 
59 
 
60 
Animal studies support a neurochemical mechanism for the effect of microbiome manipulation on 
61 
behavior. Treating mice with pre-biotics (7) or antibiotics (8) influenced brain derived neurotrophic 
62 
factor activity, NMDA glutamate receptors, oxytocin, vasopressin and central tryptophan metabolism. 
63 
Diet induced disruption of the mouse microbiome is concomitant with changes in anxiety-like behavior 
64 
(9, 10), depressive-like behaviors (10), and cognitive performance (9, 11). Disruption of the mouse 
65 
enteric microbiota by infection can mediate stress-induced memory dysfunction and this can be 
66 
normalized by probiotic (12) and prebiotic treatment (13). Taken together, these studies demonstrate 
67

that the gut microbiome is responsive to many physiological stimuli associated with the induction of 
68 
affective disorders and relevant central circuitry, and that targeting the integrity of the microbiome with 
69 
prebiotic and probiotic therapies may attenuate or prevent dysregulation. 
70 
 
71 
In the current study we surveyed the stool microbiome of bipolar and control subjects from the Prechter 
72 
Longitudinal Study of Bipolar Disorder, housed at the University of Michigan. This is a deep phenotyping 
73 
longitudinal study for which we collect bi-monthly self-report measures of physical and mental health, 
74 
allowing us to compare the taxonomical complement of the stool microbiome with self-reported burden 
75 
of disease measures.

METHODS AND MATERIALS 
78 
Human Subjects: All subjects in the current study were recruited from the Heinz C. Prechter Longitudinal 
79 
Study of Bipolar Disorder at the University of Michigan Depression Center (14). All individuals were 
80 
diagnosed using the Diagnostic Interview for Genetic Studies (DIGS) (15) and included in this current 
81 
study are bipolar individuals adhering to the DSM IV diagnostic criteria (16) and healthy unaffected 
82 
controls who were willing to return stool samples as described below. Most bipolar subjects were taking 
83 
more than one psychiatric medication, This study was not designed to specifically test for medication 
84 
effects, which for the current analyses are viewed as a component of the illness. Limitations of the 
85 
conclusions regarding the lack of separation of medication effects are discussed in the Discussion 
86 
Section of the manuscript. The Internal Review Board for Human Studies at the University of Michigan 
87 
approved all protocols. 
88 
 
89 
Collection of Self-Reports: Longitudinal participants completed self-rated questionnaires by mail every 2 
90 
months from the time of their enrollment in the longitudinal study. Due to the dynamic temporal nature 
91 
MANUSCRIPT
 
ACCEPTED
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
of these responses, for the current study we used the means of summary scores from each 
92 
questionnaire to most robustly estimate the chronic burden of disease. Limitations of this strategy are 
93 
discussed in the Discussion Section at the end of the manuscript. These questionnaires used include the 
94 
Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), the Altman Self-Rating Mania Scale (ASRM), the Short Form 
95 
Health Survey (SF-12), the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Assessment (GAD-7), and the Pittsburg Sleep 
96 
Quality Index (PSQI). The PHQ-9 defines severity and frequency of depressive states, while the ASRM 
97 
defines severity and frequency of manic states.  Similarly, the GAD-7 defines severity and frequency of 
98 
anxiety symptoms. Scores greater than or equal to 5 for the PHQ-9, ASRM, and GAD-7 indicate a 
99 
clinically significant depressive, manic, or anxious mood state, respectively, however for the current 
100 
analyses these were treated as continuous variables without thresholds. The SF-12 scores are sub-scaled 
101 
into composite scores for both physical (PCS) and mental (MCS) health. SF-12 scores are standardized on 
102 
a scale of 0 to 100, where 0 indicates lowest level of health and 100 indicates the highest level of health. 
103 
The PSQI measures sleep quality where higher scores indicate lower sleep quality. For primary statistical 
104 
analyses, PSQI measures were condensed to a single summary score by the method of Buysse et al. (17). 
105 
However, analysis of the sub components are also discussed.

107 
Stool Microbiota Analysis: Home stool collection kits (DNA Genotek, Ontario CA), were mailed to 825 
108 
active Prechter Longitudinal Study subjects with a return pre-paid mailer, opt-in consent form and 
109 
request to participate in the microbiome analysis. Returned stool samples were frozen at -80C until 
110 
aliquoted into 96 well plates for DNA extraction. DNA was isolated with a PowerMag Microbiome 
111 
RNA/DNA Isolation Kit (Mo Bio Laboratories, Inc.) using an epMotion 5075 liquid handling system. The 
112 
V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using a dual-indexing sequencing strategy and 
113 
sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform as described previously (18).  Negative control wells including 
114 
all PCR reagents with water replacing sample template DNA were included across the plates and showed 
115 
MANUSCRIPT
 
ACCEPTED
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
no visible bands upon gel analysis. Sequences were processed and analyzed using the software package 
116 
mothur (v.1.36.1) according to the MiSeq SOP (http://www.mothur.org/wiki/MiSeq_SOP)(19). Briefly, 
117 
after sequence processing and alignment to the SILVA reference alignment (20), sequences were binned 
118 
into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) based on 97% sequence similarity using the average neighbor 
119 
method. Clustering sequences by the average neighbor method produces high-quality OTUs that include 
120 
all sequences regardless of their database representation (21, 22).  After discarding 11 samples with low 
121 
sequence counts (<100 per sample) and 1 sample with a sequence count of 1187 sequences, 3692 
122 
sequence reads remained as the least common denominator and were subsampled for direct 
123 
comparison of the remaining 179 samples used in this analysis. Relative abundances of OTUs were 
124 
calculated. Analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) (23) of θYC distances (a metric that takes relative 
125 
abundances of both shared and non-shared OTUs into account) (24) between communities was used to 
126 
determine if there were statistically significant differences between the microbiota from the bipolar and 
127 
control groups. Principle coordinates analysis (PCoA) was used to visualize the θYC distances between 
128 
samples. We also investigated the taxonomic composition of the bacterial communities by classifying 
129 
sequences within mothur using a modified version of the Ribosomal Database Project (RDP) training set 
130 
(25, 26). In a few cases, OTUs that were unidentified were retrieved by manual BLAST search. 
131

Statistical Analyses: For analysis of case-control differences of OTU level data, we used logistical 
133 
regressions, adjusting for age, sex, and BMI (Table 2b). To determine associations between OTUs and 
134 
self-reported health measures, we used linear regression analyses with the intra-individual means of 
135 
self-report summary scores as the outcome measures and fractional representation of each OTU as the 
136 
main predictor, controlling for age, sex, and BMI. For these regressions we used only data from 
137 
individuals with bipolar disorder to eliminate potential confounders associated with a bipolar diagnosis. 
138 
Standardized beta coefficients for the OTU – self-report associations were extracted along with the p-
139

140 
originally described by Benjamini and Hochberg, using a tool developed by Pike et al. (27) to determine

144 
Of the 825 stool collection kits mailed out to our longitudinal study participants with request to opt-in to 
145

146

147 
data) and 179 were used in the current analysis. The remaining 54 were excluded with their diagnosis 
148 
yet to be confirmed (n=38) or a non-bipolar DSM IV diagnosis of Schizo Affective, 295.70M (n=5); Other 
149 
Affective, 555.00A (n=2); or Other Non-Affective, 555.00N (n=9) and not used for this analysis. Table 1a 
150

153

154 
subjects.

DISCUSSION 
197 
In the current study we report an analysis of stool microbiome samples collected from bipolar 
198 
individuals and healthy controls who are participants of the Prechter Longitudinal Study of Bipolar 
199 
Disorder at the University of Michigan. We found significant differences between the global microbiome 
200 
communities and specific OTUs in individuals with bipolar disorder, compared to controls. We also 
201 
found significant relationships between fractional representation of several OTUs and self-reported 
202 
burden of disease measures. These data represent the first survey of the microbiome of a relatively 
203 
large number of bipolar individuals and the first analysis of the associations between the human 
204 
microbiome and psychiatric burden of disease measures in this population. 
205 
 
206 
The most striking finding from the current study was the group difference and the clinical outcome 
207 
associations for Faecalibacterium (OTU0003). Faecalibacterium is a Gram-positive butyrate-producing 
208 
gut bacterium (28) in large abundance in the human gut (29). Faecalibacterium has previously been 
209 
shown to be lower in many human conditions. Hospitalized (30) and frail elderly (31), compared to 
210 
healthy elderly, have lower gut Faecalibacterium. Decreased representation of this genus in 
211 
MANUSCRIPT
 
ACCEPTED
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
inflammatory bowel syndrome (32, 33) Crohn’s disease (34, 35), ulcerative colitis (35-37), colorectal 
212 
cancer (38) chronic idiopathic diarrhea (39), have also been reported. Other studies have found 
213 
decreases in diabetic patients (40) and glucose intolerance (41), non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (42), 
214 
patients with cholesterol gallstones (43), association with high hepatic fat content (44) and an inverse 
215 
association with severity of acute appendicitis (45). These studies all suggest potential effects of 
216 
Faecalibacterium on organ systems outside the gut as well. Furthermore, Faecalibacterium has been 
217 
shown to have anti-inflammatory properties in vitro and in vivo (46, 47). Taken together studies 
218 
examining the gut presence of Faecalibacterium suggest that higher fractional representation associates 
219 
with a healthier state. 
220 
 
221 
One study, relevant to the current report, found decreased fractional representation of 
222 
Faecalibacterium in a small sample of actively depressed patients relative to controls that was not 
223 
evident in treatment responsive depressed patients (3). This consistency adds strength to our current 
224 
finding of decreased Faecalibacterium in bipolar subjects and suggests that the lower gut levels may be 
225 
native to the illness and relevant to a depressed state. Furthermore, our current reports of associations 
226 
between self-reported mental health scales and this gut bacteria within bipolar subjects, suggest that 
227 
increasing gut levels of Faecalibacterium may have value for psychiatric outcomes. 
228 
 
229 
Methods to increase Faecalibacterium may include dietary approaches. Dietary studies have found that, 
230 
in healthy subjects, Faecalibacterium increased secondary to prebiotic supplementation, including, 
231 
inulin (48), raffinose (49), polydextrose (50), soluble corn fiber (50) high amylose maize starch (51), 
232 
resistant maltodextrin (52), and the pro-biotic, Bacillus coagulans (53). However, bipolar subjects have a 
233 
high incidence of comorbid gastrointestinal disorders, which are sensitive to the many of the above 
234 
supplementations so other dietary approaches should be investigated for this population. In a subset of 
235 
MANUSCRIPT
 
ACCEPTED
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
subjects from our current study we also have habitual dietary information published previously (54). 
236 
Leveraging these data we found significant positive associations between habitual intake of several 
237 
polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) and Feacalibacterium fractional representation and launched a PUFA 
238 
dietary intervention study, which may be much better tolerated by individuals with gastrointestinal 
239 
disorders. 
240 
 
241 
The current analysis is limited by the cross-sectional design, which prevents any causative conclusions. 
242 
We chose to use mean summary scores from the self-report scales, rather than the single scores 
243 
collected nearest the stool collection to minimize the acute variation inherent in these data. We justify 
244 
this approach since analysis of the questionnaire data reveals wide swings, in individual scores without 
245 
clear trajectories of increasing or decreasing significantly over time. Thus any given measure may 
246 
inaccurately reflect the burden of disease for individual subjects, whereas the regression to the mean of 
247 
data collected over time likely provides a better indicator. Furthermore, the temporal relationship 
248 
between changes in the microbiome composition and changes in clinical outcome measures or behavior 
249 
are unclear. There may or may not be substantial lag period (as there is for several pharmaceutical 
250 
treatments), if indeed there is a causal effect of the microbiome composition on psychiatric outcomes. 
251 
Research with repeated measured stool and self-report sampling pre- and post- intervention strategies, 
252 
including dietary approaches, are necessary to address this limitation of the current study.  
253 
 
254 
The current analysis is further limited by the inability to control for medication use and compliance. The 
255 
study was not designed to separate the effects of psychiatric medication use from the effects of disease 
256 
on the gut microbiome composition or its relationships with clinical outcomes. This was a naturalistic 
257 
study of individuals with bipolar disorder, relative to controls, and medication use was neither used as 
258 
inclusion or exclusion criteria. Therefore, substantial polypharmacy use by individuals with bipolar 
259 
MANUSCRIPT
 
ACCEPTED
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
disorder precludes us from drawing conclusions related to how psychiatric medication use may effect 
260 
the reported observations. Studies designed specifically to address this question are necessary.  
261 
 
262 
In summary, we found several OTUs, especially Faecalibacterium to associate with bipolar illness and 
263 
negatively associate with self-reported burden of disease measures in bipolar individuals, independent 
264 
sex, age and BMI. These data suggest that therapeutically increasing Faecalibacterium in bipolar patients 
265 
may be beneficial to reduce disease burden, however this hypothesis needs to be tested with the 
266 
appropriate study design.
