BACKGROUND AND AIM: Fecal microbiome disturbances are linked to different human diseases. In the case of obesity, gut
microbiota seems to play a role in the development of low-grade inﬂammation. The purpose of the present study was to identify
speciﬁc bacterial families and genera associated with an increased obesity-related inﬂammatory status, which would allow to build
a regression model for the prediction of the inﬂammatory status of obese and overweight subjects based on fecal microorganisms.
METHODS: A total of 361 volunteers from the Obekit trial (65 normal-weight, 110 overweight, and 186 obese) were classiﬁed
according to four variables: waist/hip ratio (≥0.86 for women and ≥1.00 for men), leptin/adiponectin ratio (LAR, ≥3.0 for women and
≥1.4 for men), and plasma C-reactive protein (≥2 mg/L) and TNF levels (≥0.85 pg/mL). An inﬂammation score was designed to
classify individuals in low (those subjects who did exceed the threshold value in 0 or 1 variable) or high inﬂammatory index (those
subjects who did exceed the threshold value in 2 or more variables). Fecal 16 S rRNA sequencing was performed for all participants,
and differential abundance analyses for family and genera were performed using the MicrobiomeAnalyst web-based platform.
RESULTS: Methanobacteriaceae, Christensenellaceae, Coriobacteriaceae, Biﬁdobacteriaceae, Catabacteriaceae, and
Dehalobacteriaceae families, and Methanobrevibacter, Eggerthella, Gemmiger, Anaerostipes, and Collinsella genera were
signiﬁcantly overrepresented in subjects with low inﬂammatory index. Conversely, Carnobacteriaceae, Veillonellaceae,
Pasteurellaceae, Prevotellaceae and Enterobacteriaceae families, and Granulicatella, Veillonella, Haemophilus, Dialister
Parabacteroides, Prevotella, Shigella, and Allisonella genera were more abundant in subjects with a high inﬂammatory index. A
regression model adjusted by BMI, sex, and age and including the families Coriobacteriaceae and Prevotellaceae and the genus
Veillonella was developed.
CONCLUSION: A microbiota-based regression model was able to predict the obesity-related inﬂammatory status (area under the
ROC curve = 0.8570 ± 0.0092 Harrell’s optimism-correction) and could be useful in the precision management of inﬂammobesity.

INTRODUCTION
Inﬂammation is an orchestrated immunologic reaction to diverse
harmful stimuli aimed to maintain cellular homeostasis and
recover damaged tissue [1]. In obesity, excessive calorie intake
and increased fat accumulation lead to a chronic, low-grade
inﬂammatory state via altered secretion of adipocytokines like
tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukins (IL-1, IL-6), C-reactive
protein (CRP), leptin and adiponectin, and the recruitment of
immune cells, including activated macrophages [2]. This sustained
condition is considered a key risk factor for developing many
diseases such as atherosclerosis, hypertension, type 2 diabetes
mellitus, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, metabolic syndrome, and
some cancers [3]. Hence, the characterization of factors inﬂuen-
cing obesity-related inﬂammation is essential for the search of
potential therapeutic targets aimed to mitigate exacerbated
inﬂammation.
In recent years, metagenomic analyses have demonstrated that
some alterations of the microbiome are linked to the development
of different human diseases [4]. In this context, gut microbiota
seems to play a critical role in the onset and progression of obesity-
related inﬂammation, where genetic/epigenetic and environmen-
tal factors may be involved [5]. Indeed, unbalanced changes in
microbiota proﬁle (dysbiosis) and changes in intestinal perme-
ability are identiﬁed as potential enhancers of inﬂammation in
obesity via lipopolysaccharide (LPS) release and toll-like receptor 4
(TLR4) activation [6]. Thus, LPS translocation triggers several classic
immune
pathways,
including
nuclear
factor-κB
(NF-κB)
and
subsequent persistent low-grade inﬂammatory responses [7].

Regarding the relationship between gut microbiota composi-
tion and inﬂammation in obesity, it has been reported that the
abundance of Biﬁdobacterium, Faecalibacterium, Ruminococcus,
and Prevotella genera were inversely associated with serum levels
of different pro-inﬂammatory cytokines, including CRP and IL-6 [8].
Moreover, lower gut microbial diversity was also related to higher
blood leukocyte counts and CRP concentrations [9]. Similarly,
individuals with lower bacterial richness were characterized by
having a more pronounced inﬂammatory phenotype compared
with subjects presenting high bacterial richness [10].
The purpose of the present study was to identify speciﬁc
bacterial families and genera associated with an increased
inﬂammatory status in obesity, which could allow to build a
microbiota-based regression model for the prediction of the
inﬂammatory status of obese and overweight subjects.
MATERIAL AND METHODS
Participants
This cross-sectional study was performed on 361 adult individuals from the
OBEKIT trial (registered code: NCT02737267) using baseline data. The study
included 65 normal-weight (18 male and 47 female), 110 overweight (30
male and 80 female), and 186 obese (62 male and 124 female) participants.
Study design, protocol characteristics, and inclusion and exclusion
criteria of this trial have been described elsewhere [11]. This study was
properly approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of
Navarra (Ref. 132/2015) in compliance with the ethical principles of the
Helsinki Declaration regarding medical research involving human subjects.
All participants provided written informed consent before inclusion in
the study.
Anthropometric measurements
BMI was calculated as the ratio between body weight and squared height
(kg/m2) and the BMI classiﬁcation criteria was following according to the
World Health Organization [12]. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry was
applied to estimate fat distribution following the company instructions
(DEXA, Lunar Prodigy, software version 6.0, Madison, WI, USA).
Waist circumference (cm) was measured on standing subjects at the end
of a natural breath, at the midpoint between the top of the iliac crest and
the lower rib on the midaxillary line; whereas hip circumference (cm) was
measured in the same conditions at the level of the widest circumference
over the great trochanters. Both circumferences were collected with
stretch-resistant tape at least twice. Waist/hip ratio (WHR) was estimated
by dividing waist circumference by hip circumference. Systolic and
diastolic blood pressures were measured with a sphygmomanometer
using the standardized criteria of the World Health Organization and the
International Society of Hypertension [13].
Biochemical determinations
Peripheral blood samples were drawn by venipuncture after a 12-h
overnight fast in a clinical setting. Serum was processed in an automatic
analyzer (Pentra C200, HORIBA Medical, Kyoto, Japan) using appropriate
kits for glucose, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol
(HDL-c), triglycerides, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate amino-
transferase (AST), and uric acid provided by the company (HORIBA
medical), as described previously [14]. The serum concentrations of leptin
(Mercodia,
Uppsala,
Sweden),
adiponectin
(BioVendor,
Brno,
Czech
Republic), CRP (Demeditec, Kiel, Germany), and TNF (R&D Systems,
Minneapolis, MN, USA) were measured using speciﬁc enzyme-linked
immunosorbent (ELISA) assays and read with an automated analyzer
system (Triturus, Grifols, Barcelona, Spain). Homeostatic model assessment
for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated according to the following
formula: fasting insulin (mU/mL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL))/405. The
Friedewald
formula
was
used
to
estimate
low-density
lipoprotein
cholesterol (LDL-c) levels.
Inﬂammation score
Participants
were
classiﬁed
according
to
four
inﬂammation-related
variables associated with obesity: WHR (≥0.86 for women and ≥1.00 for
men), the LAR (≥3.0 for women and ≥1.4 for men), and the circulating
levels of CRP (≥2 mg/L) and TNF (≥0.85 pg/mL). Cut-off values were
established based on combining the use of the median (so as not to
unbalance the low and high-risk groups of inﬂammation) with the choice
of values published by other relevant research groups. An inﬂammation
score was designed to classify individuals in low (those who did exceed the
threshold value in 0 or 1 variable) or high inﬂammatory index (those who
did exceed the threshold value in 2, 3, or 4 variables).
Metagenomic analyses
Fecal samples were collected using OMNIgene®•GUT kits (DNA Genotek,
Ottawa, ON, Canada), according to the supplier instructions. Bacterial DNA
was isolated with the QIAamp®DNA kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany)
following the manufacturer’s protocol. Metagenomic analyses have been
previously described [14]. Brieﬂy, the V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the
16 S rRNA gene were ampliﬁed by paired-end DNA sequencing in the
MiSeq System (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at the Servei de Genòmica i
Bioinformàtica (Autonomous University of Barcelona, Barcelona, Spain).
Taxonomy (including species, genus, family, order, class, and phylum) was
assigned using HITdb (https://github.com/microbiome/HITdb). All sequen-
cing data have been submitted to the NCBI. SRA repository under the
accession number PRJNA623853.
Comprehensive differential abundance analyses were performed
using
the
MicrobiomeAnalyst
web-based
platform
(https://www.
microbiomeanalyst.ca/) [15]. Gene abundance data were analyzed by
Marker Data Proﬁling (MDP). Data were ﬁltered by a minimum count of
4, 20% of prevalence in samples, and a low variance ﬁlter (maximum
percentage of samples to remove) of 20%, based on inter-quantile
range. Data were then normalized by the trimmed mean of M-values
(TMM). The differential abundance analysis method used for the
comparative analysis was the RNA-seq method, using the edgeR
(Empirical analysis for Digital Gene Expression in R) algorithm, with an
adjusted p value cutoff of 0.05.
Alpha diversity proﬁling between the low and high inﬂammation score
groups was calculated by determining the Shannon and Simpson indexes
using MicrobiomeAnalyst [15], considering all OTU counts.
Statistical analyses
The
normality
of
analyzed
variables
was
screened
with
the
Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Results were expressed as means ± SEM for
continuous variables and as numbers and percentages for categorical
variables. Statistical differences between groups were assessed by
Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test depending on the distribution
of data. Pearson’s correlation was performed to assess the association
between the variables. Statistical signiﬁcance was set on a p value <0.05.
In order to select the best logistic regression model to associate bacteria
with the inﬂammation score, three different methods were combined
using the Stata 16 program (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA; http://
www.stata.com): The “lars” module to perform least angle regression
solution [16]; the “vselect” module to perform a variable selection for linear
regression [17]; and the “swboot” module to perform bootstrap stepwise
linear or logistic regression models [18]. BMI, age, and sex were used as
covariates.
Stata
16
was
used
also
to
perform
Pearson
or
Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-ﬁt test, calibration plot of prediction
model performance, compute the area under the receiver operating
characteristic curve (ROC), and graph the curve. Harrel’s bootstrap method
was used to adjust for optimism/overﬁtting of the ROC curve based on a
modiﬁed script from Patrick Royston (https://www.stata.com/statalist/
archive/2008-10/msg01137.html). Mean-variance inﬂation factor (VIF) and
condition number were determined as measures of collinearity between
variables.

Differences in the abundance of bacterial families and genera
according to the four inﬂammation variables
We initially studied the relative abundance of bacterial families
and genera differentially distributed in the low and high groups
for the four inﬂammation variables. Table S1 summarizes the taxa
associated with WHR, TNF, CRP, and LAR. LAR and WHR were
related to more families and genera than the pro-inﬂammatory
proteins. Some taxa were extremely statistically signiﬁcant, such as
Carnobacteriaceae and Granulicatella with WHR and LAR (pro-
inﬂammatory), Eggerthella and Gemmiger with WHR, and Metha-
nobacteriaceae with TNF and CRP (anti-inﬂammatory).
In order to detect those taxa speciﬁc of each variable, together
with those commonly found in more than two variables, we
performed a Venn diagram comparing the obtained results
(Fig. 1).
Methanobacteriaceae family, and also the Methanobrevibacter
genera was found to be associated with the low level of the four
inﬂammation variables. On the contrary, the genus Granulicatella
was found to be associated with the high-level group of the four
variables. Different families and genera were found to be
associated with reduced levels of the different inﬂammatory
variables.
Thus,
Coriobacteriaceae
and
Christensenellaceae
families were more abundant in individuals with reduced levels
of WHR
and LAR, together
with the genera Anaerostipes,
Eggerthella, and Gemmiger. These two latter were also associated
with reduced TNF and CRP concentrations, respectively. Moreover,
Holdemania and Collinsella were more abundant in individuals
with reduced levels of TNF and WHR.
On the contrary, individuals with higher levels of WHR and LAR
exhibited an increased proportion of the family Carnobacteriaceae
and the genera Veillonella, Rothia, and Shigella. This latter was also
associated with higher levels of CRP. The genera Parabacteroides
and Adlercreutzia were more abundant in individuals with higher
levels of LAR and TNF. Surprisingly, the genus Bacteroides was
associated with higher LAR but reduced TNF levels.
Differences in the abundance of bacterial families and genera
according to the inﬂammation score
Considering these results, we analysed the relative abundance
of those families and genera differentially distributed between
the high and the low groups of the inﬂammatory score. First,
we observed that the bacterial alpha diversity of those
individuals with the high inﬂammatory score was signiﬁcantly
reduced in comparison with individuals with a low inﬂamma-
tory score, as it is demonstrated by signiﬁcantly lower Shannon
(p value: 0.0185) and Simpson (p value: 0.0439) diversity

described in Material and Methods. The best model obtained
(Table 2) presents a Pseudo R2 of 0.3343 and p < 0.0001. BMI, sex,
and age were used as adjustment variables. In the model, it is
observed that the family Coriobacteriaceae has a protective effect
on the inﬂammation score, whereas Veillonella and Prevotellaceae
are associated with a pro-inﬂammatory effect.

was Chi-square 6.92 and p = 0.55. The calibration plot for this
multivariate model is included (Fig. 4B).

DISCUSSION
Excessive visceral fat accumulation is closely related to a chronic
low-grade inﬂammatory state [19]. In this research, an inﬂamma-
tion score was calculated using different markers, comprising WHR
ratio, the plasma levels of CRP and TNF, and the LAR ratio, which
represent different inﬂammatory mechanisms related to obesity
comorbidities. For instance, WHR has been extensively used as a
surrogate to measure visceral adiposity and a predictor for
cardiovascular outcomes [20]. LAR has been associated with a
higher risk of metabolic syndrome derangement, [21]. CRP is an
acute‐phase inﬂammatory marker that has shown to be an
independent predictor of depression and death from cardiovas-
cular incidents [22]. Finally, TNF is a pro-inﬂammatory cytokine
that
promotes
insulin
resistance
[23].
Of
note,
no
strong
correlations between these parameters were found (Fig. S1),
avoiding bias from scoring.
Accordingly, abdominal obesity (measured by waist/hip circum-
ferences) has been associated with greater blood levels of CRP, TNF,
and leptin in several studies, linking obesity with an increased risk
of developing cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, and
type 2 diabetes [24]. Moreover, this inﬂammatory environment is
characterized by a decreased expression of beneﬁcial anti-
inﬂammatory
adipokines
with
a
protective
effect,
including
adiponectin [25]. Indeed, similar inﬂammation scores using classical
circulating cytokines/adipokines have been identiﬁed as important
predictors of certain obesity phenotypes involving low-grade
inﬂammation in some populations [26, 27], but not in others [28].
Cumulative
evidence
identiﬁes
microbiota
dysbiosis
and
reduced bacterial richness as key players in triggering obesity-
related systemic inﬂammation and metabolic dysfunction [29]. In
this study, we demonstrated that the bacterial alpha diversity of
those individuals with high inﬂammatory score was signiﬁcantly
reduced in comparison with individuals with low inﬂammatory
score (Fig. S2). Moreover, the family Prevotellaceae and the genus
Veillonella were associated with a pro-inﬂammatory phenotype,
whereas
the
family
Coriobacteriaceae
seemed
to
exert
a
protective
effect.
Remarkably,
these
taxa
were
exclusively
associated with the inﬂammation score and were not detected
by the four inﬂammation-related variables at the individual level
(Fig. 3). In this context, animal and human studies support a causal
role of the family Prevotellaceae in promoting low-grade chronic
systemic inﬂammation through activation and dissemination of
inﬂammatory mediators at mucosal sites involving antigen-
presenting cells [30]. Also, the relative abundance of the bacterial
family Prevotellaceae and markers of low-grade inﬂammation
were higher in obese pregnant women compared to their
overweight pregnant counterparts [31]. Moreover, Prevotellaceae

reported to be abundant in healthy phenotypes but depleted in

Furthermore,
an
association
between the abundance of Coriobacteriaceae and the risk of
developing metabolic syndrome was found in young patients with
obesity [34].
Emerging predictive models in medicine using precision biomar-
kers and integrative computational approaches are allowing the
generation of meaningful clinical and translating information to the
implementation
of
novel
diagnosis,
prognosis,
and
treatment
strategies for a wide range of diseases [35]. In this study, a
multivariate logistic regression model based on microbiome and
phenotype data was able to predict the obesity-related inﬂammation
status in this sample. Of note, it has been reported that dysbiotic gut
microbiome in combination with unhealthy diets predicted the
development of obesity and concomitant inﬂammation features in
children [36]. Also, the functional metagenomic analysis identiﬁed 16
different taxa that might explain the observed metabolic alterations in
Mexican women with obesity, [37]. Similarly, clinical trials and larger
modeling studies have shown the predictive capacity of baseline
microbiota for clinical response to dietary counseling in obesity and
inﬂammatory bowel syndrome, although some inconsistencies have
been reported [38]. Nonetheless, these ﬁndings highlight the
importance of incorporating gut microbiota information in persona-
lized nutrition models within the context of research and clinical
practice [38]. In this regard, a species-level gut microbiome index for
predicting the likelihood of disease independent of the clinical
diagnosis has been recently proposed [39].
The strengths of this investigation include the screening of robust
statistical approaches to select the best multiple logistic regression
model explaining obesity-related inﬂammation status using micro-
biome data. Also, the inﬂammatory index was constructed with
classical anthropometric and laboratory measurements in the clinical
setting. On the other hand, some drawbacks of this research included
a relatively small sample analyzed, the enrollment of only European
individuals, and the lack of dietary data analysis. Other limitations
comprise the arbitrary choice of the criteria used for building the
inﬂammation score as well as the need to separate the role of
microbiota from that of obesity per se. Thus, further studies
considering these factors are required. Moreover, the analysis of the
interplay of additional precision parameters inﬂuencing obesity-
related inﬂammatory phenotypes such as epigenetic signatures [40],
metabolomics proﬁles [41], and host genomic variants [42] is
warranted.
In conclusion, a multiple logistic regression model based on
differences in gut microbiota composition is able to predict the
inﬂammatory degree associated with obesity. Although caution
should be exercised, this knowledge may contribute to the
prevention, prognosis, and management of the obesity-related
inﬂammation
status
through
innovative
precision
medicine
approaches targeting the microbiota. Furthermore, these results
highlight several bacterial families and genera whose study should
deserve more attention in the next years for precision nutrition
management.
