a b s t r a c t
Increasing evidence suggests that elevational gradients of soil fungal richness are highly variable, but few
studies have examined how diversity components of each guild contribute to overall fungal diversity.
Here, we aimed to disentangle the relationships between total, saprotrophic, and ectomycorrhizal fungal
richness, and environmental factors across elevational gradients in cool-temperate montane forests. We
observed that total and saprotrophic richness decreased but ectomycorrhizal richness increased with
increase in elevation. Elevational range size and nestedness analyses illustrated that saprotrophic and
ectomycorrhizal source communities were located at lower and higher elevations, respectively. The
observed total and saprotrophic richness were directly inﬂuenced by soil properties and indirectly
inﬂuenced by climate and plant communities. Ectomycorrhizal fungal richness was affected by climate
and the dominance of ectomycorrhizal trees. We highlight that two directional sourceesink dynamics
lead to opposite elevational patterns between saprotrophic and ectomycorrhizal fungal richness, shaping
the variation in elevational richness gradients.

1. Introduction
Identifying how soil fungal communities are assembled and
their response to environmental change is a crucial challenge for
soil ecology and biodiversity science (Peay et al., 2016). In partic-
ular, elevational gradients have been widely investigated as a nat-
ural laboratory for understanding the relationships between soil
fungal communities and environmental factors (Bahram et al.,
2012; Shi et al., 2014; Peay et al., 2017). However, elevational gra-
dients of soil fungal richness are highly variable, including patterns
of monotonous decline (Bahram et al., 2012; Nottingham et al.,
2018), unimodal distributions (Shi et al., 2014), or increase in
richness with elevation (Peay et al., 2017). Several studies of
changes in soil fungal richness on latitudinal gradients, by contrast,
revealed distinct patterns among functional guilds (Tedersoo et al.,
2014; Wang et al., 2019). Therefore, not all fungal groups may
correlate with elevation in the same manner, potentially resulting
in guild bias in the relationships between elevation and soil fungal
richness. Studies on elevational diversity gradients have typically
treated fungal communities as a whole (Peay et al., 2017) or focused
on a speciﬁc group (e.g., mycorrhizal fungi; Bahram et al., 2012; Shi
et al., 2014). Although including soil fungi of extremely different
functional guilds in the same analysis may hinder the detection of
ecologically meaningful patterns, no previous study has examined
the contribution of guild-level diversity to overall richness patterns
along elevational gradients.
Fungal ecological studies have taken diverse approaches to
classify soil fungal communities to better understand the function
of individual species as well as the whole community (Tedersoo
et al., 2014; Nguyen et al., 2016a; Wang et al., 2019). A functional
guild is a group that exploits the same type of resources in a similar
manner (Simberloff and Dayan, 1991), therefore distinguishing
fungal guilds provides knowledge of the fungal community
composition based on trophic strategies rather than species iden-
tities (Nguyen et al., 2016b). Saprotrophic (SAP) and ectomycor-
rhizal (ECM) fungi represent two major guilds in forest soils and
