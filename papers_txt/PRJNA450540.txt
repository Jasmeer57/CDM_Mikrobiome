OBJECTIVES:
Crohn’s disease (CD) is a chronic relapsing-remitting gut inflammatory disorder with a heterogeneous
unpredictable course. Dysbiosis occurs in CD; however, whether microbial dynamics in quiescent CD
are instrumental in increasing the risk of a subsequent flare remains undefined.
METHODS:
We analyzed the long-term dynamics of microbial composition in a prospective observational cohort of
patients with quiescent CD (45 cases, 217 samples) over 2 years or until clinical flare occurred, aiming
to identify whether changes in the microbiome precede and predict clinical relapse. Machine learning
was used to prioritize microbial and clinical factors that discriminate between relapsers and
nonrelapsers in the quiescent phase.
RESULTS:
Patients with CD in clinical, biomarker, and mucosal remission showed significantly reduced microbial
richness and increased dysbiosis index compared with healthy controls. Of the 45 patients with
quiescent CD, 12 (27%) flared during follow-up. Samples in quiescent patients preceding flare showed
significantly reduced abundance of Christensenellaceae and S24.7, and increased abundance of
Gemellaceae compared with those in remission throughout. A composite flare index was associated
with a subsequent flare. Notably, higher individualized microbial instability in the quiescent phase was
associated with a higher risk of a subsequent flare (hazard ratio 11.32, 95% confidence interval 3–42,
P 5 0.0035) using two preflare samples. Importantly, machine learning prioritized the flare index and
the intrapersonal instability over clinical factors to best discriminate between relapsers and
nonrelapsers.
DISCUSSION:
Individualized microbial variations in quiescent CD significantly increase the risk of future exacerbation
and may provide a model to guide personalized preemptive therapy intensification.

INTRODUCTION
Crohn’s disease (CD) has no cure, and it is a chronic relapsing-
remitting disease with an unpredictable course (1). Cross-
sectional studies have revealed dysbiosis, or deviation from
what is considered a healthy microbiota, in the gut of patients
with CD. Dysbiosis was primarily noted at the community level,
comprising a general decrease in alpha diversity with genus- and
clade-speciﬁc enrichment and depletion (2–4). However, it is
hitherto poorly deﬁned whether these observations reﬂect a pri-
mary process, which contributes to pathogenesis, or are they
secondary to a survival advantage in the inﬂammatory micro-
environment (5). We have previously shown that ileal dysbiosis
exists in CD in the absence of overt local ileal inﬂammation (2)
and that dysbiosis in early-onset treatment-naive patients is very
similar to that of adults with long-standing disease on medi-
cations (6), indicating that dysbiosis is not just an eﬀect of
treatment or local inﬂammation. Moreover, murine studies have
shown that gut dysbiosis from patients with CD augments the
host’s proinﬂammatory immune responses (7), thereby possibly
playing a role in disease pathogenesis.
Disease-speciﬁc changes may be patient speciﬁc, and it was
already suggested that longitudinal, patient-focused studies will
provide an important step toward understanding microbiome-
related diseases and their roles in personalized medicine (4). It has

METHODS
Study design
Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Sheba Research
Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained. This
was a prospective observational cohort study of adult patients
(aged .18 years) with quiescent CD involving the small bowel
(either L1 small bowel or L3 ileocolonic CD by the Montreal
classiﬁcation). To be included, patients had to have quiescent
stable disease for 3–24 months before inclusion. Stable disease
was deﬁned as being corticosteroid-free and having clinical re-
mission as per the Crohn’s Disease Activity Index (CDAI) of
,150 or experiencing mild disease symptoms (CDAI ,220) not
necessitating alteration of treatment or its dose in the last 3
months before inclusion. Patients were excluded if they were
unable/unwilling to provide informed consent, had severe
comorbidities, were pregnant, or if they had isolated colonic CD.
Clinical observations from this study at study inception were
previously reported (11). A subcohort of 45 patients of the orig-
inal cohort of 61 patients with quiescent CD was included in this
analysis, as allocation of the fecal samples for microbial analyses
started already after study initiation. The primary outcome was
the occurrence of a clinical ﬂare, deﬁned as an increase of .70
points on the CDAI from baseline and a CDAI of .150, or the
need for rescue medication for CD necessitated by disease
worsening as determined by physician global assessment. For
simplicity, we categorized patients into either clinical remission
(quiescent) or active disease. In addition to clinical assessment,
fecal calprotectin (B ¨UHLMANN Laboratories AG, Basel, Swit-
zerland), fecal microbial characterization, and C-reactive protein
were measured routinely every 3 months. Patients underwent
video capsule endoscopy every 6 months. In patients with isolated
small bowel disease (L1), SB-III capsule (Given Imaging,
Yokneam, Israel) was used. In patients with small bowel and
colonic CD (L3), a colonic capsule (PillCam colon capsule; Given
Imaging, Yokneam, Israel) was administered. Mucosal inﬂam-
mation was quantiﬁed using the Lewis score (LS), and mucosal
healing was deﬁned as an LS of ,135 (12). Fecal samples for
parallel fecal calprotectin measurement and microbial charac-
terization were self-collected in sterile containers, brought to the
laboratory within 24 hours, and frozen immediately in 280 °C
until DNA extraction. Samples obtained within 6 weeks of anti-
biotics exposure were excluded. Samples from this cohort of patients
in remission were compared with samples obtained from a diﬀerent
local cohort of 17 patients with CD with active inﬂammation and 22
healthy controls (Table 1). The inclusion of healthy controls and
active CD groups are used as 2 extreme references for clinical and
microbial variations. The caring physicians were blinded to the
microbial composition analyses at all time points.
Microbial characterization and statistics
DNA extraction and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) ampliﬁ-
cation of the 16S rRNA gene V4 variable region using Illumina-
adapted universal primers 515F/806R (13) was conducted as
previously described (14–16). Paired-end reads (175 bp) were
processed in QIIME 1.9.1 (17). Sequences that passed quality
ﬁltering were clustered into phylotypes (operational taxonomic
units, OTUs) at 97% sequence identity using a uclust-based (18)
closed-reference protocol, against the Greengenes August 2013
database (19). Samples had amedian of12,638 (average of14,258)
paired-end sequences. Faith’s phylogenetic diversity (20) was
used as a measure of microbial richness, or within-sample di-
versity after samples was randomly rariﬁed to 2000 sequences.
Unweighted UniFrac distance was used as a measure of b di-
versity, or between-sample diversity (21). All downstream data
analyses were performed in R. All zeroes were replaced with
a minimal value of the lowest taxonomic relative abundance in
the data set to account for the discrete nature of sequencing and
enable log transform. R package randomForest (22) version 4.6-12
was used, with the out-of-box estimate of error rate, and each patient
was sampled once to avoid bias, using average values from the entire
patients’ available samples for variables that change over time.
Mann-Whitney U and Yates’ chi-square tests were used to test the
statistical diﬀerences with Bonferroni correction as needed. Spear-
man’s rank correlation was used to assess monotonic relationships
betweencontentiousvariables.Ascatterplotisshownforcorrelation
results, with a local polynomial regression ﬁtting calculated by
weighted least squares method and a 0.95 conﬁdence interval using
at-based approximation. P , 0.05 was considered statistically sig-
niﬁcant. R MaAsLin (Multivariate Association with Linear Models)
package (23) version 0.0.5 was used for comparison between deﬁned
clinical groups. Control, active, and remission CD patients were
compared, using all samples in the cohort. Samples taken during
remission from patients who subsequently ﬂared were compared to
those who remained in remission. For both analysis, a Q value
thresholdof,0.1wasused,whilecontrollingfortheeﬀectofsex,age,
bodymassindex(BMI),andsmoking.Rareaunderthecurve(AUC)
package version 0.3.0 was used to calculate the receiver operating
characteristic (ROC) curve and the ROC AUC. Hazard ratios for
survival without a ﬂare per individualized microbial instability was
calculated using the Youden index of 0.502.
Data and material availability. The study data sets were de-
posited at the National Center for Biotechnology Information as
BioProject PRJNA450540.

DISCUSSION
We capture the long-term dynamic behavior of the gut micro-
biota in quiescent CD and identify microbial factors that are
associated with a subsequent ﬂare. Speciﬁc changes at the taxa
(“ﬂare index”) and at the community (instability) levels were
found to precede clinical ﬂare and to predict disease exacerbation.
We further emphasize the instability at the microbial community
as a predicator of a future ﬂare as taxonomic changes are highly
variable between studies, populations, and patients (4). It has
already been shown that in patients with CD, the microbiome
composition varies more than in healthy individuals (10). Similar
to these results, we noted an association between the individual
microbial instability and lower diversity. However, we expand
these observations and show that within patients with quiescent
CD, higher intrapersonal microbial ﬂuctuations or instability
increase the likelihood of a subsequent ﬂare with a high sensitivity
of 89% and negative predictive value of 95%. In a broader view,
these results may imply that microbiome changes during the
quiescent phase may be the cause or an associated reporter of
other factors upstream of the inﬂammatory process preﬂare that
subsequently lead to a disease ﬂare.
We have identiﬁed speciﬁc taxa that were associated with
a consequent ﬂare. Most showed reduced abundance in patients
with a subsequent ﬂare, which is in correlation with the overall
lower microbial richness observed before ﬂares. Three taxa in
particular were predictive of ﬂare. The Christensenellaceae family
of the Clostridiales order and S24.7 family of the Bacteroidales
order had lower abundance in patients with a subsequent ﬂare.
Christensenellaceae was previously observed to be missing from
active CD gut microbiome (33), and S24.7 abundance was re-
duced in experimental colitic mice after the onset of colitis (34).
The Gemellaceae family showed higher abundance in patients
with a subsequent ﬂare and was also previously associated with
CD (3,35). These three taxa are all part of the MDI (3). However,
the ﬂare index outperformed the MDI in predicting ﬂares in our
cohort, as the MDI was not associated with a subsequent ﬂare.
Interestingly, when prioritizing clinical, demographic, and
microbial factors in a supervised learning RF algorithm to predict
a subsequent ﬂare, the top contributing factors (Figure 4c) were the
“ﬂare index” and the intrapersonal instability. The following con-
tributing factors were BMI, LS, and microbial richness. Importantly,
C-reactive protein and treatment exposure that were shown to be
signiﬁcantly diﬀerent in the univariate analyses (Table 1) were not
within the top 5 contributing factors. The strong contribution of the
BMI is consistent with the signiﬁcantly higher BMI values seen here
in patients who ﬂared compared with patients who remained in
remission (Table 1) and with older studies connecting higher BMI
with unfavorable disease outcome (36–38).
Limitations of this study include the relatively modest cohort
size, which is nonetheless among the largest CD cohorts ever to
undergo comprehensive prospective follow-up and microbial
characterization in patients is remission. We included only
patients who had stable disease for 3–24 months before inclusion.
Thus, whether the results can be extended to patients with CD
with longer duration ofremission remains to be proven. Strengths
of the study include its prospective nature with a median of 5
longitudinal fecal samples per patient that were submitted in
parallel to both microbial characterization and fecal calprotectin
measurement and the focus on only patients with CD rather than
ulcerative colitis and CD altogether. Future studies will be needed
to further validate these results in an independent cohort, whereas
supplemental shotgun metagenomic and metatranscriptomic
and mechanistic studies in murine models may provide further
insights into the role of the microbiome in eliciting CD ﬂare.
In summary, the optimal monitoring strategy for predicating
CD ﬂare in CD is undeﬁned. Greater understanding of personal
pathways driving disease exacerbations is key to proactively ad-
just therapeutic approaches to reduce personal and global disease
burden. Our results support the idea that microbial changes exist
in the absence of clinical, systemic, and mucosal inﬂammation, as
