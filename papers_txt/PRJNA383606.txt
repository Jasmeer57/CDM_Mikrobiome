Mucosa-associated microbiota signature in colorectal cancer
R. Gao1,2 & C. Kong1,2 & L. Huang1,2 & H. Li1,2 & X. Qu1,2 & Z. Liu3 & P. Lan3 & J. Wang3 &
H. Qin1,2
Received: 19 April 2017 /Accepted: 22 May 2017
# Springer-Verlag Berlin Heidelberg 2017
Abstract The aim of this study was to explore the gut micro-
biota profiles of colorectal cancer (CRC) patients and to ex-
amine the relationship between gut microbiota and other key
molecular factors involved in CRC tumorigenesis. In this
study, a 16S rDNA sequencing platform was used to identify
possible differences in the microbiota signature between CRC
and adjacent normal mucosal tissue. Differences in the micro-
biota composition in different anatomical colorectal tumor
sites and their potential association with KRAS mutation were
also explored. In this study, the number of Firmicutes and
Actinobacteria decreased, while the number of Fusobacteria
increased in the gut of CRC patients. In addition, at the genus
level, Fusobacterium was identified as the key contributor to
CRC tumorigenesis. In addition, a different distribution of gut
microbiota in ascending and descending colon cancer samples
was observed. Lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis-associated
microbial genes were enriched in tumor tissues. Our study
suggests that specific mucosa-associated microbiota signature
and function are significantly changed in the gut of CRC pa-
tients, which may provide insight into the progression of
CRC. These findings could also be of value in the creation
of new prevention and treatment strategies for this type of
cancer.
Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common causes of
cancer-associated mortality in developed countries [1, 2]. The
estimated numbers of new CRC cases and deaths are over
132,000 and 49,000, respectively, in the USA [1]. The etiolo-
gy of CRC is multifactorial and is associated with mutations in
many established oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes,
such as mutations in the K-ras and p53 genes, as well as with
a panel of risk factors, including family history, high-fat diet,
red meat consumption, and gut microbiota composition [3–7].
In recent years, due to the rise of high-throughput sequencing
methodology, gut microbiota that play a vital role in gut ho-
meostasis have attracted wide attention and have been exten-
sively investigated, which has resulted in the accelerated iden-
tification of many bacteria closely related to CRC [8–13].
Gut microbiota are defined as a community consisting of
various bacteria that colonize the surface and cavities of the
human body. The estimated number of bacteria residing in/on
the human body is nearly 100-fold higher than the number of
human body cells [14]. Many factors have been identified to
affect the composition of gut microbiota, such as antibiotics,
alcohol consumption, diet, race, and geographical site.
Antibiotics can also significantly lower the microbial diversity
and downregulate the immunity of an organism [15]. Alcohol
has been reported to change the microbiota composition at the
phylum level in rodent models and human studies [16]. Diet
patterns, which had been widely explored by numerous pro-
spective cohort and case–control studies, are also major con-
founding factors in shaping the gut microbiota, and studies
have shown that microbiota composition patterns can even

and pathophysiological processes associated with a number of
human diseases, including type II diabetes, obesity, inflamma-

[24–28]. Evidence from germ-free and gnotobiotic mice
models confirmed that the presence of microbiota is of vital
importance for proper functioning of the mucosal barrier, as
well as the immune system as a whole [29].
To understand how microbiota contribute to the progres-
sion of diseases, more effort must be devoted to examine host–
microbiota interactions. Previous studies have shown that gut
dysbiosis is involved in the progression of gastrointestinal
tract diseases, especially CRC. Notably, Fusobacterium and
Streptococcus have been associated with an increased risk of
CRC and have been reported to stimulate a cascade of reac-
tions that could potentially be of diagnostic utility [11, 14,
30–35]. In addition, microRNA from fecal samples were rec-
ognized as a host mediator that can shape the gut microbiota
landscape [36]. Furthermore, Tahara and colleagues reported
an association between carcinogenetic abundance of
Fusobacterium and gene mutations in CRC [37].
Specifically, a high abundance of Fusobacterium was associ-
ated with CIMP status, wild-type p53, and MSI in colon tumor
tissue. Nevertheless, the association between various factors
and gut microbiota signature remain not fully understood, and
more research is needed to establish its exact role in the
etiopathology of CRC.
Given these thoughts, we decided to investigate the
mucosa-associated microbiota in colorectal tumors and adja-
cent normal mucosal tissues at various anatomical sites in the
colon and rectum using the Illumina sequencing platform. The
relationship between the host gene mutation status and gut
microbiota was also explored in this study.
Materials and methods
Sample collection
This was a prospective investigation designed to study the
microbiota in tissue samples of CRC patients. Tissue samples
including tumor and adjacent normal mucosal tissue (more
than 5 cm away from the tumor) were collected from 65 pa-
tients with CRC during surgery from Tissue Banks, Shanghai
Tenth People’s Hospital, Tongji University, and the Sixth
Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University. Obtained tissue
samples were divided into aliquots and snap-frozen in liquid
nitrogen until further use. Patients exposed to antibiotics and
probiotics within 4 weeks, acute diarrhea, confirmed simple
adenoma or polys, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), or irri-
table bowel syndrome were excluded from the study. Blood
samples taken from CRC patients were centrifuged at
3000 rpm for 10 min to extract the serum. All tumor markers,
including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), alpha-fetoprotein
(AFP), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), CA 125, and CA15-3,
and other routine blood tests were performed in the clinical
laboratory. The clinical parameters of patients, such as body
mass index (BMI), age, gender, and disease history, were also
recorded. The study was approved by the Shanghai Tenth
People’s Hospital and the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun
Yat-sen University Ethic Committees. Written informed con-
sent was obtained from all patients.
Gene mutation detection
In this study, the most common somatic mutations in exons 2,
3, and 4 of the K-ras gene were examined in all 130 samples,
including tumor and normal tissue samples. The DNA used in
this test was extracted from tumor tissue samples, and muta-
tions were detected using the AmoyDx KRAS Mutation
Detection Kit (AmoyDx, Xiamen, China). All analyses were
performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.
Briefly, extracted DNA was diluted to 1.5 ng/μL, and 4.2 μL
enzyme mixture was added to each DNA sample, as well as to
the positive and negative control samples. Next, 5 μL of each
obtained mixture was transferred to a separate polymerase
chain reaction (PCR) tube, and a real-time PCR (ABI 7500,
Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) was immediately
performed. The cycling conditions were 95 °C for 5 min,
followed by 5 cycles of 95 °C for 25 s, 64 °C for 20 s, and
72 °C for 20 s. After the initial 5 cycles, the next 31 cycles
consisted of 93 °C for 25 s, 60 °C for 35 s, and 72 °C for 20 s.
KRAS mutation status was determined according to the cut-
off values defined in the kit instructions.
Whole genome sequencing
In this study, whole genome sequencing was performed in
20 samples (10 colorectal tumors and 10 adjacent normal tis-
sues) of the 130 samples. DNA was extracted from frozen
tissue samples using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN,
Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturers’ instruc-
tions and was quantified using the Qubit Fluorometer
(Invitrogen, Life technologies, Grand Island, NY, USA). A
total of 0.5 μg of DNA per sample was used as input material
for the DNA library preparations. A sequencing library was
generated using TruSeq Nano DNA HT Sample Prep Kit
(Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), following manufacturer’s
recommendations, and index codes were added to each sam-
ple. Briefly, the genomic DNA sample was fragmented by
sonication to a size of 350 bp. Next, DNA fragments were

end-polished, A-tailed, and ligated with the full-length adapter
for Illumina sequencing, followed by further PCR amplifica-
tion. After the PCR products were purified (AMPure XP sys-
tem, Beckman Coulter Inc., USA), the libraries were analyzed
for size distribution using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer
(Agilent, USA) and quantified by real-time PCR. Clustering
of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot Cluster
Generation System using the HiSeqX PE Cluster Kit v2.5
(Illumina), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.
After cluster generation, DNA libraries were sequenced on
the Illumina HiSeq platform, and 150-bp paired-end reads
were generated.
DNA extraction and 16S rRNA gene pyrosequencing
The DNA from tumor and adjacent normal mucosal tis-
sue were isolated using an OMEGA-soil DNA Kit
(Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA), according to
the manufacturer’s instructions. Next, amplification of
the V4 conserved region of bacterial 16S rRNA was
conducted using primers 515F (5′ →3′ GTGCCAGC
MGCCGCGG) and 907R (5′ →3′ CCGTCAAT
TCMTTTRAGTTT), which were labeled with a 12-bp
error-correcting barcode in triplicate. Twenty-microliter
reactions were amplified under the following conditions:
94 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 63 °C for
1 min and 72 °C for 1 min, with a final extension at
72 °C for 10 min. Finally, an AxyPrep DNA Gel
Extraction kit (Axygen, Tewksbury, MA, USA) was
used for gel purification after combined replicated
PCR reactions (5 Prime, Inc., Gaithersburg, MD,
USA), followed by another purification step with
AMPure beads and quantification with the PicoGreen
DNA assay (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), before
library pooling. Pyrosequencing was conducted in the
MiSeq system using a paired-end 2 × 300-bp reagent
kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).
Bioinformatics analysis
The microbiota-associated data obtained from the
Illumina platform were optimized, and paired reads
were merged as a sequence according to the overlap
of PE reads. The quality of the reads and the result
of the merger were filtrated as follows: bases lower
than 20 on the reads tail were filtered; the minimum
overlap was 10 bp when merging the paired reads; the
maximum mismatch ratio was 0.2 in the overlap and
the maximum barcode and primer mismatch number
were set as 0 and 2, respectively. Bioinformatics anal-
ysis was performed using high-quality sequences. Raw
DNA sequence data were analyzed by QIIME (version
1.17, http://qiime.org). Then, selected sequences were
clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) with
USEARCH (version 7.1, http://drive5.com/uparse/),
with a threshold similarity of 97%. Chimera
sequences derived from the PCR amplification were
deleted using UCHIME (version 4.2.40, http://drive5.
com/usearch/manual/uchime_algo.html), and the final
representative OTUs were obtained and used for the
next step. Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities
by Reconstruction of Unobserved States (PICRUSt)
was used to predict the gut microbiota function [38].
For whole genome sequencing, clean data were obtained
after filtering adapter, low-quality reads, and reads with a pro-
portion of N > 10%. Reads were aligned to the reference
human genome (UCSC hg19) using the Burrows–Wheeler
Aligner [39–41]. Next, the Picard and Genome Analysis
Toolkit (GATK) methods were adopted for duplicate removal,
local realignment, and base quality score recalibration, and to
give quality statistics, such as for mapped reads, mean map-
ping quality, and mean coverage [42, 43]. Finally, the GATK
HaplotypeCaller was used for single nucleotide variation
(SNV) indel analysis. Variants were annotated using the
ANNOVAR software tool [44]. Annotations for mutation
function (including frameshift insertion/deletion, non-
frameshift insertion/deletion, synonymous SNV,
nonsynonymous SNV, stopgain, and stoploss), mutation loca-
tion (such as exonic, intronic, splicing, upstream, downstream,
UTR3, UTR5, etc.), and amino acid changes were performed
using 1000 Genomes Project data and a dbSNP reference
number [45–47]. Somatic mutations in tumors were compared
to the status of matched normal tissue, and their function was
annotated using the MuTect software [48]. Control-FREEC
software was used for calling and annotating copy number
variations, such as gain, loss, or copy-neutral loss of hetero-
zygosity [49, 50]. Structural variations were identified using
BreakDancer, including inversions, intra-chromosomal trans-
locations, and inter-chromosomal translocations [51].
Data access
The 16S sequence information in this study has been submit-
ted to the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA), with acces-
sion number SRP104334.
Statistical analysis
All clinical data associations were analyzed by Student’s t-test
(SPSS 22.0, IBM) or the Mann–Whitney U-test (GraphPad
Prism 7.00, USA), where appropriate. The microbiota-
associated figures were generated by R software [52].
Spearman correlations were calculated to evaluate the associ-
ation between the host and microbiota indexes. All data with
two-sided p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results
Clinicopathological status of patients
A total of 65 CRC patients, including 35 males and 30 fe-
males, were enrolled in this study. The diagnosis of CRC
was confirmed by two independent pathologists. The average
age of the patients was 63.89 years for males and 63.08 years
for females. The BMI was 23.05 and 22.50 kg/m2 for the male
and female patients, respectively. Hypertension history was
recorded in 21.54% (14/65) of male patients and in 16.92%
(11/65) of female patients. The majority of tumors included in
the study were located in the ascending or sigmoid colon. In
69.23% (45/65) of the patients, a K-ras mutation was detected
in the tumor tissue. Concentrations of the total protein, hemo-
globin, platelet, and leukocytes were similar between the two
genders. However, the albumin concentration was lower in the
male than female patients. In addition, tumor markers, such as
CEA, AFP, CA19-9, CA125, and CA15-3, were determined
and their mean and median values did not differ significantly
between genders. Detailed clinicopathological characteristics
of the CRC patients included in the study are presented in
Table 1.
Microbiota alterations in the colorectal tissues
A total of 4,871,715 valid sequences (382 bp on average) were
created based on 130 samples. After data filtration, 355,855
reads and 237 OTUs per sample were identified. Up to
61.96% of the OTUs were shared between the two groups,
and 512 and 474 OTUs were specific to the tumor and adja-
cent normal mucosal tissue-associated microbiota, respective-
ly (Fig. 1a). To obtain the richness and diversity characteriza-
tion of the tissue-associated community in cancer and adjacent
normal tissue, we used the ACE and Chao indexes, as well as
the Shannon and Simpson indexes. However, no statistically
significant differences in these indexes were observed be-
tween samples. We then compared the microbiota composi-
tion in the tumors and paired adjacent normal mucosal tissue.
At the phylum level, the relative abundances of Bacteroidetes
(27.66% vs. 23.82%, p > 0.05), Proteobacteria (14.54% vs.
12.74%, p > 0.05), and Fusobacteria (8.80% vs. 4.28%,
p = 0.0031) in tumor tissue were detected at higher percent-
ages than in the adjacent mucosa. Firmicutes, the dominant
phylum, were less frequent in tumors than in the correspond-
ing normal mucosa (37.12% vs. 44.72%, p = 0.0076), as was
the case for Actinobacteria (1.01% vs. 1.37%, p = 0.027)
(Fig. 1b). At the genus level, we identified that
Fusobacterium (8.50% vs. 4.13%, p = 0.004) were more
abundant in the tumor tissue than in the adjacent normal mu-
cosal tissue. In accordance with this, we also discovered that
Prevotella (1.51% vs. 1.09%, p = 0.0091), Alloprevotella
(1.74% vs. 0.66%, p = 0.0113), Porphyromonas (0.94% vs.
0.33%, p = 0.0197), Peptostreptococcus (0.82% vs. 0.33%,
p = 0.0008), and Parvimonas (0.69% vs. 0.27%, p = 0.0071)
were dominant bacteria genera in the tumor tissue. In addition,
Granulicatella, Lachnospiraceae_UCG-010, Gemella,
Dialister, Selenomonas, Hungatella, Ruminiclostridium,
Lachnoanaerobaculum, Campylobacter, Leptothrix, and
Morganella were also relatively abundant genera in tumor
tissue. In contrast, the relative abundances of the Bacillus
(8.14% vs. 14.32%, p < 0.0001), Lactococcus (6.45% vs.
10.12%, p = 0.0007), Acinetobacter (1.38% vs. 2.68%,
p = 0.0005), Pseudomonas (1.24% vs. 2.25%, p = 0.0001),
and Parabacteroides (1.15% vs. 1.78%, p = 0.0339) genera
were significantly lower in tumors than in the adjacent non-
tumor mucous tissue. The same tendency was observed for the
Leuconostoc, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Pantoea,
Geobacillus, and Propionibacterium genera (Fig. 1c).
To further explore the distinct pattern of the gut microbiota,
we applied the enterotype analysis and identified key
enterotypes in this study as Bacteroides and Bacillus
(Fig. 1d, f). The percentages of Bacteroides and Bacillus
among the total gut microbiota were 83.08% and 16.92%,
respectively, in tumor tissue compared to 55.38% and
44.62%, respectively, in the adjacent non-tumor tissue.
Sample plots in the NMDS under a Bray–Curtis distance also
indicated a separated trend (Fig. 1e). Linear discriminant anal-
ysis coupled with effect size measurements (LEfSe) indicated
that bacilli/Bacillus, Lactococcus, Acinetobacter, and
Pseudomonas were the predominant phenotypes that contrib-
ute to the distribution of adjacent normal mucosal tissue-
associated microbiota, while Fusobacterium was the key fac-
tor contributing to the dysbiosis of microbiota in tumor tissues
(Fig. 1g, h).
Microbiota composition changes, along with different
anatomical locations in the gut
To explore the impact of different anatomical localization on
the composition of gut microbiota, we studied the differences
in the prevalence of key gut microorganisms as identified by
LEfSe between CRC and para-cancer samples at various gut
anatomical locations. The principal coordinates analysis
(PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity showed that
PC1, PC2, and PC3 explained the variance by 57.16%,
13.82%, and 5.13%, respectively. The relative abundance
curves of these microbiota were depicted according to the
continuous anatomical sites (Fig. 2a). From the ascending
colon to the rectum colon, the relative abundance of Bacillus
was relatively stable at approximately 10%, but decreased in
CRC patients with sigmoid and rectal tumors. However, the
Lactococcus abundance increased from approximately 7% to
25% in patients with tumors located in the rectum.
Acinetobacter and Pseudomonas displayed a decreasing trend
along the anatomical sites of the gut. Nevertheless, the relative

Microbiota variation associated with gene mutation
To observe the relationship between the relative abundance of
Fusobacterium and gene mutations, we first divided the sam-
ples into high and low abundance groups with respect to
Fusobacterium abundance. However, we did not determine
any difference in K-ras mutation rates between samples with
high and low abundance of Fusobacterium (p > 0.05).
Next, to test whether the K-ras mutation status was
associated with the level of Fusobacterium, we com-
pared the abundance of Fusobacterium in K-ras
mutation-positive tumors (n = 47) and K-ras mutation-
negative tumors (n = 18); however, no statistical differ-
ence was observed for either. We then separated the
tumor tissues into three groups according to the detected
relative abundance of Fusobacterium. The K-ras muta-
tion rates in each group were 61.90% (13/21), 81.82%
(18/22), and 72.73% (16/22) for the groups with high,
intermediate, and low Fusobacterium abundance, respec-
tively. Nevertheless, no significant differences were not-
ed between the three groups.

Discussion
CRC is a major worldwide public health problem, and much
of its etiology is due to an interplay between genetic predis-
position, lifestyle, and environmental factors [53]. In recent
years, the interaction between food, gut microbiota, and
chronic inflammation has attracted substantial attention [54].
Notably, a hypothesis that a different gut microbiome compo-
sition may affect the initiation and progression of CRC has
been increasingly studied [54–56]. In this study, we examined
the microbiota signatures in tumor and adjacent normal mu-
cosal tissue samples of CRC patients using the 16S rRNA
sequencing approach. In a previous single-center study, we
reported that the gut microbiota composition differed signifi-
cantly in CRC tumor tissues and adjacent normal mucosal
tissue, as well as in healthy controls [30]. In this study, we
collected CRC patient tissue samples from two independent
medical centers to perform an integrated analysis of the mi-
crobiota signature in CRC.
In this study, the diversity and richness of gut microbiota
were compared in cancer and adjacent normal tissues. The
diversity was examined by Shannon and Simpson indexes,
and richness was represented by the ACE and Chao indexes;
however, no differences were detected. The possible reason
for these findings may be because microbiota in tumor and
adjacent normal mucosa tissues were examined in relatively
close locations. Furthermore, in this study, we demonstrated
that CRC was associated with significant changes in gut mi-
crobiota composition. In comparison with the adjacent normal
tissue, the relative abundance of Firmicutes and
Actinobacteria were significantly reduced in tumors, while
Fusobacteria were substantially more enriched at the phylum
level. Our previous study showed that the relative abundance
of Fusobacteria in tumor tissue was higher than in correspond-
ing normal tissue [30]. At the genus level, we identified that
Prevotella, Peptostreptococcus, and other opportunistic bac-
teria were enriched at the cancer sites, which was consistent
with other studies [57]. We also identified several microbes
that were enriched in normal tissues, such as Bacillus,
Lactococcus, Acinetobacter, and Pseudomonas. Moreover,
we also confirmed an increased presence of Fusobacterium
in tumor tissue, which was an important and dominant candi-
date pathogen in the progression of CRC in this study.
Interestingly, the microbiota alterations in tumor tissue were
finally identified as the key OTUs by LEfSe analysis.
Kostic et al. reported a close connection between Kostic
and CRC using 16S rRNA and qPCR [58]. Subsequently,
many studies confirmed this association, and Fusobacterium
had been recognized as a risk factor in connection with IBD,
including both ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease [59].
Because patients with IBD have an increased risk of CRC,
this bacterium could act as a potential carcinogen. Notably,
Fusobacterium has been shown to elicit proinflammatory re-
actions and accelerate the adhesion and invasion to the host
colon epithelial cells [60–62]. The structural framework for
binding to tumor cells depended upon the expression of the
fusobacterial lectin (Fap2) as well as the polysaccharide from
the host [63]. Fusobacterium nucleatum has also been report-
ed to harbor a conserved fragment named FadA that mediates
its adhesion to E-cadherin on tumor and normal colon cells
[64]. Once attached to tumor cells, F. nucleatum activates the
β-catenin signaling transduction cascade and induces the con-
stitutive expression of oncogenes and other growth signals
promoting CRC progression. Additionally, we recently report-
ed that F. nucleatum promoted the proliferation of CRC cells
and tumor development by activating TLR4 signaling to
NFκB in an animal model [65].
Another possible use of F. nucleatum identification in CRC
is in the diagnosis and prognosis of CRC patients. Notably, Yu
et al. established a set of F. nucleatum genetic markers, includ-
ing butyryl-CoA dehydrogenase, and have validated their
high ability to distinguish CRC patients from healthy controls
in an independent cohort [66, 67]. Nevertheless, in our study,
the distinction of colorectal tumors from adjacent normal mu-
cous tissue using Fusobacterium was moderate, with a cut-off
of 2.47%. Possible reasons may include the relative quantita-
tion of Fusobacterium and the comparison between tumor and
adjacent normal tissue and not healthy controls. Compared
with F. nucleatum-negative cancer patients, low and high
Fusobacterium-positive patients showed an increased risk
for CRC-associated mortality [68]. This may be due, in part,
to the inverse association between F. nucleatum and CD3+ T-
cell density in CRC [69].

nificantly dominant at the cancer site. The genus of
Peptostreptococcus, formerly known as Peptococcus, was

genus contains several other candidate pathogens, such as
Peptococcus magnus, Peptostreptococcus asaccharolyticus,
and Peptostreptococcus anaerobius, which were underestimated

recently identified to be associated with CRC and was also found
to increase CRC cell proliferation by inducing intracellular cho-
lesterol biosynthesis [66, 72]. Nevertheless, more studies are
warranted to identify candidate pathogens in CRC.
This study also examined the impact of anatomical locali-
zation of the tumor on the distribution of mucosa-associated
microbiota. We observed the relative abundance of antecedent
identified key OTUs. Interestingly, the genera Acinetobacter
and Pseudomonas showed an increasing trend, while Bacillus
showed a decreasing trend in the distal colon. However, the
distribution of Fusobacterium was more unstable. As the sam-
ple size was very small, a study including more samples
should be performed to determine the possible differential
signature pattern of microbiota distribution in association with
the tumor location. Nevertheless, this study observed the mi-
crobiota discrepancies between the ascending and descending
colon, which were previously shown to activate different sig-
naling pathways and various prognosis [73, 74]. Notably,
there was a significant difference in the microbiota composi-
tion between these two groups in this study. We also deter-
mined the association between K-ras mutation status and
Fusobacterium. Our study found a negative correlation, which
was similar to the findings reported in the study by Tahara
et al. [37]. In addition, we also identified the microbial func-
tion alteration in tumor and adjacent normal mucosal tissue.
An amino acid metabolism change was confirmed by COG
and KEGG analysis. We also hypothesized that lipopolysac-
charide biosynthesis may be an important factor involved in
an altered microbiota structure. There are several limitations to
this study. Firstly, the distribution of various anatomical can-
cer sites was not equal. Secondly, we did not explore the
relationship between other common CRC gene mutations with
gut microbiota composition. Finally, we used semi-
quantitative data to analyze the association between different
correlations.
Nevertheless, our study provides insight into the mucosal
microbiota landscape in CRC and identified Fusobacterium as
the key contributor to this type of cancer. We also evaluated
the distribution of microbiota at different anatomical sites of
the tumor and found significant differences, which may be
associated with the biological traits of CRC; however, these
findings remain to be confirmed by larger prospective studies.
More candidate pathogens will likely be identified and studied
in larger cohorts of CRC patients as well as in animal models
to better understand tumorigenesis in CRC. These insights
may prove helpful in formulating public strategies to prevent
and treat CRC.
