Abstract 
The central nervous system (CNS) and gastrointestinal tract (GIT) are linked through neuro-
endocrine and humoral pathways. Critically ill patients suffer severe physical and emotional 
stress and frequently receive acid suppressants; however, stress and acid suppression may 
alter GIT microbiota. This study evaluated the effects of acid suppression on the GIT microbiota 
and genome-wide expression of brain-specific genes in a murine model of restraint stress. 
Twenty-four male C57BL/6J mice were randomly assigned to three days of restraint stress by 
hypothermic immobilization or control environment for three hours daily and either 
esomeprazole 2 mg/kg or saline by intraperitoneal injection daily. Bacterial communities 
associated with the stomach, ileum, cecum, and mid-colon were determined by broad-range 
16S rRNA gene sequencing, while RNA-sequencing assessed mRNA expression in the 
hippocampus. Both stress (p<0.001) and esomeprazole (p=0.006) had significant, independent 
effects on the composition of stomach microbiota. Stress had no impact on the hippocampus 
but the addition of esomeprazole induced differential expression of 124 genes, many of which 
are involved in cognitive and behavior pathways. Gene expression was correlated with the 
abundances of multiple microbial families. Acute stress has region-specific effects on the 
distribution of GIT commensal bacteria which is heightened with acid suppression. Several key 
biological processes in the hippocampus that are needed for neurocognition are affected by 
dysbiosis caused by acid suppression during stress. Further studies should evaluate 
associations between microbiota, host gene expression, the abundance of CNS neurocognitive 
modulators, and their impact on cognition and behavior.

Introduction 
 
The central nervous system (CNS) and gastrointestinal tract (GIT) are connected 
through neuro-endocrine pathways and by humoral and cellular mediators (Frank and Pace, 
2008; Cryan and Dinan, 2012; Mayer et al, 2015; Vuong et al, 2017; Dinan and Cryan, 2017). 
The homeostatic communication between the emotional and cognitive centers of the CNS and 
the GIT is bidirectional. The GIT is inhabited by an ecosystem of microbial communities that 
exist in symbiosis with their host under normal physiologic conditions (Rea et al, 2016). Both 
emotional and physical stress conditions alter GIT microbiota and dysbiosis has been 
associated with numerous CNS disorders (Cryan and Dinan, 2012; Mayer et al, 2015; Vuong et 
al, 2017; Dinan and Cryan, 2017). Animal models demonstrate disturbances to the GIT mucosa 
(e.g. inflammation, ischemia) or microbiota (e.g. probiotic supplementation) acutely influence the 
expression of key CNS mediators that modulate behavior and cognition (Mayer et al, 2015). 
Therefore, a homeostatic relationship exists between the GIT microbiome and the CNS and 
disturbances to one may rapidly influence the other.  
Dysbiosis occurs quickly in critically ill patients and many patients show symptoms of 
acute brain dysfunction, manifested by disturbances in consciousness and cognition 
(Pandharipande et al, 2013; Wischmeyer et al, 2016; Kitsios et al, 2017). The mechanism of 
acute brain dysfunction is not fully elucidated but involves many of the same neuro-endocrine 
and humoral / cellular mediators that link the CNS and GIT (Pandharipande et al, 2013). Proton 
pump inhibitors (PPIs) are frequently used to prevent GIT hemorrhage from stress-related 
mucosal disease (SRMD); however, acid suppression may contribute to dysbiosis and infectious 
complications (Barletta et al, 2014; Preslaski et al, 2014; MacLaren et al, 2015). An increase in 
gastric pH affects the distribution of commensal organisms and the overgrowth of non-
  
6 
 
commensal microbes (Vesper et al, 2009; Wu et al, 2014). The concomitant influences of 
dysbiosis related to physiologic stress and acid suppression on CNS activity has not been 
evaluated. Using a murine model of stress, we examined the influence of PPI on GIT microbiota 
and mRNA expression profiles in the frontal lobe and the hippocampus and assessed the 
relationships between alterations in GIT microbiota and gene expression profiles. 
 
Materials and Methods 
Animals 
The protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. 
Twenty-four male mice (C57BL/6J, Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME) 8-12 weeks old were 
acclimated for at least seven days. This strain of mice was chosen because they demonstrate 
rapid gastric mucosal damage with physiologic stress and the expression of key neurocognitive 
mediators are influenced by alterations to the microbiota (Mileva et al, 2003; Toma et al, 2005; 
Horiuchi et al, 2010; Martins et al, 2014).  
 
Experimental Procedures 
All 24 mice were individually housed during the experiment. Animals were randomized to 
physiologic restraint stress or control environment. Restraint stress was induced daily for three 
days by placing a single mouse in a clear plastic tube (~2 inches by 1.5 inches closed on both 
ends except ventilation holes) for one hour at room temperature followed by another two hours 
at 4°C in a hibernaculum. Mice in the control group were isolated in a standard cage at room 
temperature without food or water for the same three hour period. Sixty minutes after each 
session, mice received 2 mg/kg esomeprazole sodium (AstraZeneca, Wilmington, DE) or saline 
(placebo vehicle) by intraperitoneal injection for a total of three doses. The dose of 2 mg/kg 
corresponds to the molar ratio of esomeprazole that inhibits 67-75% of gastric acid production in 
other rodent models (Colucci et al, 2009; Koch et al, 2004). Therefore, six mice received 
  
7 
 
restraint stress environment plus PPI, six received restraint stress environment plus placebo, six 
received control environment plus PPI, and six received control environment plus placebo. 
Three days of intervention was chosen to ensure microbiota disturbances and to represent the 
clinical scenario of critical illness. 
Twelve hours after the last dose of PPI or placebo vehicle was administered, animals 
were sacrificed. The gastric antrum, ileum, cecum, mid-colon, frontal lobe, and hippocampus 
were harvested and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80ºC. A portion of the gastric 
antrum was fixed in buffered formalin, mounted on glass slides, and stained with 
hematoxylin/eosin for histological examination using microscopy to assess SRMD (Peura and 
Johnson, 1985; Ruiz-Santana et al, 1991).  
 
Microbiome Analyses 
 
The mucosae of the stomach, ileum, cecum, and mid-colon were scraped off with a 
spatula. Microbiome profiling of these scrapings used broad-range PCR amplification, 
sequencing, and analysis of 16S rRNA genes (Frank et al, 2011; Dillon et al, 2014; Markle et al, 
2013; Brumbaugh et al, 2016; Frank et al, 2016; Lemas et al, 2016; Pandrea et al, 2016; Rick et 
al, 2017). In brief, PCR amplicons were generated using primers that target base pairs of the 
V3V4 variable region of the 16S rRNA gene (primers 338F and 806R). Amplicons were paired-
end sequenced and paired-end reads were aligned to mouse reference genome mm10 with 
bowtie2 and matching sequences discarded (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012). The sorted paired 
reads were assembled using phrap (Ewing and Green, 1998; Ewing et al, 1998). Potential 
chimeras identified with Uchime (usearch6.0.203_i86linux32) (Pruesse et al, 2012)  using the 
Schloss (Schloss and Westcott, 2011) Assembled sequences were aligned and classified with 
SINA (1.3.0-r23838) (Pruesse et al, 2012) using the 418,497 bacterial sequences in Silva 
115NR99 (Quast et al, 2013) as reference configured to yield the Silva taxonomy. Operational 
taxonomic units (OTUs) were produced by clustering sequences with identical taxonomic 
  
8 
 
assignments. This process generated 9,675,094 sequences for 96 samples with a median of 
105,740 sequences/sample (interquartile range of 52866-135683). The minimum Goods 
coverage score was 99.8% at the rarefaction point of 11,321. All de-multiplexed, paired-end 16S 
rRNA gene sequence files along with associated metadata were deposited into the NCBI 
Sequence Read Archive under project number PRJNA433163. 
 
 
CNS Gene Expression 
Brain tissues were immediately placed into RNAlater (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) 
(Dowell et al, 2016). Following polytron homogenization of the tissue, total RNA was isolated 
using the RNeasy Total RNA Isolation Kit (Qiagen Inc., Valencia, CA) and quantified using the 
Qubit Fluorometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA). RNA was enriched for the poly-
adenylated (polyA) fraction with the use of oligo(dT) magnetic beads. Bar-coded libraries were 
constructed using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina) and then 
sequenced for a length of 151 bp on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 Sequencing System. Twelve bar-
coded samples were run per lane which yielded between 33 and 56 million reads per sample.  
Reads were mapped back to the reference genome (mm10) using the STAR algorithm 
(Dobin et al, 2013). Whole gene quantification was determined using HTSeq (La Rosa et al, 
2012). HTSeq output was filtered using the RPLG method (Reads Per Length of Gene) as 
follows: RPLG = (reads * 151) / gene length. Genes with a median RPLG greater than 1 in any 
of the four treatment groups were retained for further analysis. This filtering procedure yielded 
15,623 and 15,304 genes for the cortex and hippocampus, respectively. Raw read counts were 
then used as DESeq2 input for differential gene expression analysis (Love et al, 2014). All RNA-
seq data along with associated metadata were deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus with 
accession numbers GSM3423544 through GSM3423591.

Statistical Analyses 
The primary outcome of this study was the relative abundances of OTUs between 
treatment groups. Six animals are required in each group to show a shift of 22% in the 
abundance of any bacterial taxon between any two groups, assuming Dirichlet-multinomial 
distribution, 25,000 reads, an alpha level of 0.01 (to account for multiple comparisons), and 
power of 0.8. The Dirichlet-multinomial model accounts for over dispersion and is interpreted in 
a manner analogous to the mean / median and variance of the microbial diversity. Differences in 
overall community composition between treatment groups was assessed using one-way and 
two-way permutation-based multiple analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) test of Bray-Curtis 
dissimilarities (the R statistical package v.2.14.0, Institute for Statistics and Mathematics, Wien, 
Austria) (Oksanen, 2008; Anders et al, 2015). P-values were obtained through 1,000,000 
permutations. Non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis tests identified individual OTUs that differed in 
relative abundance or prevalence between groups. Results are reported as median relative 
abundances across treatment groups along with uncorrected P-values.  Measures of alpha-
diversity (Chao1, evenness, Shannon complexity) were estimated (Explicet v2.10.5,) (Robertson 
et al, 2013) through 1,000 replicate re-samplings at the rarefaction point of 11,432 sequences; 
between-group differences were assessed by one-way and two-way analysis of variance. 
For the gene expression analysis, relevant pairwise comparisons were made for each 
brain structure using the standard DESeq2 workflow with default parameters (Love et al, 2014). 
DESeq2 uses generalized linear model (GLM) fits to obtain maximum-likelihood estimates of the 
gene-wise logarithmic fold change (LFC) between experimental conditions. The observed 
distribution of maximum-likelihood estimates of all genes is then fit to a zero-centered normal 
distribution which is used as a prior in second step of GLM fits. The maximum a posteriori 
estimates are kept as final estimates of the LFC. These estimates along with their derived 
standard errors are used in Wald tests for differential expression. P values from the Wald tests 
are adjusted for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure of Benjamini 
  
10 
 
and Hochberg (Benjamini et al, 1995). Genes having a FDR of ≤0.15 were considered 
significantly differentially expressed (Wagner et al, 2011). Enrichment analysis was conducted 
on significant DE gene sets using the online tool DAVID (Huang et al, 2009).Spearman rank 
correlation tests were used to assess associations between microbiome abundances and 
neurocognitive gene expression.  
 
Results  
 
Animal weights did not differ between groups.  
 
Effects of Stress and Esomeprazole Treatment on Gastrointestinal Microbiome  
 
Both restraint stress and PPI treatment were independently associated with the 
distribution of commensal bacteria within the stomach at the genus level (two-way permutation-
based multiple analysis of variance [PERMANOVA] tests: p≤0.01for stress, p≤0.0001 for PPI, 
p=0.74 for stress x PPI interaction term). At the phylum level, restraint stress but not PPI was 
significantly associated with stomach bacterial community structure (Figure 1). The effects of 
restraint stress, PPI, or both on the microbiome of other GIT regions were less apparent (Figure 
1).. No significant differences were noted in alpha-diversity measures of richness, community 
evenness, or complexity (data not shown) (Schloss and Handelsman, 2007; Magurran, 2013). 
A variety of bacterial taxa within the stomach microbiome varied between treatment 
groups (Figure 2).  Restraint stress in the absence of PPI treatment insignificantly increased the 
relative abundances of Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes. While the overall increased levels of 
Firmicutes were insignificant in restraint stressed animals not exposed to PPI, the relative 
abundance of Ruminococcaceae increased (median relative abundance of 11.0% vs. 6.5%; 
W(6,6) = 0, p≤0.01) while Lachnospiraceae (44.2% vs. 28.3%; W(6,6) = 6, p≤0.10) and 
Lactobacillaceae (3.9% vs 10.1%; W(6,6) = 31, p≤0.10) showed large opposing changes in 
composition that were insignificant . Restraint stress in the presence of esomeprazole increased 
  
11 
 
Bacteroidetes (20.8% vs. 11.3%; W(6,6) = 2, p≤0.01), which was mainly attributable to 
insignificant increases in S24-7 composition (17.9% vs. 11.2%; p≤0.10). Although Firmicutes 
were unchanged (75.9% vs. 81.7%; W(6,6) = 26, p=0.24) in restraint stressed vs. unstressed 
animals treated with esomeprazole, stress with PPI reduced Lactobacillaceae (13.1 vs. 41.7%; 
W(6,6) = 34, p≤0.01) while  Lachnospiraceae (38.8% vs. 27.7%; W(6,6) = 7 ,p≤0.10) showed 
insignificant increases in composition compared with unstressed treatment with PPI.  
Esomeprazole treatment in the absence of restraint stress insignficantly decreased 
Bacteroidetes (16.3% vs. 11.3% for placebo vs PPI; W(6,6) = 29, p≤0.10). These results were 
concomitant with increased Firmicutes (62.2% vs. 81.7%; W(6,6) = 4, p≤0.05). In contrast to the 
effects of the restraint stressed vs. unstressed comparison, PPI treatment increased the 
abundance of Lactobacillaceae (10.1% vs. 41.7%; W(6,6) = 4, p≤0.05) in the stomach, whereas 
Lachnospiraceae (28.3% vs. 27.7%; W(6,6) = 22, p=0.59) and Ruminococcaceae (6.5% vs. 
8.2%; W(6,6) = 14, p=0.59) were not notably affected. When PPI and placebo were compared 
among restraint stressed animals, only the Tenericutes (e.g., mycoplasma, ureaplasma) differed 
(0.83% vs. 0.15%; W(6,6) = 33, p≤0.05). Under these conditions, S24-7 (W(6,6) = 20, p=0.82), 
Lachnospiraceae (W(6,6) = 25, p=0.31) and Ruminococcaceae (W(6,6) = 15, p=0.70) were 
unchanged, whereas Lactobacillaceae were increased in restraint stress with PPI group 
compared with stress with placebo group (3.9% vs. 13.1%; W(6,6) = 4, p≤0.05). 
 
CNS Gene Expression 
 
 
In the absence of restraint stress, esomeprazole had no impact on the expression 
profiles of the frontal cortex or hippocampus. In the frontal cortex, restraint stress altered the 
expression of three and five genes in the presence (polypeptide N-acetylgalactosaminyl-
transferase 15; hypoxia inducible factor 3, alpha subunit; perilipin 4) or absence of 
esomeprazole (polypeptide N-acetylgalactosaminyl-transferase 15; hypoxia inducible factor 3, 
  
12 
 
alpha subunit; perilipin 4; circadian associated repressor of transcription; neurotransmitter 
transporter, betaine/GABA, member 12), respectively. In the presence of PPI, restraint stress 
had a pronounced effect on gene expression in the hippocampus: 124 genes were differentially 
expressed with the majority being down-regulated (Figure 3; Appendix A). Almost all of these 
genes responded similarly to restraint stress in the absence of esomeprazole, although 
insignificantly (Figure 3). Several biological pathways associated with neurocognition were 
influenced by these 124 genes (Appendix B). None of the genes were significantly altered in the 
control environment condition. 
 
CNS Gene Expression and Microbiome Interactions 
 
 
To identify potential links between altered gene expression and gut microbiota, 
Spearman rank correlation tests were performed on all pairs of host genes that were 
differentially expressed in either the frontal cortex or hippocampus vs. the most abundant 
bacterial families identified in the stomach samples. Hierarchical clustering of host genes and 
bacterial groups by similar patterns of Spearman correlation coefficients (rho) revealed 
associations between two main clusters of bacteria and roughly four clusters of host genes 
(Figure 4). Bacterial phyla did not segregate neatly (Figure 4).  
 
Discussion 
 
This study showed that both restraint stress and acid suppression alter the composition 
of the GIT microbiota and these effects are heightened when both conditions exist. The most 
drastic changes were observed in the stomach, where both PPI treatment and acute stress 
exhibited highly significant and independent associations with dysbiosis. Restraint stress or PPI 
alone had little effects on the expression of genes in the frontal lobe or hippocampus, but acid 
suppression in the setting of stress induced differential expression of 124 genes in the 
hippocampus, the majority of which were down-regulated. Restraint stress, in the absence of 
  
13 
 
PPI, caused these 124 genes to show a near identical insignificant pattern of up- or down-
regulation. This may indicate that restraint stress modestly affects the regulation of these genes 
and PPI exacerbates the effect. The biological processes affected by these genes are key to 
cognition and behavior. Moreover, gene expression was correlated both positively and 
negatively with the abundances of multiple microbial families. Genera and families within the 
same phylum frequently displayed differing patterns of correlation with host gene expression, 
suggesting very complex interrelationships between particular members of the GIT microbiota 
and the host CNS system. 
 
Many clinical and animal model studies have demonstrated altered GIT microbiota 
following exposure to stress or PPI. Acutely stressful conditions like pain, restraint, or cecal 
ligation in rodent models decrease the relative abundances of Bacteroides and Lactobacillus 
spp., while increasing Clostridium spp. in the cecum or colon (Frank and Pace, 2008; Mayer et 
al, 2015; Vuong et al, 2017; Dinan and Cryan, 2017). Studies of PPIs consistently show 
decreased microbiome diversity, increased relative abundance of Streptococcaceae within the 
phylum Firmicutes along the entire GIT, and suppressed abundance of Bacteroidetes (Vesper et 
al, 2009; Freedberg et al, 2014). Some studies also indicate that PPIs are associated with 
increased growth of Enterococcaceae, Staphylococcus, and Escherichia coli and decreased 
growth of Bacteroides and Bifidobacterium in the colon. Although our results suggest little 
impact of stress or PPI on intestinal or colonic microbiomes, stress substantially altered the 
microbiota of the stomach by increasing the abundance of Lachnospiraceae relative to 
Lactobacillus and also enhancing the abundance of Bacteroidetes. In contrast to what we found 
with stress and many other studies with PPIs (Vesper et al, 2009; Freedberg et al, 2014; Wu et 
al, 2014), our results showed PPI alone increased Lactobacillus relative to Lachnospiraceae 
while also decreasing the abundances of Actinobacteria and Bacteroidetes. No other study has 
investigated the combined impact of stress and PPI on GIT microbiota. This is important 
clinically because critically ill patients undergo physiologic stress and often receive PPIs to 
  
14 
 
prevent SRMD-related hemorrhage. We demonstrated that the combination of stress and PPI 
resulted in phylum-level increases in the abundance of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes relative to 
Actinobacteria and Cyanobacteria. Within the Firmicutes, the relative abundance of the 
Lactobacillus was reduced, thus suggesting that the effect of stress on this phylum had greater 
impact than PPI.  
 
Dysbiosis is common in the critically ill, often attributed to interventions like 
antimicrobials, nutrition, and acid suppression (McDonald et al, 2016; Yeh et al, 2016). 
However, both emotional and physical stress of critical illness alter GIT function via the enteric 
nervous system, the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis, and numerous host-derived signaling 
molecules (e.g. catecholamines, serotonin, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), ghrelin / motilin, 
cytokines) (Mayer et al, 2015; Vuong et al, 2017; Rea et al, 2016; Dinan and Cryan, 2017). 
Physiologic manifestations include delayed gastro-intestinal motility, altered secretion of gastric 
acid and intestinal bicarbonate / mucus, enhanced GIT inflammation and permeability, and 
reduced gut immunity (Kunturek et al, 2011; Holtmann and Talley, 2014; Rea et al, 2016; 
Sundman et al, 2017). All of these alterations may influence GIT microbiomes.  
 
Disturbances in the GIT microbiome can influence the CNS. Numerous animal models 
demonstrate alterations to the GIT microbiome acutely influence the expression of key 
mediators of behavior and cognition (e.g. c-Fos, acetylcholine, serotonin, dopamine, brain-
derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), GABA, and N -methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors) 
(Khailova et al, 2013; Reichmann et al, 2013; Laursen et al, 2014; Smith et al, 2014; Wu et al, 
2014). Many microbes generate neuro-modulators. For example, species of Lactobacillus and 
Bifidobacterium produce GABA and acetylcholine, streptococci generates dopamine, and 
Escherichia and Saccharomyces synthesize norepinephrine. These neuro-modulators can enter 
the blood stream to possibly influence neurocognition (Cryan and Dinan, 2012; Mayer et al, 
2015; Vuong et al, 2017). GIT microbes also secrete bioactive chemicals (choline, short chain 
fatty acids, and bile acids) that affect the growth of other microbes and influence the secretion of 
  
15 
 
gastrointestinal hormones that may affect CNS activity (Cryan and Dinan, 2012; Mayer et al, 
2015; Vuong et al, 2017). The etiology of acute brain dysfunction in the critically ill involves 
synaptic and neurochemical imbalances, many concentrated in the hippocampus, including 
decreased acetylcholine relative to increases in dopamine, norepinephrine, glutamate, and 
serotonin (Pandharipande et al, 2013; Wischmeyer et al, 2016). Changes to the microbiome 
directly and indirectly affect hippocampal neurogenesis and mRNA expression (Chen et al, 
2017; Ogbonnaya et al, 2015). Our results indicate the administration of PPI during stress 
affects the regulation of genes in the hippocampus including dopamine receptors (Drd1, Drd2), 
glutamate receptors (Grik3, Grm4), an adrenergic receptor (Adra2b), a GABAA receptor subunit 
(Gabrr2), an opioid receptor (Oprk1) and opioid peptide (Penk), a tachykinin receptor (Tacr1), a 
serotonin receptor (Htr1d), and a variety of neurotransmission and second messenger signaling 
genes (e.g., Cacna2d2, Kcnj5, Kcna5, Kcnn2, Kcnh4, Map3k6, Prkch, Ptpn7, and others). 
Moreover, these genes were enriched in several key biological processes including behavior, 
associative learning, and dopamine receptor signaling and synapse. While we showed a direct 
correlation between the abundance of microbiota in the stomach and the expression of these 
genes in the CNS, further study is needed to assess the nature of these interactions. Many 
animal models have demonstrated acute microbiota disturbances alter behavioral responses to 
stress, fear, and cognition and these responses are minimized when the dysbiosis is reversed 
(Frank and Pace, 2008; Cryan and Dinan, 2012; Mayer et al, 2015; Rea et al; 2016; Vuong et al, 
2017; Dinan and Cryan, 2017). Therefore, dysbiosis of the GIT as the result of PPI 
administration during stress may contribute to altered gene expression in the hippocampus to 
produce changes in neurocognition that manifest as acute brain dysfunction. 
 
This study has limitations. The small sample size of 24 mice precludes definitive 
conclusions and mandates that the results be validated in other models of stress. Because of 
the exploratory nature of this study with multiple analyses of correlation across a small sample 
size, some false positive results may have arisen. While the hypothermic restraint model of 
  
16 
 
stress used in this study produced gastritis, the extent of injury was minor suggesting the 
physiologic stress induced was minimal and the extent of emotional stress is immeasurable. 
This, the small sample size, and the lack of treatment duration may explain why stress or PPI 
alone had minimal influence on the microbiota of the ileum, cecum, and colon and little impact 
on CNS gene expression. Moreover, delineating the impact of physiologic or emotional stress is 
not possible. Only gene expression was quantified in the CNS, so influences on neurocognitive 
mediators represent indirect comparisons.

Figure 2: Individual Taxa Differing Across Treatment Groups and Gastrointestinal Sites. 
Bacterial families are listed along the x-axes, while y-axes show the –log(p-values) for each 
statistical test, which results in longer lines for smaller p-values. The plots with only blue 
lines involve multiple group comparisons for each anatomical site. The plots with blue and 
red lines involve only two group comparisons assessing whether an OTU differed in relative 
abundance in one or the other group (blue means that the OTU was more abundant in the 
first comparison group listed at the top of a plot and red means the opposite).  CEC, cecum. 
COL, colon. ILM, ileum. PPI, proton pump inhibitor. STO, stomach.  Taxa with p-values less 
than 0.1 in any test are listed to the right of the plots.  Genus names are preceded by

Elusimicrobium, Firm = Firmicutes, Prot = Proteobacteria, Tene = Tenericutes.

Figure 4. Heat Map of Spearman Correlation Coefficients of Hippocampal Genes that were 
Differentially Expressed Against the Most Abundant Bacterial Families Found in Stomach 
Samples. Blue represents positive correlations. Red represents negative correlations. The 
matrix is sorted along the axes to cluster like genes and bacterial families. Spearman 
correlation coefficients (rho) revealed two main clusters of bacteria (labeled 1-2) and roughly

Firmicutes, members of the clostridia (e.g., Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococaceae, 
Clostridiaceae) were associated with cluster 1, while Erysipeoltrichacea, Lactobacillaceae, 
and Peptostreptococcaceae) belonged to cluster 2.
