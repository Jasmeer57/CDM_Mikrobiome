Abstract 
17 
Intestinal microbiota is now widely known to be key roles in the nutrition uptake, 
18 
metabolism, and the regulation of human immune responses. However, we do not 
19 
know how changes the intestinal microbiota in response to the chemotherapy. In this 
20 
study, we used network-based analytical approaches to explore the effects of five 
21 
stages of chemotherapy on the intestinal microbiota of colorectal cancer patients. The 
22 
results showed that chemotherapy greatly reduced the alpha diversity and changed the 
23 
specie-specie interaction networks of intestinal microbiota, proved by the network 
24 
size, network connectivity and modularity. The OTU167 and OTU8 from the genus 
25 
Fusobacterium and Bacteroides were identified as keystone taxa by molecular 
26 
ecological networks in the first two stages of chemotherapy, and were significantly 
27 
correlated with tumor makers (P < 0.05). Five stages of chemotherapy did not make 
28 
the intestinal micro-ecosystem regain a steady state, because of the lower alpha 
29 
diversity and more complicated ecological networks compared to the healthy 
30 
individuals. Furthermore, combing the changes of ecological networks with the tumor 
31 
markers, the intestinal microbiota was closely linked with clinical chemotherapeutic 
32 
effects. 
33 
Importance 
34 
A deeply understanding of the role of intestinal microbiota contributes to help us find 
35 
path forward for improving the prognosis of colorectal cancer patients. In addition, 
36 
diet or probiotics interventions will be a possible attempt to improve the clinical 
37 
chemotherapeutic effects for colorectal cancer patients.

Introduction 
42 
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a major killer of people in the world all the time, though 
43 
continuing medical advances on several fronts. The CRC etiology is very complicated 
44 
and still not fully understood, but CRC carcinogenesis is a heterogeneous progression 
45 
with a train of genetic and epigenetic variations, and is profoundly influenced by 
46 
dietary, environment, host and intestinal microbiota (1). Although genetic 
47 
predisposition is closely associated with certain types of CRC, intestinal microbiota 
48 
increasingly plays an indispensable role in human intestinal health. It has been proved 
49 
that intestinal microbiota is concerned with the initiation and progression of colorectal 
50 
cancer (2, 3). Heterogeneity of CRC-associated microbiota might reflect discrepancies 
51 
in the host immunoinflammatory (4). Despite such heterogeneity, considering a 
52 
combination of several bacteria in the faecal microbiota of CRC individuals as a 
53 
marker for detecting the disease is feasible (5, 6). As a consequence, the plasticity of 
54 
intestinal microbiota can be leveraged for therapeutic interventions (7) and to improve 
55 
therapeutic effect (8). 
56 
Cytotoxic drugs continue to be the mainstay of therapy for most CRC patients, 
57 
whereas the related treatment response is unpredictable. The personalized cancer 
58 
therapies are now emerging, and targeted therapies have made revolutionized 
59 
outcomes in CRC (9). However, it still appears novel problems such as idiosyncratic 
60

61

62

63

64

66

71

72

73

74 
Microbes coexist in complex environments in which interactions among 
75 
individuals are indispensable for community assembly and ecosystem function (17, 
76 
18). However, few studies explore the interactions among intestinal microbiota, or 
77 
determine which individuals share niches within the intestinal environment. Therefore, 
78 
identifying and defining the interactions that occur among intestinal microbiota 
79 
contributes to understanding microbial diversity and function to provide a possibility 
80 
in better therapeutic interventions. Molecular ecological network analysis provides a 
81 
promising future for exploring the dynamics of microbial interactions and niches (19).

Network analysis can identify putative keystone taxa which are important in 
83 
maintaining community structure and function (20). In recent years, molecular 
84 
ecological network analysis has been used as a tool to determine complex microbial 
85 
assemblages in various environments such as human (21), groundwater (22) and soil 
86 
(23).  
87 
To identify intestinal microbial assemblages that potentially interact or share 
88 
niches within intestine, we used molecular ecological network analysis (24) to 
89 
construct co-occurrence networks for healthy volunteers and CRC patients throughout 
90 
the treatment. We conducted a study of intestinal microbiota using faecal samples 
91 
from CRC patients and healthy volunteers. High-throughput sequencing of 16S rRNA 
92 
gene amplicons was used to describe the intestinal bacterial assemblages. We mainly 
93 
examined the changes of intestinal microbiota during the treatment based on the 
94 
molecular ecological network. Molecular network analysis was used to address three 
95 
questions: (1) if the microbial diversity and community structure were affected by 
96 
chemotherapy treatment for CRC patients? (2) How does the bacterial interaction 
97 
change during the treatment for CRC patients? (3) Are there taxa that play particularly 
98 
important roles within CRC networks, suggesting that they may serve as keystone 
99 
taxa in CRC communities? Our work identifies a previously undocumented dimension 
100 
of the intestinal microbiota, and offers insight into the specie-specie interaction 
101 
networks of intestinal microbiota associated with CRC patients in response to the 
102 
chemotherapy.

106

109

Discussions 
207 
Chemotherapy remains the mainstay of treatment for patients with CRC, except 
208 
available surgical debulking (28). In recent years, the changes that occur to the 
209 
intestinal microbiota are gradually understood in response to cancer chemotherapy. 
210 
Chemotherapy can damage the mucus layer and disrupt the mucosal barrier in the 
211 
intestine. Then some intestinal microbiota probably penetrates the lamina propria, 
212 
leading to life-threatening systemic infections to activate the innate immune system to 
213 
influence the efficacy of chemotherapy (29, 30). Previous study reported that 
214 
chemotherapy severely influenced the homeostasis of intestinal mcirobiota (31); in 
215 
turn, intestinal microbiota modulated the efficacy and toxicity by key mechanisms as 
216 
translocation, immunomodulation, metabolism, reduced diversity and ecological 
217 
variation (32). Thus, to optimize current therapeutic strategies, which could help 
218 
improve the patients’ survival, we compare the intestinal microbiota from the faeces 
219 
of CRC patients in response to five stages of the chemotherapy based on the 
220 
molecular ecological network analysis. 
221 
Microbes in the intestine are not independent individuals but complex 
222 
inter-connected ecological communities. Microbes compete for resources or exchange 
223 
genetic material influencing the microbial composition and host health. Therefore, 
224 
understanding microbial interactions is important to understand their function. 
225 
Multiple mechanisms may be responsible for changing network size and complexity 
226 
in the intestinal microbiota. Chemotherapy distinctly influenced the ecological 
227 
networks within the intestinal microbiota in CRC patients (T), identified in terms of 
228

Exploring the modular topological roles is a key step in the identification of key 
274 
OTUs roles in their modules. Topologically, different OTUs play individual roles in 
275 
the ecological networks (43). Structurally, the loss of peripherals could not influence 
276

277

278

281

282

283

284 
in response to the chemotherapy.

Materials and methods 
299 
Experiment description 
300 
Total of 22 CRC patients from affiliated hospital of Qingdao University (Qingdao, 
301 
China), aged 34-73 years, were enrolled in our study (Table 1). This study was 
302 
selected from histopathological diagnosis of primary CRC, newly diagnosed and 
303 
untreated, and no history of other tumors. All CRC patients were guided by the 
304 
National Comprehensive Cancer Network Guidelines. The 21 healthy volunteers, 
305 
aged 27-64 years, were selected as controls. During a routine physical examination, 
306 
all these participants who used antibiotics within two months, or were regularly using 
307 
non-steroidal anti-inﬂammatory drugs, probiotics or statins before sampling, were 
308 
excluded from the study. Other exclusions included chronic bowel disease, food 
309 
allergies, dietary restrictions, and other signs of infections. Based on the national 
310 
comprehensive cancer network (NCCN) guideline, these CRC patients were 
311 
performed by standard chemotherapy (45), the following-up samples were obtained 
312 
from CRC patients before the treatment of every stage, named T0, T1, T2, T3, T4 and 
313 
T5. Total of 123 fecal samples from the CRC patients were collected for about six 
314 
times. All these participants were local residents of Qingdao city for more than five 
315 
years. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of 
316 
.

319 
DNA extraction, purification, sequencing and data processing 
320 
Microbial DNA was extracted from fecal samples by QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini 
321 
Kit as previous reported (46). The freshly extracted DNA was purified by 1% melting 
322 
point agarose gel followed by phenolchloroform-butanol extraction. The V3-V4 
323 
region of the 16S rRNA gene from each sample was amplified by the bacterial 
324 
universal primers (forward primer: 5’-ACTCCTACGGGRSGCAGCAG-3’; reverse 
325 
primer: 5’-GGACTACVVGGGTATCTAATC-3’). PCR amplification was performed 
326 
in a 30μl reaction, containing 15μl 2 × KAPA HiFi Hotstart ReadyMix, 10 ng 
327 
template DNA, 1μl of each primer (forward and reverse primer), and the rest of the 
328 
ddH2O. The reaction mixtures were operated to a denaturation at 95℃ for 1 min, 
329 
followed by 12 cycles of 98℃ for 15 s, 72℃ for 10 s, 94℃ for 20 s, 65℃ for 10 s 
330 
and 72℃ for 10s, followed by 11 cycles of 94℃ for 20 s, 58℃ for 30 s, 72℃ for 
331 
30 s, and a final extension at 72℃ for 150 s. The PCR amplification products were 
332 
purified through AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen, USA), eluted in 30μl 
333 
water, and aliquoted into three PCR tubes. DNA quality and quantity were assessed by 
334

Network construction and analysis  
344 
Networks were constructed for intestinal microbiota from CRC patients and healthy 
345 
individuals based on OTU relative abundances, yielding a total of 7 networks. 
346 
Covariations were calculated across more than 19 biological replicates to create each 
347 
network. Only OTUs detected in more than 50% replicate samples were used for 
348 
network construction. Before the network construction, the random matrix theory 
349 
(RMT), which is a reliable, sensitive and robust tool for analyzing high-throughput 
350 
genomics data (47), was used to automatically get the appropriate similarity threshold 
351 
(St) for identifying modular network and elucidating network interactions. St means 
352 
the minimal strength of the connections between each pair of nodes (48). Global 
353 
network properties were characterized according to previous research (24). Modules 
354 
reflect divergent selection regimes, clusters of phylogenetically and closely related 
355 
species, habitat heterogeneity and the key unit of species co-evolution (49). 
356 
Modularity is used to demonstrate a network which is divided into distinct sub-groups 
357 
naturally (47). Average degree (avgK) represents the complexity of the network. 
358

359 
the network. Average clustering coefficient (avgCC) is described to that how well a
