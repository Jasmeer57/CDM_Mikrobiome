Healthcare Micro-Environments Define Multidrug-Resistant Organism Persistence



Background:Multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs) colonizing the healthcare environment have been shown to contribute to risk for healthcare-associated infections (HAIs), with adverse effects on patient morbidity and mortality. We sought to define how bacterial contamination and persistent MDRO colonization of the healthcare environment are related to the position of patients and wastewater sites.Methods:We performed a prospective cohort study, enrolling 51 hospital rooms at the time of admitting a patient with an eligible MDRO in the prior 30 days. We performed systematic sampling and MDRO culture of rooms, as well as 16S rRNA sequencing to define the environmental microbiome in a subset of samples.Results:The probability of detecting resistant Gram-negative organisms, including Enterobacterales, Acinetobacter, and Pseudomonas, increased with distance from the patient. In contrast, Clostridioides difficile and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus were more likely to be detected in close proximity to the patient. Resistant Pseudomonas and S. aureus were enriched in these hotspots despite broad deposition of 16S rRNA gene sequences assigned to the same genera, suggesting modifiable factors that permit the persistence of these MDROs.Conclusions:MDRO hotspots can be defined by distance from patient and wastewater reservoirs. Evaluating how MDROs are enriched relative to bacterial DNA deposition helps to identify healthcare micro-environments and suggests how targeted environmental cleaning or design approaches could prevent MDRO persistence and reduce infection risk.



Introduction:The healthcare environment is a reservoir for multidrug-resistant bacterial organisms (MDROs) that contribute to risk for healthcare-associated infections (HAIs). Prior studies have highlighted specific sites in the healthcare environment as reservoirs for MDROs. Surface contamination with MDROs frequently occurs on high-touch objects (HTOs) and within bathroom and wastewater sites in hospitals.1-8 In the case of Clostridioides difficile, this contamination has been shown to contribute directly to infection risk: patients occupying rooms where the previous inhabitant was infected with C. difficile are at increased risk of this infection, and surface contamination has been shown to precede acquisition.9-11The persistence of MDROs and HAI-associated bacteria in the healthcare environment depends on the deposition of bacteria from patients and healthcare workers, environmental features that sustain bacterial growth, and on environmental cleaning practices.12 Studies of the hospital microbiome have demonstrated bidirectional exchange between environmental surfaces and patient skin, with increased similarity over time.13 However, the hospital environment is not uniform. Because deposition, environmental surface selection, and cleaning practices vary across sites within a single hospital room, the environmental microbiome may likewise vary across close sites. We hypothesized that the competing distance between the source patient and the wastewater reservoirs may serve as an effective means to model bacterial colonization of the hospital room environment.To define the impact of distance from patient and wastewater sources (i.e., sinks and toilets) on the specific MDROs colonizing the healthcare environment, we performed a prospective cohort study with systematic sampling and MDRO culture of rooms occupied by patients from whom an MDRO had been isolated. To further discern the relationship between culturable MDROs and bacterial DNA deposition in the healthcare environment, we complemented our systematic, bacterial culture with 16S rRNA gene sequencing of the environmental samples.

Results:Detection of MDROs in the Proximity to Patient-Associated MDROs:We enrolled 51 patient rooms at the time of admitting a patient who had been recently diagnosed with MDRO colonization or infection by clinical culture and sampled the rooms repeatedly, obtaining a median of 3 sampling events per patient room (interquartile range (IQR) 2-4). Prior to patient entry, 75% of rooms (n=38) underwent terminal cleaning with ultraviolet germicidal irradiation (UVGI). All rooms received daily cleaning using Oxivir Plus, a hydrogen-peroxide cleaning product, with the exception of patients in C. difficile isolation whose rooms were cleaned with bleach. The most common enrolling isolate for patients included in the study was C. difficile (n=16, 31%), followed by ESBL-producing Enterobacterales (ESBL) (n=15, 29%), MRSA (n=13, 25%), VRE (n=7, 14%), and CR Gram-negative organisms (n=4, 8%). Across 51 subjects, we performed a total of 136 sampling events, with each event collecting composite samples from three in-room sites (near, intermediate, and far), and each composite sample plated on six MDRO-selective media. Table 1 shows the 11 MDROs identified across all enrolled subjects, the total number of times they were recovered from the healthcare environment, and frequency with which they were detected from cultured environmental specimens.Impact of Distance from Patient on MDRO Detection by Bacterial Culture:We related the probability of MDRO detection by selective culture to the distance from the patient and towards the wastewater sites, using mean distances from each of the three in-room sites sampled at each sampling event. Figure 1 depicts the impact of distance from patient on each of the 11 MDROs encountered in across our cohort. The probability of detecting ESBL and CR Gram-negative organisms, including Enterobacterales, Acinetobacter, and Pseudomonas, increased with distance from the patient. In contrast, C. difficile and MRSA were more likely to be detected in close proximity to the patient, and less likely to be detected in the wastewater sites. Of note, VRE was distributed more evenly across all areas. Given the patterns of MDRO detection observed at the genus level, we also examined the relationship between aggregate Gram-negative or Gram-positive MDROs and the distance from patient (Supplemental Figure 1). This analysis confirmed the substantial increase in Gram-negative MDRO detection with increasing distance from the patient (and towards the wastewater site), with the opposite pattern observed among Gram-positive MDROs.Impact of Distance from Patient on Bacterial DNA Deposition:We were able to complement comprehensive, culture-based surveillance with 16S rRNA gene sequencing to detect bacterial DNA deposition in the healthcare environment in a subset of 123 specimens from 16 of our cohort’s subjects. In Figure 2, we compare the impact of distance from patient on the bacterial DNA reads assigned to the 11 genera from which MDROs were cultured. We found that Enterobaceriaceae, including Citrobacter, Enterobacter, Klebsiella, and Raoultella, exhibited a similar pattern as did bacterial cultures, increasing with distance from the patient and in proximity to the wastewater sites. In contrast, bacterial DNA assigned to Acinetobacter and Pseudomonas was distributed more evenly across all areas, though bacterial culture was more likely to detect these genera in the wastewater sites. Bacterial DNA reads assigned to Staphylococcus were also distributed evenly across all areas, though bacterial culture was more likely to detect MRSA in close proximity to patients.Relationship Between Bacterial DNA Deposition and MDRO Detection in the Healthcare Environment:To evaluate the relationship between bacterial DNA contamination detected by untargeted 16S rRNA gene sequencing and environmental MDRO risk, we performed mixed-effects regression to determine the association between genus-level bacterial DNA abundance and the probability of MDRO detection across all observed MDRO genera and sampling sites. A positive association was observed across multiple genera and sites, but the only association that achieved high posterior certainty was Pseudomonas in the far-from-patient site, finding that as the probability of detecting Pseudomonas DNA increased, so too did the probability of culturing CR Pseudomonas at the site (OR 1.47, 95%CI 1.09 - 1.99). We further examined how bacterial community features, defined by 16S rRNA gene sequencing and qPCR, changed with distance from the patient and wastewater sites, as well as the association between bacterial community features and MDRO detection. We found that total bacterial abundance (16S rRNA gene copies per cm2 of surface area measured by qPCR) increased with proximity to wastewater sites, but the increase was small (1466 copies per cm2, 95%CI 670 – 2454 copies per cm2). Bacterial community Shannon diversity and richness (number of ASVs) did not change significantly with distance (Figure 3). The only association between a bacterial community feature (i.e., total bacterial abundance, diversity or richness) and probability of MDRO detection that achieved high posterior certainty was the association between total 16S rRNA gene copies and Pseudomonas detection (OR 1.19, 95%CI 1.03 – 1.41).

Relationship Between Bacterial DNA Deposition and MDRO Detection in the Healthcare Environment:To evaluate the relationship between bacterial DNA contamination detected by untargeted 16S rRNA gene sequencing and environmental MDRO risk, we performed mixed-effects regression to determine the association between genus-level bacterial DNA abundance and the probability of MDRO detection across all observed MDRO genera and sampling sites. A positive association was observed across multiple genera and sites, but the only association that achieved high posterior certainty was Pseudomonas in the far-from-patient site, finding that as the probability of detecting Pseudomonas DNA increased, so too did the probability of culturing CR Pseudomonas at the site (OR 1.47, 95%CI 1.09 - 1.99). We further examined how bacterial community features, defined by 16S rRNA gene sequencing and qPCR, changed with distance from the patient and wastewater sites, as well as the association between bacterial community features and MDRO detection. We found that total bacterial abundance (16S rRNA gene copies per cm2 of surface area measured by qPCR) increased with proximity to wastewater sites, but the increase was small (1466 copies per cm2, 95%CI 670 – 2454 copies per cm2). Bacterial community Shannon diversity and richness (number of ASVs) did not change significantly with distance (Figure 3). The only association between a bacterial community feature (i.e., total bacterial abundance, diversity or richness) and probability of MDRO detection that achieved high posterior certainty was the association between total 16S rRNA gene copies and Pseudomonas detection (OR 1.19, 95%CI 1.03 – 1.41).

Discussion:



We sought to define how bacterial deposition and persistence in the healthcare environment is affected by proximity to patient and wastewater sources, and we found clear distinctions between near-patient and far-from-patient/near-wastewater sites in patient rooms. The probability of detecting ESBL-producing and CR Gram-negative organisms, including Enterobacterales, Acinetobacter, and Pseudomonas, increased with distance from the patient. In contrast, C. difficile and MRSA were more likely to be detected in close proximity to the patient, and less likely to be detected in the wastewater sites.



We compared culturable MDROs to bacterial DNA deposition in order to identify areas where bacteria are deposited but are not viable due to either environmental features (e.g., microenvironments that do not support bacterial growth or persistence) or cleaning practices. In some cases, the pattern of culturable MDROs matched bacterial DNA deposition: Enterobacterales detection increased with distance from the patient and in proximity to the wastewater sites, mirroring the overall detection of 16S copies or bacterial abundance. But in other cases, the discrepancy between bacterial DNA detection and culturable MDROs suggested environmental selection: though bacterial DNA assigned to Pseudomonas and Staphylococcus was distributed evenly across all areas, bacterial culture identified distinct hotspots for CR Pseudomonas and MRSA, far from and near patients, respectively. Of note, both bacterial DNA assigned to Enterococcus and culturable VRE were detected across distances, suggesting that bacterial deposition from stool is extensive. The decreasing probability of C. difficile and decreasing Clostridium DNA deposition as sampling became closer to the wastewater sites may be attributable to more effective cleaning practices specific to these pathogens within wastewater sites.



Because deposition of bacteria from patients and healthcare workers, environmental features that sustain bacterial growth, and environmental cleaning practices vary within patient rooms, we identified micro-environments as we sampled along an axis from the patient’s bed towards the wastewater sites. By simultaneously measuring viable MDROs by selective bacterial culture and bacterial DNA deposition by 16S rRNA gene sequencing, and by incorporating distance from patient and wastewater reservoirs, we defined hotspots specific to each MDRO and linked those hotspots to specific features of the healthcare environment. Our approach yields new insight into the contamination of surfaces within the hospital rooms and may be used to guide future studies focused on preventing transmission of MDROs through targeted environmental cleaning approaches.



Our study has several limitations. First, sampling sites were divided into three composite in-room categories rather than multiple individual specimens. While this limits the specificity of surface-specific contamination, this approach decreased the resources needed for sampling a greater number of surfaces in total. Second, bacterial culture was limited to only multidrug-resistant pathogens and C. difficile. This approach was taken to limit detection to pathogens of greatest clinical significance within healthcare settings, but it is possible that culturable, non-MDRO bacteria persist in different distributions. Third, culture was non-quantitative to limit complexity of specimen processing. These limitations decrease the specificity of site-specific features, but should not reduce the effectiveness of this methodology for modeling contamination of the healthcare space. Fourth, 16S sequencing was used to assign genus-level taxonomic assignment rather than species-level identification, and only a subset of specimens underwent 16S sequencing. Finally, our study was restricted to a single, academic medical center. We expect that the environmental MDROs observed would differ at other medical facilities, according to local prevalence. But we believe that modeling MDRO deposition and persistence using our distance-based approach will yield similar insights.



In conclusion, using distance modeling between the patient bed and wastewater sites is an effective method to identify organism-specific hot spots for bacterial contamination. While further studies are needed to validate this method, this technique may be useful in identifying a patient-specific approach to room design and environmental cleaning to prevent persistence of contamination and transmission of pathogens to subsequent room occupants.
