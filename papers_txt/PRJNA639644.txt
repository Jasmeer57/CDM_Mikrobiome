Abstract
Gut microbiota have been implicated in the development of cancer. Colorectal and gastric cancers, the major gastrointestinal tract
cancers, are closely connected with the gut microbiome. Nevertheless, the characteristics of gut microbiota composition that
correlate with gastric cancer are unclear. In this study, we investigated gut microbiota alterations during the progression of gastric
cancer to identify the most relevant taxa associated with gastric cancer and evaluated the potential of the microbiome as an indicator
for the diagnosis of gastric cancer. Compared with the healthy group, gut microbiota composition and diversity shifted in patients
with gastric cancer. Different bacteria were used to design a random forest model, which provided an area under the curve value of
0.91. Verification samples achieved a true positive rate of 0.83 in gastric cancer. Principal component analysis showed that gastritis
shares some microbiome characteristics of gastric cancer. Chemotherapy reduced the elevated bacteria levels in gastric cancer by
more than half. More importantly, we found that the genera Lactobacillus and Megasphaera were associated with gastric cancer.
Key Points
• Gut microbiota has high sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing patients with gastric cancer from healthy individuals,
indicating that gut microbiota is a potential noninvasive tool for the diagnosis of gastric cancer.
• Gastritis shares some microbiota features with gastric cancer, and chemotherapy reduces the microbial abundance and
diversity in gastric cancer patients.
• Two bacterial taxa, namely, Lactobacillus and Megasphaera, are predictive markers for gastric cancer.

Introduction
The human gut microbiota is a complex ecosystem with ap-
proximately 100 trillion gut bacteria (Kamada et al. 2013).
The intestinal microbiota have a symbiotic relationship with
their host, regulating host immune responses, energy metabo-
lism, pathogen elimination, and cancer development (Ohtani
2015). The gut microecosystem plays an important role in
human health. Recent research revealed that dysbiosis of the
gut microbiota gives rise to many diseases (Holmes et al.
2012), especially in the gastrointestinal tract. Colorectal can-
cer and gastric cancer are major gastrointestinal tract cancers
that have attracted widespread attention in the past 5 years
(Meng et al. 2018). There is no doubt that gut microbiota play
an important role in promoting colorectal carcinogenesis
(Alhinai et al. 2019; Song et al. 2020). A large number of
sequencing studies have revealed microbial compositional

and ecological changes in colorectal cancer patients, and these
changes can potentially be used as biomarkers for the detec-
tion of colorectal cancer (Ai et al. 2019; Liang et al. 2017;
Scott et al. 2019). Experiments in germ-free mice have pro-
vided evidence for the importance of host–microbe interac-
tions in colorectal cancer development (Kim et al. 2007).
However, little is known about alterations in the gut microbi-
ota composition during the progression of gastric cancer.
Gastric cancer is the third most common cancer worldwide
and is one of the primary causes of cancer-related deaths,
especially in China (Bray et al. 2018). Gastric cancer is a
multifactorial disease involving host genetics, environmental
factors, and microbial factors (Vinasco et al. 2019). Previous
studies have shown that Helicobacter pylori infection is the
most important factor for gastric cancer (Herrera and
Parsonnet 2009). Chronic H. pylori stimulates immune and
inflammatory responses that reduce acid secretion, leading to
excessive gastric bacterial colonization. Thus, H. pylori infec-
tion contributes to chronic gastritis and gastric cancer
(Doorakkers et al. 2016; Thorell et al. 2017). Many reports
show that gastric microbiota change in gastric cancer patients
(Wang et al. 2016; Yu et al. 2017). Recently, Wong et al.
(2018) demonstrated a significant difference in gastric micro-
biota in patients with intestinal metaplasia and gastric cancer.
Hsieh et al. (2018) showed that the abundance of Clostridium
and Fusobacterium was increased in patients with gastric can-
cer than in those with gastritis and intestinal metaplasia. These
reports clearly show that gastric cancer patients have different
gastric microecologies than other gastric diseases.
Nevertheless, only a very small number of studies explore
the relationship between gut microbiota and gastric cancer.
In this study, the gut microbiota differed when comparing 33
gastric cancer patients (GC) to 32 healthy individuals (H).
When analyzing the data using a random forest model, the area
under the curve (AUC) was 0.91, distinguishing GC from H.
To verify the reliability of this distinguishing ability, 29 gastric
cancers and 29 controls were collected for receiver operating
characteristics (ROC) analysis, and a true positive rate of 0.83
was achieved. In addition, gastritis patients (Gas) share some
microbiome features with GC, chemotherapy reduces the mi-
crobial abundance and diversity in the microbiome of GC, and
colorectal cancer patients (CRC) share similar features with
GC. Ultimately, we found two bacterial taxa, namely,
Lactobacillus and Megasphaera, which can be used as a pre-
dictive marker for gastric cancer diagnosis.
Materials and methods
Patient enrollment and fecal sample collection
This study was conducted at the Zhejiang Provincial People’s
Hospital, Hangzhou, China; 227 individuals were recruited:
83 with gastric cancer, 54 with gastritis, 29 with colorectal
cancer, and 61 healthy individuals. Participants who received
any antibiotics within 4 weeks of study enrollment and those
receiving medicine associated with stomach diseases were
excluded. The participants with gastritis and gastric cancer
underwent an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. The diagnosis
of gastritis is chronic atrophic gastritis. For gastric cancer,
only those with test results of non-cardia cancer were includ-
ed. Histologically, we did not differentiate between the
intestinal- and diffuse-type gastric cancers. Healthy individ-
uals were recruited from the Department of Health
Examination Center, Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital;
there were no H. pylori infection and no gastrointestinal symp-
toms. Clinical data for the participants were collected by
reviewing medical records (Table 1) and included age, gender,
body mass index, and tumor stage. Fecal samples were col-
lected from every participant and frozen immediately at −80
°C for subsequent analysis.
DNA extraction
DNA extraction was performed from 100-mg stool samples
using the Guhe StoolMag DNA Kit (Guhe info, Zhejiang,
China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Negative
controls involved performing the extraction without any clin-
ical sample.
16S rRNA gene sequencing
PCR amplification of the bacterial 16S rRNA genes V4
region was performed using the forward primer 515F (5′-
GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and the reverse prim-
er 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′).
Sample-specific 7-bp barcodes were incorporated into the
primers for multiplex sequencing. The PCR components
contained 25 μl of Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master
Mix (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), 3 μl
(10 μM) of each forward and reverse primer, 10 μl of
DNA Template, and 6 μl of ddH2O. Thermal cycling
consisted of initial denaturation at 98 °C for 30 s, followed
by 25 cycles consisting of denaturation at 98 °C for 15 s,
annealing at 58 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 15
s, with a final extension of 1 min at 72 °C. PCR amplicons
were purified with Agencourt AMPure XP Beads
(Beckman Coulter, Indianapolis, IN) and quantified using
the PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad,
CA, USA). After the individual quantification step,
amplicons were pooled in equal amounts, and pair-end
2× 150 bp sequencing was performed using the Illumina
HiSeq4000 platform at Guhe Info technology Co. Ltd
(Hangzhou, China).

Sequence and statistical analysis
The Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology (QIIME,
v1.9.1) pipeline was employed to process the sequencing data,
as previously described (Caporaso et al. 2010). Briefly, raw
sequencing reads with exact matches to the barcodes were
assigned to respective samples and identified as valid se-
quences. The low-quality sequences were filtered through the
following criteria (Chen and Jiang 2014; Gill et al. 2006): se-
quences that had a length of < 150 bp, sequences that had
average Phred scores of < 20, sequences that contained ambig-
uous bases, and sequences that contained mononucleotide re-
peats of > 8 bp. Paired-end reads were assembled using FLASH
(Magoc and Salzberg 2011). Operational taxonomic unit
(OTU) picking using VSEARCH v2.4.4 included dereplication
(–derep_fulllength), cluster (–cluster_fast,–id 0.97), and detec-
tion of chimeras (–uchime_ref) (Rognes et al. 2016). A repre-
sentative sequence was selected from each OTU using default
parameters. OTU taxonomic classification was conducted by
VSEARCH searching the representative sequences set against
the SILVA128 database (Quast et al. 2013). An OTU table was
further generated to record the abundance of each OTU in each
sample and the taxonomy of these OTUs. OTUs containing less
than 0.001% of total sequences across all samples were
discarded. To minimize the difference of sequencing depth
across samples, an averaged, rounded rarefied OTU table was
generated by averaging 100 evenly resampled OTU subsets
under the 90% of the minimum sequencing depth for further
analysis.
Sequence data analyses were mainly performed using
QIIME (Caporaso et al. 2010) and R packages (v3.2.0)
(https://www.r-project.org/). OTU-level alpha diversity indi-
ces, such as Chao1 richness estimator, ACE metric
(Abundance-based Coverage Estimator), PD_whole_tree,
Shannon diversity index, and Simpson index, were calculated
using the OTU table in QIIME. OTU-level ranked abundance
curves were generated to compare the richness and evenness
of OTUs among samples. Beta diversity analysis was per-
formed to investigate the structural variation of microbial
communities across samples using UniFrac distance metrics
(Lozupone and Knight 2005; Lozupone et al. 2007) and visu-
alized via principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) (Ramette
2007). Differences in the Unifrac distances for pairwise com-
parisons among groups were determined using Student’s t test
and the Monte Carlo permutation test with 1000 permutations
and visualized through the box-and-whiskers plots. Principal
component analysis (PCA) was also conducted based on the
genus-level compositional profiles (Ramette 2007). The sig-
nificance of differentiation of microbiota structure among
groups was assessed by PERMANOVA (permutational mul-
tivariate analysis of variance) (McArdle and Anderson 2001)
using the R package “vegan” (Oksanen et al. 2014). Taxa
abundances at the phylum and genus levels were statistically
compared among samples or groups by the Kruskal test from
the R stats package. Random forest analysis was applied to
discriminate the samples from different groups using the R
package “random Forest” with 1000 trees and all default set-
tings (Breiman 2001; Pang et al. 2006). The generalization
error was estimated using 10-fold cross-validation. The ex-
pected “baseline” error was also included, which was obtained
by a classifier that simply predicts the most common category
label. The linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size
(LEfSe) method was performed to identify significantly dif-
ferent genus. The LDA value threshold was set at 3.0, and p
value < 0.05 was determined to have significantly different
abundances. The output file was further analyzed using the
Statistical Analysis of Metagenomic Profiles (STAMP) soft-
ware package v2.1.3 (Parks et al. 2014).

Results
The gut microbiota profile differs in gastric cancer
patients than in healthy individuals
To determine if the gut microbiota differed between patients
with GC and H, we compared the microbiota structure between
the two groups. The top ten microbial communities for each
group were analyzed and presented at the phylum and genus
levels (Fig. 1 a and b). We found that gut microbiota composi-
tion in GC was significantly different from H at both the phy-
lum and genus levels (effect size > 0.1%). Eight phyla, namely,
Chloroflexi, Verrucomicrobia, Acidobacteria, Planctomycetes,
Nitrospirae, Actinobacteria, TM7, and Fusobacteria, were in-
creased in GC than in H (Fig. 1c). Twenty-eight genera signif-
icantly differed between the two groups; all were increased in
the GC group except Megamonas (Firmicutes phylum), which
decreased in gastric cancer. The 27 enriched genera in GC
patients were Leptotrichia, Fusobacterium (Fusobacteria phy-
lum), Prevotella, Porphyromonas, Capnocytophaga
(Bacteroidetes phylum), Lactococcus, Streptococcus, Bacillus,
Selenomonas, Peptoniphilus, Anaerococcus, Finegoldia
(Firmicutes phylum), Methylobacterium, Proteus,
Aggregatibacter, Haemophilus, Kingella, Neisseria,
Cardiobacterium (Proteobacteria phylum), DA101
(Verrucomicrobia phylum), Nitrospira (Nitrospirae phylum),
Candidatus Solibacter, Corynebacterium, Actinomyces,
Bifidobacterium, Atopobium (Acidobacteria phylum), and
Thermus (Thermi phylum). Alpha and beta diversity were
assessed to determine the bacterial diversity of the gut microbi-
ota in the GC and H groups (Fig. 1d). Patients with GC had a
significantly higher microbial diversity (p = 0.002) (Fig. 1e), as
determined using the Shannon index. A principal component
analysis (PCoA) based on weighted UniFrac was conducted to
display microbiome space between groups. This analysis re-
vealed a significant difference in gut microbiota between the
H and GC groups (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1f). Compared with H, GC
has different composition and diversity in gut microbiota.
Diagnostic value of gut microbiome for gastric cancer
To evaluate the potential ability of the gut microbiome to
distinguish GC from H, we established and tested a random
forest classifier model. From top to bottom, the major bacteria
responsible for distinguishing between GC and H are shown
in Fig. 2a. The error rate (16.67%) represents the error rate of
using the characteristic microbiota to predict the classification
model. The diagnostic efficacy of gut microbiota was assessed
using an ROC analysis; the AUC value was 0.91 in the GC
compared with the H group (Fig. 2b). For verification, 29
controls and 29 patients with gastric carcinoma were used to
validate the diagnostic efficacy for gastric cancer. The true
positive rate achieved an AUC value of 0.83 when extracting
the top 28 altered bacteria for ROC curve analysis (Fig. 2c).
Gastritis patients share some community and
diversity features with gastric cancer
To further analyze the evolution of GC-associated microbiota
as the disease progressed, we analyzed fecal specimens of gas-
tritis patients. The alpha diversity of the microbiome analyzed
using the Shannon index showed no significant differences be-
tween Gas and GC subjects (p = 0.052) (Fig. 3a). The altered
bacteria from GC were added to the PCoA for Gas. The PCoA
changes are shown in a two-dimensional plot using dimension
reduction. The PCoA diagram shows a significant separation of
the H and Gas. Meanwhile, Gas was well separated from GC in
the PC2 dimension but partly overlapping in the PC1 dimen-
sion, indicating that Gas shares some characteristics with GC
(Fig. 3b). Nevertheless, the bacterial communities did not over-
lap completely between Gas and GC. The bacterial phyla,
Verrucomicrobia, Chloroflexi, and Acidobacteria, and the gen-
era, Bifidobacterium, Anaerococcus, Finegoldia, and DA101,
accumulated in GC but not gastritis (Supplemental Fig. S1a and
b). In contrast, the abundance of the genus of Dorea and
Bacteroides was rich in Gas but not in GC patients.
Chemotherapy reduces the microbial communities
and diversity in gastric cancer
To understand the effects of chemotherapy on gut microbiota
in GC, we compared the gut microbiota from GC patients
before and after chemotherapy. The results revealed 6 phyla
and 14 genera that were reduced in chemotherapy patients
(Supplemental Fig. S1c and d), indicating that chemotherapy
reduces the microbial abundance in GC. More importantly, we
found that most of the reduced bacteria induced by chemo-
therapy were the characteristic bacteria of GC. At the phylum
level, four of the six chemotherapy-reduced bacteria were el-
evated in GC, including p_TM7, p_Fusobacteria,
p_Chloroflexi, and p_Verrucomicrobia (Fig. 3d). At the genus
level, 12 of the 14 bacteria reduced by chemotherapy were
characteristic of GC, including g_Lactococcus,
g_Corynebacterium, g_DA101, g_Actinomyces, g_Kingella,
g_Capnocytophaga, g_Selenomonas, g_Leptotrichia,
g_Haemophilus, g_Streptococcus, g_Cardiobacterium, and
g_Methylobacterium (Supplemental Fig. S1e). Alpha diversi-
ty was reduced after chemotherapy in GC (p = 0.036) (Fig.
3a), and the PCoA analysis using weighted UniFrac showed
no difference in gut microbiota between GC and GC after
chemotherapy (p = 0.267) (Fig. 3c). Taken together, our re-
sults show that chemotherapy reduces the microbial abun-
dance and diversity in GC, and most of the reduced bacteria
induced by chemotherapy are the bacteria that are elevated in
GC.

Part of the increased bacteria in the gut microbiota may
come from the gastric mucosa. For example, in gastric cancer,
the gastric microbiomes were enriched in bacteria, such as

growth and leading to the overgrowth of bacteria in the stom-

detected in fecal samples. On the other hand, we discovered
that the microbiota in Gas is more similar to GC than H group,

Another important finding in our study is that chemother-
apy decreased the microbiota elevated in gastric cancer. This
finding is analogous to the decrease in gut microbiota diver-
sity and abundance after chemotherapy in non-Hodgkin lym-
phoma (Montassier et al. 2014, 2015). In our study, 14 genera
were decreased after chemotherapy, including 12 genera
enriched in GC versus H. This is in agreement with
Alexander et al. (2017) who stated that many cancer therapies
will affect not only the metabolism of the host but also the gut
bacteria themselves. We suspect that chemotherapy drugs im-
pact on the pathogenic bacteria and then gastric cancer.
Another unique discovery in our study is that the bacterial
shift in gastric cancer has some similarities to colorectal can-
cer (Fig. 4). In our study, the altered bacteria in colorectal
cancer patients were consistent with previous studies
(Jahani-Sherafat et al. 2018; Nakatsu et al. 2015). Several
studies show that a shift in intestinal microbiota promotes
the development of colorectal cancer (Farhana et al. 2018).
We suppose that this effect also exists in the development of
gastric cancer, since the two gastrointestinal cancer types have
similar microbiome shifts. For example, Fusobacterium may
regulate inflammatory and oncogenic responses; a recent
study revealed that these bacteria induce inflammation via
the Toll-like receptor 4 (Chen et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2018)
in Chinese individuals developing gastric cancer (Castano-
Rodriguez et al. 2013, 2014).
Of note, the patient age in the GC was different from the H
group in our study. However, there is some evidence that the
differences in the microbiome were not caused by age. For
example, Gas and H had similar ages, but the microbiota from
Gas and H shared fewer features than the microbiota in Gas
and GC. Furthermore, the microbiota structure alterations of
CRC in this study were consistent with previous studies
(Flemer et al. 2017; Hale et al. 2017; Jahani-Sherafat et al.
2018; Wang et al. 2012).

large, and we did not highlight the classification of tumors
according to their molecular phenotype. Thus, a larger number
of samples and tumor staging should be considered in future
