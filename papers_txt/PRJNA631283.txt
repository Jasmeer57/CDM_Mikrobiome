Autoreactivity in naïve human fetal B cells is
associated with commensal bacteria recognition
Jeff W. Chen1*, Tyler A. Rice1, Jason M. Bannock1, Agata A. Bielecka1†, Juliet D. Strauss1‡,
Jason R. Catanzaro1, Haowei Wang1, Laurence C. Menard1§, Jennifer H. Anolik2,
Noah W. Palm1¶, Eric Meffre1,3¶
Restricted V(D)J recombination during fetal development was postulated to limit antibody
repertoire breadth and prevent autoimmunity. However, newborn serum contains abundant
autoantibodies, suggesting that B cell tolerance during gestation is not yet fully established.
To investigate this apparent paradox, we evaluated the reactivities of more than 450 antibodies
cloned from single B cells from human fetal liver, bone marrow, and spleen. We found that
incomplete B cell tolerance in early human fetal life favored the accumulation of polyreactive
B cells that bound both apoptotic cells and commensal bacteria from healthy adults. Thus, the
restricted fetal preimmune repertoire contains potentially beneficial self-reactive innate-like
B cell specificities that may facilitate the removal of apoptotic cells during development and
shape gut microbiota assembly after birth.
V
(D)J recombination generates a diverse
adult antibody repertoire, but immuno-
globulin heavy chain (IgH) rearrange-
ments are restricted during prenatal
development (1). Restriction of the fetal
antibody repertoire is thought to limit auto-
immunity by preventing the generation of self-
reactive B cell clones (1). However, fetal B cell
receptors are often polyreactive (2), and hu-
man newborns display abundant serum IgM
autoantibodies (3), which suggests incomplete
B cell tolerance during gestation. We sought
to determine the extent to which B cell tol-
erance is established in early human life and
whether differences between fetal and adult
B cell receptor repertoires result in distinct
antibody binding specificities (4). We gen-
erated more than 450 recombinant anti-
bodies cloned from single B cells isolated from
liver, bone marrow (BM), and spleens from
four second-trimester human fetuses and ob-
served an enrichment in the downstream var-
iable regions of immunoglobulin heavy chain
(VH), D7-27, and upstream heavy-chain join-
ing region (JH) gene segment usage, as pre-
viously reported (tables S1 to S3 and figs. S1
and S2) (1, 5).
Central B cell tolerance in adultsisestablished
via elimination of developing autoreactive clones
between the early immature and immature
B cell stages in the BM (4). We observed a sig-
nificant purging of self-reactive and anti-
nuclear clones in immature B cells from both
fetal liver and BM, suggesting that central
B cell tolerance is largely functional in early
fetal life (Fig. 1, A to D, and fig. S3, A to D). By
contrast, polyreactive clones were only mod-
estly reduced during early fetal B cell selection
but were purged from adult immature B cells
(Fig. 1E). We next evaluated whether central
B cell tolerance was mediated by secondary
recombination of the immunoglobulin kappa
(Igk) and then lambda (Igl) loci (i.e., receptor
editing) (6). Diverse V–J recombination events
were observed on both the Igk and Igl loci in
fetal B cells, but fetal immature B cells were
enriched in in-frame Igl transcripts as com-
pared with their adult counterparts (Fig. 1, F
and G, and figs. S5, A to C, and S6). Increased
secondary recombination events in fetal im-
mature B cells were also evidenced by greater
usage of distal variable regions of immuno-
globulin k chain (Vks) and downstream kappa
chain joining region 2 (Jk2) and Jk3 seg-
ments, decreased usage of the most-upstream
Jk1 segment, and increased recombination with
the k-deleting element, which enables Igl re-
combination, as compared with adult counter-
parts (Fig. 1, H to J). Thus, central B cell tolerance
in fetal primary lymphoid organs silences de-
veloping autoreactive clones via enhanced sec-
ondary recombination(6) but is not as efficient
at eliminating polyreactive clones.
In adults, a peripheral B cell tolerance
checkpoint counterselects autoreactive new
emigrant or transitional B cells before they
enter the mature naïve B cell compartment
(Fig. 2, A and B) (4). Spleens from second-
trimester human fetuses are immature and
contain naïve B cells that may resemble those
from fetal peripheral blood (figs. S7 and S8).
Thus, we assessed peripheral B cell tolerance
by evaluating autoreactivity in new emigrant
B cells and mature naïve B cells from fetal
spleens versus adult peripheral blood (Fig. 2, A
and B, and fig. S7). Unlike in adults, human
epithelial type 2 (HEp-2) reactive clones in
fetuses remained stable between the new
emigrant or transitional B cell and mature
naïve B cell stages (Fig. 2A and fig. S9A). Fetal
mature naïve B cells also contained an elevated
proportion of polyreactive clones (Fig. 2B and
fig. S9B). These data suggest that the peripheral
B cell tolerance checkpoint is not yet functional
during second-trimester gestation.
Because peripheral B cell tolerance is con-
trolled by T cells, including regulatory T cells
(Tregs) (7, 8), we assessed the proportions of
T cells and Tregs versus B cells in fetal spleens
(Fig. 2, C to F). Although the proportion of
T cells in adult blood far exceeded that of
B cells, B cells were more prevalent than T cells
in fetal spleens (Fig. 2, C and D) (5). In addition,
the ratios of Tregs to new emigrant or transi-
tional B cells were significantly reduced in fetal
spleen, liver, and BM compared with adult
blood and spleen, despite Tregs being more
prevalent in fetuses than adults (Fig. 2, E and
F, and fig. S8) (9). The limited numbers of
T cells and Tregs present during early fetal
life may be insufficient to establish robust
peripheral B cell tolerance and thus enable the
accumulation of autoreactive and polyreactive
innate-like B cells in fetuses. These cells may
resemble murine autoreactive B-1 cells produced
by the fetal liver and positively selected to
secrete natural antibodies (10, 11). Thus, human
fetal liver-derived B cells, which can self-renew
and persist long-term in the peripheral B cell
pool (12), may be an important source of nat-
ural antibodies into adulthood.
To explore the hypothesis that fetal self-
reactive B cells encode potentially beneficial
natural antibodies that may aid in clearance
of dead cells (13), we examined the binding of
recombinant antibodies cloned from fetal and
adult B cells to live, early apoptotic, or dead
cells (Fig. 3A). Although antibodies from a few
early immature fetal B cells bound live cells,
live-cell reactivity was purged from more ma-
ture B cell compartments (Fig. 3B). By contrast,
early apoptotic and dead-cell-reactive clones
remained present throughout B cell devel-
opment in both fetuses and adults and were
significantly more prevalent in fetal than in
adult B cells (Fig. 3B and table S3). Thus,
tolerance does not appear to counterselect early
apoptotic and dead-cell-reactive clones, and in-
creased autoreactivity among fetal B cells may
favor the clearance of cellular debris generated
by developmental apoptosis.

reactivity to commensal bacteria in IgA+ B cells
in both mice and humans (14, 15). We therefore

To determine which commensal bacteria
were bound by stool-reactive recombinant
antibodies, we stained human fecal bacteria
with 10 stool-reactive antibodies cloned from
fetal B cells and 12 stool-reactive antibodies
cloned from adult B cells, separated the antibody-
bound and -unbound bacteria, and performed
16S rRNA gene sequencing (17). Stool-reactive
antibodies enriched bacteria from the genera
Blautia and Dorea by threefold and more
than 100-fold, respectively, but did not enrich
phylogenetically related taxa from the genera
Faecalibacterium and Ruminococcus (Fig. 3, E
and F, and fig. S10, C and D). Dorea species
were similarly enriched across four stool sam-
ples from independent donors, whereas other
bacterial binding patterns were specific to each
individual microbiome (fig. S10E). Blautia
and Dorea were not enriched by six non-stool-
reactive antibodies (fig. S11). Stool-reactive anti-
bodies also bound pure Dorea and Blautia
isolates but did not bind Faecalibacterium or
Brevundimonas strains (figs. S12 and S13).
Mature naïve B cells with intrinsic commensal
bacteria reactivity may serve as precursors for
IgA-secreting plasma cells in the gut. The re-
sulting antibodies may favor the selection or
expansion of Blautia and Dorea species, which
are highly coated with secretory IgA in healthy
humans and reduced in IgA-deficient subjects
(17, 18).
Fetal mature naïve B cells were four times
more likely to be polyreactive, recognize dead
cells, and/or bind commensals than were their
adult counterparts (Fig. 4, A and B). Hence,
self-reactivity of the fetal antibody repertoire is
associated with binding to commensal bacteria.

These studies illustrate that incomplete
B cell tolerance mechanisms in early human
fetal life favor the accumulation of autore-
active fetal innate-like B cells that also bind
to specific commensal bacteria. The preva-
lence of anticipatory commensal reactivities
before microbial exposure may promote co-
lonization of the gut by beneficial commen-
sal taxa and facilitate the development of
advantageous host-microbiota interactions
after birth (21, 22).
