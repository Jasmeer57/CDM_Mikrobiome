Summary 
Bifidobacterium bifidum is reported to be among the first colonizers of the newborn’s 
gastrointestinal tract due to its ability to metabolize Human Milk Oligosaccharides (HMOs). In 
order to investigate biological features that allow this bifidobacterial species to colonize a newborn, 
bifidobacterial ITS profiling of stool samples of 50 mother-infant dyads, as well as corresponding 
breastmilk samples, was performed. Hierarchical clustering based on bifidobacterial population 
profiles found in infant fecal samples revealed the presence of four bifidobacterial clusters or so-
called bifidotypes. B. bifidum was shown to be a key member among bifidotypes, in which its 
presence correlate with several different bifidobacterial species retrieved in infant fecal samples. 
For this reason, we investigated cross-feeding behavior facilitated by B. bifidum on a bioreactor 
model using human milk as growth substrate. Transcriptional profiles of this strain were evaluated 
when grown on nine specific glycans typically constituting HMOs. Remarkably, these analyses 
suggest extensive co-evolution with the host and other bifidobacterial species in terms of resource 
provision and sharing, respectively, activities that appear to support a bifidobacteria-dominant 
microbiome.

Introduction 
Bifidobacteria are among the first colonizers of the human intestine (O'Callaghan and van Sinderen, 
2016), where these commensals are believed to modulate various metabolic and immune activities 
of their host (Pokusaeva et al., 2011; Ruiz et al., 2017). It has become clear that several species of 
this genus, e.g. Bifidobacterium bifidum, Bifidobacterium breve and Bifidobacterium longum, 
possess genetic adaptations to facilitate colonization of the infant gut (Matamoros et al., 2013; 
Milani et al., 2017b). Recently, a number of studies have presented comprehensive data showing 
that these three species can be maternally inherited by vertical transmission (Milani et al., 2015a; 
Duranti et al., 2017b; Vatanen et al., 2018; Moossavi et al., 2019). Further evidence has found that 
horizontal transfer of bifidobacterial DNA from mother to fetus may occur also via placenta 
(Satokari et al., 2009). In recent years, substantial efforts have been made to uncover and 
understand the molecular mechanisms that underpin interactions between members of the B. 
bifidum taxon and their human host. These in vitro and in vivo research efforts have revealed that 
various extracellular proteins produced by members of the B. bifidum species, such as sortase-
dependent pili and the murein lytic enzyme TgaA, are involved in host-microbe interactions 
(Turroni et al., 2013; Guglielmetti et al., 2014; Ruiz et al., 2016). In particular, B. bifidum strains 
have been shown to adhere strongly to human epithelial intestinal cell lines, such as Caco-2 and 
HT29 (Guglielmetti et al., 2009; Serafini et al., 2013). 
Another intriguing feature of B. bifidum supporting its genetic adaptation to the human gut is 
represented by its ability to metabolize host-derived glycans, in particular mucin (Ruas-Madiedo et 
al., 2008; Turroni et al., 2010; Turroni et al., 2011). Pan-genome-based analyses coupled with 
growth experiments involving various B. bifidum strains showed that the genetic information

responsible for mucin metabolism is constituted by a gene arsenal, which is uniquely present in the 
core-genome sequences of this bifidobacterial species (Duranti et al., 2015). Furthermore, the B. 
bifidum pan-genome contains genes encoding extracellular glycosyl hydrolases related to the 
metabolism of Human Milk Oligosaccharides (HMOs) (Asakuma et al., 2011; Kitaoka, 2012). 
These extracellular enzymes release carbohydrates, which can also be utilized by other 
(bifido)bacterial components of the infant microbiota, are responsible for a key microbe-microbe 
interaction described as cross-feeding (Egan et al., 2014a; Pereira and Berry, 2017; Turroni et al., 
2018). This syntrophic behavior is believed to directly affect the microbiota composition, thereby 
making B. bifidum an important component of the infant gut microbial community. 
The aim of the current study was to obtain insights into genetic adaptions of members of the B. 
bifidum species that facilitate vertical transmission from mother to newborn by exploiting milk as a 
vector. Transmission of this bifidobacterial species culminates in its establishment in the large 
intestine following passage through the gastric barrier. Its successful colonization of the infant gut 
enables interactions with other (bifido)bacterial taxa thereby promoting the development of a 
bifidobacteria-abundant community. In this context, co-existence of B. bifidum with other 
bifidobacterial species was evaluated by means of Internally Transcribed Spacer (ITS) 
bifidobacterial profiling. Our findings revealed that B. bifidum is the key member of a specific 
consortium of co-occurring bifidobacterial species. In order to detail the role exerted by B. bifidum 
in the newborn gut, we employed our model strain B. bifidum PRL2010 to determine transcriptomic 
profiles associated with growth on nine HMO/HMO-derived glycans as well as to investigate 
putative cross-feeding behavior by other co-occurring bifidobacterial taxa as facilitated by B. 
bifidum.

Results and Discussion 
The role of vertical transmission of bifidobacteria in the development of infant bifidotypes. 
Despite the major role played by bifidobacteria in defining the infant gut microbiome, we do not 
know if and how vertical transmission of bifidobacterial species among individuals may allow the 
formation of specific consortia of co-occurring bifidobacterial taxa, i.e. bifidotypes. This knowledge 
hiatus is primarily due to the lack of a precise species-level profiling of the bifidobacterial 
population in a sufficiently large cohort of infants. For this reason, the bifidobacterial population of 
22 mothers (fecal samples) and corresponding babies (fecal samples) and breast milk, was 
determined by bifidobacterial ITS profiling (Milani et al., 2014). In addition, previously published 
metagenomic data from 28 sets of mother-newborn pairs (based on analysis of fecal samples) were 
included in this study following bioinformatic re-analyses to ensure uniformity of the data (Milani 
et al., 2015a; Duranti et al., 2017b). This ITS-based approach allows accurate distinction between 
very closely related subspecies that exhibit an ITS amplicon identity of <93 % (Milani et al., 
2017a). Remarkably, this approach also permitted the identification of 89 putative novel 
bifidobacterial (sub)species (Milani et al., 2017a), 12 of which have subsequently been isolated and 
genomically decoded (Duranti et al., 2017a; Lugli et al., 2018; Duranti et al., 2019). Bifidobacterial 
ITS profiling of the above mentioned 50 mother-newborn sets, including re-analyzed sets, produced 
a total of 9,870,778 reads, encompassing an average of 48,625 reads per collected sample (Table 
S1). To validate these DNA sequencing data both in terms of qualitative and quantitative accuracy 
in the reconstruction of the bifidobacterial population, we included a mock community in the 
sequencing run constituted by known amounts of DNA extracted from 13 bifidobacterial species, as

Our ITS sequencing data revealed that OTUs corresponding to four bifidobacterial species were 
identified in more than half of the analyzed samples, corresponding to B. longum subsp. longum, 
Bifidobacterium adolescentis, B. breve and B. bifidum (Fig. 1). Focusing on the infant fecal 
samples, we also observed a bifidobacterial prevalence (>50 % of the analyzed samples) of these 
four above-mentioned species, while three additional species were identified in milk samples, i.e. B. 
pseudocatenulatum, B. longum subsp. infantis and B. catenulatum (Fig. 1). Intriguingly, we also 
observed species whose type strains have been isolated from non-human hosts, such as B. boum, B. 
thermophilum, B. tsurumiense and B. saguini, thus confirming previous observations that 
bifidobacterial species are widespread across the mammalian branch of the tree of life and that host-
specific adaptation may exist only at strain level (Milani et al., 2017a). Evaluation of the most 
abundant OTUs observed in the 197 data sets highlighted bacterial phylotypes, defined as strains of 
the same species that share the same ITS sequence, which are frequently vertically transmitted by 
mothers to their offspring. In this context, evaluation of phylotypes that were observed in mother’s 
feces and milk, as well as in infant feces of the 50 mother-infant pairs revealed that B. longum 
phylotype_1 and B. longum phylotype_2 were shown to exhibit the highest frequency of 
transmission within the examined data set (60.8 % and 40.8 %, respectively), followed by B. 
adolescentis phylotype_1 and B. adolescentis phylotype_2 (30.4 % and 26.4 %, respectively) and B. 
bifidum phylotype_1 (24 %) (Fig. 2). Furthermore, in cases where B. bifidum appeared to be 
vertically transmitted from mother to child, we observed that certain phylotypes elicited a high co-

Infant fecal samples were cross-compared with each other to unveil the occurrence of similar 
assembly/compositional profiles between newborns in terms of bifidobacterial species. Hierarchical 
clustering based on the relative abundance of bifidobacterial species evaluated by ITS profiling 
revealed the presence of four major clusters, demonstrating the existence of profiles that showed a 
similar composition in terms of bifidobacterial species, and here referred to as bifidotypes, being 
further subtyped based on the most abundant species (Fig. 2). Among the analyzed infant fecal 
samples, seven were included in the B. bifidum bifidotype (cluster A), 22 in the B. breve bifidotype 
(cluster B), 29 in the B. longum bifidotype (cluster C) and 15 in the B. adolescentis bifidotype 
(cluster D) (Fig. 2). Interestingly, cluster A represented by B. bifidum exhibits a high abundance of 
B. longum subsp. infantis as well, indicating a bifidotype that is highly specialized in HMO 
degradation. Additionally, a sub-cluster (named B1) can be identified in cluster B, which in addition 
to the presence of B. bifidum contains a high number of other bifidobacterial species, and therefore 
represents the most complex bifidobacterial ecosystem characterized by simultaneous colonization 
by B. bifidum, B. breve and B. longum subsp. infantis (Fig. 2).

The collected ITS sequencing data from mothers and corresponding infants also allowed evaluation 
of co-occurrence or co-exclusion connections between bifidobacterial species profiled in the 
samples (Table S3). Focusing on milk and infant fecal samples, statistically significant (P-value < 
0.05) covariance data sets were used to construct force-driven networks of bifidobacterial species 
that possess a relative abundance higher than 5 % in at least one sample (Fig. 3). While significant 
co-exclusion values (P-value < 0.05) were identified between eight (milk samples) and nine (infant 
samples) bifidobacterial species, co-occurrence values were observed between 19 (infant samples) 
and 33 (milk samples) species reported in Figure 3 (Table S3). These correlations revealed the 
presence of four clusters of bifidobacterial species with a high degree of co-occurrence in both 
infant and milk samples, involving one to 16 species (Fig. 3). Interestingly, B. bifidum is part of a 
cluster composed of those bifidobacterial strains that are typically identified within the infant gut in 
both the milk-based and infant-based networks, highlighting a correlation between B. bifidum and 
other vertically transmitted species. In this context, the B. bifidum cluster of the milk-based network 
was composed of species able to degrade HMOs, such as B. longum subsp. infantis and 
Bifidobacterium crudilactis (Milani et al., 2015b), as well as commonly (vertically) transmitted 
species, such as B. breve and B. adolescentis, as mentioned above (Fig. 3). Furthermore, the B. 
bifidum cluster of the infant-based network displayed species included in the B. bifidum cluster of 
the milk-based network, i.e. B. longum subsp. infantis and B. breve. This information corroborates 
and extends previous findings about cross-feeding activities that occur between B. bifidum and B. 
breve, in which growth of the latter species depends on saccharides released by B. bifidum-mediated 
hydrolysis of complex carbohydrates (Egan et al., 2014b).

Cross-feeding activities between B. bifidum PRL2010 and other bifidobacterial strains. In 
order to expand our understanding on the saccharide-releasing behavior of B. bifidum and the 
impact of this on other bifidobacteria through cross-feeding opportunities, we exploited the model 
strain B. bifidum PRL2010, which represents a prototype strain of this species (Turroni et al., 2014; 
Turroni et al., 2015), to detail its biological role as supporter of colonization by co-occurring 
bifidobacterial taxa. In this context, cross-feeding behavior has been documented by studies 
focusing on a small number of bifidobacterial species under in vitro conditions (Egan et al., 2014b; 
Turroni et al., 2015; Turroni et al., 2016; Bottacini et al., 2017; Turroni et al., 2018). Nevertheless, 
cross-feeding of human-derived glycans has been shown only for the species B. bifidum and B. 
breve through batch cultivation on a limited number of substrates, i.e. mucin and 6' sialyllactose 
(Egan et al., 2014b; Egan et al., 2014a; Nishiyama et al., 2018). For this reason, in order to obtain a 
comprehensive and accurate overview of cross-feeding interactions between B. bifidum PRL2010 
and other co-occurring key members of the infant bifidobacterial population, we developed a 
bioreactor model using human milk as growth substrate. Notably, we recently reconstructed the 
infant gut bifidobacterial communities through the isolation of different bifidobacteria from human 
milk samples, such as B. bifidum, B. longum, B. adolescentis, B. catenulatum, B. 
pseudocatenulatum and B. breve taxa, predicted to be vertically transmitted from mothers to their 
children (Duranti et al., 2017b). Thus, we decided to evaluate PRL2010 with regards to its cross-
feeding properties towards a representative strain for each of these bifidobacterial species by 
monitoring their growth performance on human milk in a bioreactor. The growth capabilities of B. 
longum subsp. longum 1897B, B. adolescentis 1892B, B. catenulatum 1899B, B. 
pseudocatenulatum 1896B and B. breve 1895B cultivated on their own (mono-association) on

human milk as substrate were evaluated (Fig. 4) and compared to those achieved when B. bifidum 
PRL2010 was co-cultivated in pairs (bi-association) on the same medium. Interestingly, co-

To further corroborate the hypothesis of cross-feeding between B. bifidum PRL2010 and other 
bifidobacteria, and to clarify the role of lactose as a confounding factor, we performed an additional 
cultivation experiment involving the inoculation of B. longum subsp. longum 1897B, B. 
adolescentis 1892B, B. catenulatum 1899B, B. pseudocatenulatum 1896B or B. breve 1895B on 
‘spent’ and filtered human milk in which B. bifidum PRL2010 or L. rhamnosus DSM20021 had 
been grown for 24 hours in order to metabolize lactose and release HMO derivates, or to remove 
lactose, respectively (Fig. S1). Prior to the addition of bifidobacterial strains, spent human milk-
based growth medium was filter sterilized, pH was adjusted to 6.5 and the presence or absence of 
lactose from the pre-conditioned media was evaluated through a Lactose/Galactose Assay Kit. 
Interestingly, qPCR evaluation of B. longum B. longum subsp. longum 1897B, B. catenulatum 
1899B, B. pseudocatenulatum 1896B and B. breve 1895B abundances revealed that these taxa 
benefit from growth on human milk pre-conditioned by B. bifidum PRL2010 as compared to growth 
on human milk pre-conditioned with L. rhamnosus DSM 20021 (Fig. S1), indicating that HMO-
derived components are released by B. bifidum PRL2010 and act as cross-feeding substrates for 
other bifidobacteria. On the other hand, B. adolescentis 1892B was the only strain that did not

exhibit growth on this pre-conditioned milk (irrespective of prior growth by either B. bifidum 
PRL2010 or L. rhamnosus DSM 20021). This latter  observation confirms previous genomic data

Overall, co-cultivation of B. bifidum PRL2010 with various bifidobacterial strains on human milk 
showed an increase in cell numbers of one partner, thus confirming the cross-feeding abilities of 
bifidobacterial strains. Notably, validation of cross-feeding between vertically co-transmitted 
species, and the pre-conditioned milk experiment, provides a clear indication that these taxa 
specifically co-evolved to maximize their colonization capabilities in the infant gut through efficient 
energy harvesting and resource sharing.   
In this context, it is worth mentioning that cross-feeding may also co-exist between members of 
different genera that co-evolved within the same ecological niche (Medlock et al., 2018), or may 
even be exploited by (opportunistic) pathogens (Ng et al., 2013), thus its impact on the overall 
bacterial population is strictly dependent on gut microbiota homeostasis and eubiosis.

bifidobacterial population harbored by the infant gut through co-operative HMO degradation, we 
investigated transcriptomic profiles of strain PRL2010 obtained when it is grown on particular 
HMOs. Human milk contains a mix of approximately 200 unique HMOs and its composition may 
vary considerably between mothers and lactation stage, ranging between 23 to 130 different glycans

substrates, we unveiled the transcriptome, as determined by RNAseq analysis, of B. bifidum 
PRL2010 cultivated in the presence of these carbohydrates as unique carbon sources and compared 
these results to the transcriptomes of PRL2010 grown on glucose. Interestingly, growth of PRL2010 
on each of these nine substrates induced overexpression (more than 2-fold induction) of an average 
of 111 genes, ranging from 56 in 3-FL to 198 in sialic acid (Fig. 5). The upregulated genes were 
classified using Clusters of Orthologous Group (COG) analysis in order to identify differentially 
transcribed genes that may contribute to specific biological functions (Fig. 5). As illustrated in 
Figure 5, carbohydrate metabolism, corresponding to COG category G, is the COG function of 
PRL2010 most significantly affected by the presence of HMOs or its glycan constituents, followed 
by COGs related to cellular processes and signaling.

Conclusions 
This study provides data that support the notion of B. bifidum ability to colonize the newborn 
through maternal transfer. By analyzing fecal samples of 50 mothers and their corresponding 
babies, as well as their associated breast milk samples, we revealed the presence of four 
bifidobacterial compositional clusters within infants defined as bifidotypes, represented by specific, 
commonly co-occurring players, i.e. B. bifidum, B. breve, B. longum or B. adolescentis. In this 
context, B. bifidum was identified in each milk sample suggesting that milk represents a key vector 
for vertical transmission of bifidobacteria. 
The ability of B. bifidum PRL2010 to metabolize HMOs or HMO constituents was verified by 
means of growth and RNAseq experiments. Furthermore, the glycan cross-feeding activities 
between B. bifidum PRL2010 and other bifidobacterial species typically found in the newborn gut 
were demonstrated by means of cultivation in mono- and bi-associations in media containing 
complex carbohydrates present in human milk as carbon sources. These findings highlight the 
ability of B. longum subsp. longum 1897B, B. adolescentis 1892B, B. catenulatum 1899B, B. 
pseudocatenulatum 1896B and B. breve 1895B to cross-feed on sugars released by the HMO-

terms of the development of specific bifidobacterial communities during this crucial developmental

Experimental procedures 
Subject recruitment and sample collection. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics 
Committee of the “Azienda Unità Sanitaria Locale di Reggio Emilia - IRCCS” in Reggio Emilia, 
Italy, as well as by the Ethics Committee of the University of Parma, Italy, and informed written 
consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. Mother and infant pairs enrolled 
in this study were not taking any probiotics or antibiotics, and reported to be healthy. The age of the 
mothers ranged from 18 to 40 years and newborns were delivered by natural childbirth. Stool 
samples of mothers and infants were collected in the first month following the birth of their child 
(Table S1). At the same time, 10 ml of breast milk sample was collected from each mother in sterile 
tubes (Table S1). Stool and milk samples were collected following the “Stool Packaging 
Instructions” of the “Core Microbiome Sampling Protocol A” reported in the “Manual of

Illumina MiSeq sequencer with MiSeq Reagent kit v3 chemicals. The generated results were 
compared with the ITS profiling findings previously described by Milani et al. (Milani et al., 
2015a). A mock community of 13 bifidobacterial species was included in the MiSeq sequencing run 
as positive control. Notably, the obtained profile qualitatively and quantitatively was shown to 
correspond to the expected microbial composition, thus confirming that the bifidobacterial ITS 
profiling protocol did not introduce biases that may reduce the discriminatory power of the analysis 
(Table S2). 
Growth conditions of bifidobacterial strains. Bifidobacterial cells were grown at 37 °C under 
anaerobic conditions (2.99 % H2, 17.01 % CO2 and 80 % N2) in a chamber (Concept 400; Ruskin) 
in De Man-Rogosa-Sharpe (MRS) broth (Sharlau Chemie, Barcelona, Spain) supplemented with 
0.05 % (wt/vol) L-cysteine hydrochloride.  
Fermentation profiles on specific HMO components. B. bifidum PRL2010 cells were grown 
overnight from stock in MRS broth as previously described. Subsequently, viable cells was 
inoculated in 6 ml of freshly prepared modified MRS (mMRS) medium supplemented with 1 % of a 
particular carbohydrate as described by Duranti et al. (Duranti et al., 2017b), in order to adapt the 
strain to grow in specific carbon source. Furthermore, 50 µl of such a stationary phase culture were

inoculated in 10 ml of mMRS with each carbon source. After inoculum, growth was monitored and 
at an OD600nm between 0.6 and 0.8 (exponential phase) cells were harvested by centrifugation at

acid, 3’-sialyllactose, 6’-sialyllactose, 2’-fucosyllactose, 3’-fucosyllactose, lacto-N-tetraose or

RNA concentration and purity was evaluated by a Picodrop microliter spectrophotometer (Picodrop, 
UK). 
RNAseq analysis performed by NextSeq Illumina. For RNA sequencing, 2 μg of total RNA was 
treated to remove ribosomal RNA by the Ribo-Zero Magnetic Kit (Illumina), followed by 
purification of the rRNA-depleted sample by ethanol precipitation. RNA was processed according 
to the manufacturer’s protocol (Illumina). The yield of rRNA depletion was checked by assessment 
using a Tape station 2200 (Agilent Technologies). Following this, 500 ng of an rRNA-depleted 
RNA sample was fragmented using a Bioruptor NGS ultrasonicator (Diagenode, USA) and size 
evaluated using a Tape station 2200 (Agilent Technologies). A whole transcriptome library was 
constructed using the TruSeq Stranded RNA LT Kit (Illumina). Samples were loaded into a Flow 
cell V2 75 cycles (Illumina) according to the technical support guide. Demultiplexed reads were 
quality filtered (with overall quality, quality window and length filters) and aligned to the B.
