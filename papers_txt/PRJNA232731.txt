Abstract 
22	  
Despite the large interest in the human microbiome in recent years, there are no reports of bacterial 
23	  
DNA methylation in the microbiome.  Here metagenomic sequencing using the Pacific Biosciences 
24	  
platform allowed for rapid identification of bacterial GATC methylation status of a bacterial species  
25	  
in human stool samples.  For this work, two stool samples were chosen that were dominated by a 
26	  
single species, Bacteroides dorei.  Based on 16S rRNA analysis, this species represented over 45% of 
27	  
the bacteria present in these two samples. The B. dorei genome sequence from these samples was 
28	  
determined and the GATC methylation sites mapped.  The Bacteroides dorei genome from one 
29	  
subject lacked any GATC methylation and lacked the DNA adenine methyltransferase genes.  In 
30	  
contrast, B. dorei from another subject contained 20,551 methylated GATC sites. Of the 4,970 open 
31	  
reading frames identified in the GATC methylated B. dorei genome, 3,184 genes were methylated as 
32	  
well as 1,735 GATC methylations in intergenic regions.  These results suggest that DNA methylation 
33	  
patterns are important to consider in multi-omic analyses of microbiome samples seeking to discover 
34	  
the diversity of bacterial functions and may differ between disease states.   
35	  
 
36	  
1. 
Introduction 
37	  
DNA adenosine methylation (Dam methylation) of the 5'-GATC-3' motif in bacterial genomes, 
38	  
particularly in Escherichia coli, has been well characterized for decades (Barras and Marinus, 1989; 
39	  
Marinus, 1987; Marinus and Casadeus, 2009).   Of all of the DNA methylation systems known in 
40

bacteria, GATC methylation appears to have the highest impact on gene expression (Barras and 
41	  
Marinus, 1989).  The biochemistry of the adenosine methylation reaction catalyzed by bacterial DNA 
42	  
adenine methyltransferase (DamMT) is also well characterized (Thielking et al., 1997; Urig et al., 
43	  
2002).  As DamMT mutants are not lethal, the phenotype of DamMT mutants in several bacteria 
44	  
have shown that methylation of the GATC motif affects gene expression of many processes including 
45	  
chromosome replication, mismatch repair, and nucleoid structure (Løbner-Olesen et al., 2005).  The 
46	  
GATC methylation system has been shown to increase virulence in Salmonella enterica serovar 
47	  
Typhimurium, Vibrio cholerae, Yersinia pestis, and Yersinia pseudotuberculosis, (Heithoff et al., 
48	  
1999; Robinson et al., 2005; Julio et al., 2001; Julio et al., 2002).  As a result, significant efforts have 
49	  
been made in recent years to design antibiotics that inhibit DamMT (Hobley et al., 2012; Mashhoon 
50	  
et al., 2004; Mashhoon, 2006; McKelvie et al., 2012).  
51	  
 
Despite the enormous interest in recent years to characterize the bacterial diversity of the 
52	  
human microbiome, particularly as it relates to gut diseases, the role that DNA methylation plays in 
53	  
function of the microbiome remains unknown.  The PacBio RS II system is capable of detecting 
54	  
DNA methylation through analysis of polymerase kinetics (Clark et al., 2012; Fang et al., 2012; 
55	  
Flusberg et al., 2010; Murray et al., 2012).  This technology was used here to discover the profound 
56	  
differences in the extent of Dam methylation in one dominant bacterial species in the gut between 
57	  
two children at high genetic risk for type 1 diabetes and one year prior to the development of type 1 
58	  
diabetes autoimmunity in one of these children.   
59	  
 
60	  
2. 
Materials and methods 
61	  
	  
62	  
The stool samples used here were collected by the Finnish Type 1 Diabetes Prediction and 
63	  
Prevention Study (DIPP) (Kukko et al., 2005).  Newborns were screened for high-risk HLA-DR and 
64	  
HLA-DQ genotypes using a previously described method (Kukko et al., 2005). Stool samples were 
65	  
collected by the subjects' parents at home and mailed to the DIPP Virus Laboratory for virology in 
66	  
Tampere, Finland, where they were stored at -80 °C.  Detection of beta-cell autoimmunity was done 
67	  
as described in (Parikka et al., 2012).  Sample 105 was collected from the subject at 13.5 months of 
68	  
age.  This subject became autoimmune for type 1 diabetes at 15.1 months of age.   Sample 439 was 
69	  
collected from a subject who remained healthy at 3.3 months of age.  Both subjects were genetically 
70	  
at high risk for type 1 diabetes given their HLA genotype.   
71	  
 
In this study, DNA extraction and 16S rRNA amplification, sequencing, and analysis was 
72	  
done as described previously (Fagen et al., 2012) except the Qiagen AllPrep DNA/RNA/Protein Mini 
73	  
Kit (QIAGEN) was used to extract DNA, RNA, and protein from stool. Based on the 16S rRNA 
74	  
results, two samples were chosen for long-read Pacific Biosciences sequencing based on the high 
75	  
relative abundance of this organism was 63.7% and 47.9% from samples 105 and 439, respectively.  
76	  
These samples, 105 and 439, were collected from children who became autoimmune or remained 
77	  
healthy, respectively.   
78	  
	  
Pacific Biosciences (PacBio RS II system) library construction and sequencing was done by 
79	  
the University of Florida's Interdisciplinary Center for Biotechnology Research.  Prior to sequencing, 
80	  
a PacBio library was made with SMRTbell Adaptors. The Bacteroides dorei genome was assembled 
81	  
to closure from sample 105 after obtaining eight SMRT cells of sequence data. A total of 1,502,920 
82	  
reads and 1,860,712,096 bases were obtained, with a mean read length of 2,706 bp. Average read

93	  
 
The complete B. dorei genome from sample 439 metageomic DNA was closed in the same 
94	  
manner as described above for the 105 sample.  The 439 closed genome was significantly smaller 
95	  
than the 105 genome with 5,243,219 bp.  Genome annotation for both genomes was done by using 
96	  
the NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation pipeline (Angiuoli et al., 2008), which relies on 
97	  
GeneMarkS+ for gene prediction (Besemer et al., 2001). The NCBI accession numbers for the closed 
98	  
105 B. dorei genome and the 439 B. dorei genome are CP007619 and CP008741, respectively.  Two-
99	  
way average nucleotide identities between pairs of genomes were determined as described by Goris 
100	  
et al. (2007).   
101	  
 
Methylation patterns obtained from the PacBio data were recovered from both genomes using 
102	  
the assembled genomes as a reference. Methylation data was extracted from the full set of sequence 
103	  
data using Kinetics Tools. This tool utilizes the P_ModificationDetection module in SMRT Portal, 
104	  
which is utilized by the RS Modification Detection as well as the RS Modifications and Motif 
105	  
Detection protocol. The Motif Detection protocol generated motifs by comparing methylation 
106	  
patterns to genomic context. 
107	  
 
108	  
3. 
Results 
109	  
PacBio sequencing and assembly from 105 and 439 metagenomic DNA resulted in two closed 
110	  
Bacteroides dorei genomes of 5,726,633 and 5,243,219 bp, respectively,  The 105 and 439 genomes 
111	  
assembled to an average coverage across each genome of 306.72- and 249.47-fold, respectively.  The 
112	  
16S rRNA and whole genome average nucleotide identities were compared between the 105 and 439 
113	  
genomes as well as two reference genomes, the nearly complete genome of B. dorei DSM 17855 and 
114	  
the complete genome of B. vulgatus 8482 (Tables 1 and 2).  These data show that the 105 and 439 
115	  
genomes are far more closely related to B. dorei than B. vulgatus.    
116	  
 
Methylated GATC motifs were only found in the B. dorei from the 105 sample and were 
117	  
completely absent in the B. dorei contigs obtained for sample 439 (Table 1).  There were 49,007 total 
118	  
methylations detected in B. dorei 105, and 38,203 total methylations detected in B. dorei 439. Of 
119	  
these, 14,322 and 24,770 methylation sites for the 105 and 439 genomes, respectively, could not be 
120	  
clustered to any motif.  In the B. dorei genome from the 105 sample, 20,551 GATC methylations and 
121	  
one copy of the DamMT gene were identified.   Of 20,554 GATC sites in the 105 genome, only 3 
122	  
were not detected as methylated.  In contrast, the B. dorei genome of 5,243,219 bp from sample 439 
123	  
lacked a DamMT gene and no GATC motif methylations were detected among the 18,908 GATC 
124	  
sites identified.

A total of 5,107,008 bp or 89% of the B. dorei 105 genome codes directly for ORFs.  Of the 
126	  
20,551 methylated GATC sties found in B. dorei 105, 18,816 were found within 3,184 of the 4,970 
127	  
annotated open reading frames.  Of the 1,735 methylated GATC sites identified between ORFs many 
128	  
are likely within promoter regions.  Thus, there are 2.80 GATC methylation sites per kbp of non-
129	  
coding genome space which is slightly lower than the 3.68 GATC methylation sites per kbp of the 
130	  
coding region.   This suggests that the evolutionary pressure to maintain GATC sites is stronger in 
131	  
coding regions compared to non-coding regions.   
132	  
 
Of the 245 SEED subsystems identified by RAST annotation of the complete B. dorei 105 
133	  
genome, only 13 subsystems had no GATC methylation sites (Table S1).  Those include purine 
134	  
utilization, carbon starvation, copper homeostasis, gentisate degradation, salicylate and gentisate 
135	  
catabolism.   
136	  
 
The remaining 232 subsystems were methylated at nearly every GATC site.  The Ton and Tol 
137	  
transport systems which provide energy for transport across the outer membrane in Gram negative 
138	  
bacteria was the most highly GATC methylated subsystem, accounting for 6.7% of all GATC 
139	  
methylations and 5.5% of the genome. Transport plays a crucial role in the heterotrophic bacteria that 
140	  
reside in the gut.   
141	  
 
Other motifs in both genomes were detected in this analysis as well (Table 1).  About 8.6% 
142	  
and 35.2% of all of the motifs detected in the 105 and 439 genomes, respectively, cannot be 
143	  
characterized as to the position of modification on the adenosine ring.  The remaining motifs were all 
144	  
methylations of the 6-position of the adenosine ring and less abundant than the GATC modification.  
145	  
Excluding the GATC motif, there are 15,991 motifs remaining in the 105 genome, of which 14,134 
146	  
(88.4%) are modified. In the 439 genome, there are 22,969 motifs detected, however, only 13,433 
147	  
(58.5%) are modified. Thus, even non-GATC adenosine methylation sites are much more likely to be 
148	  
methylated in the 105 genome compared to the 439 genome.  The phenotypic effects of these 
149	  
methylations are unknown.    
150	  
 
 
151	  
 
As the primary objective of this work was to obtain closed B. dorei genomes and examine 
152	  
GATC methylations, the libraries were not prepared in a way that would allow detection of 5-
153	  
methylcytosine modifications.  Both the 105 and 439 genomes each have genes that code for putative 
154	  
DNA cytosine methyltransferases.  Hence, we predict that 5'-cytosine methylation is normal in both 
155	  
the 105 and 439 samples.  
156	  
 
157	  
 
This is in contrast to the Dam methyltransferase gene, which is only present in the 105 
158	  
genome.  In the 105 genome, a single Dam methyltransferase gene was annotated that is predicted to 
159	  
recognize the GATC motif (Figure 1).  This gene is within a bacterial prophage region that is 47,617 
160	  
bp in length (between bases 1,014,516 and 1,062,132 bp in the genome), making it similar to other 
161	  
bacteriophage orphan DNA methyltransferases found in bacteria (Murphy et al., 2013).   
162	  
 
 
 
 
163	  
 
Another interesting observation is that of all of the methylation motifs observed in these two 
164	  
genomes, none is methylated in both genomes. This suggests that the primary source of 
165	  
methyltransferases in these genomes is through lateral transfer, often from phage.  Nevertheless, the 
166	  
GATC motif methylation and abundance is the primary difference between the two genomes.

4. 
Discussion 
170	  
Given that nearly all subsystems can be virtually completely methylated in one strain of a dominant 
171	  
gut bacterium and completely unmethylated in another strain of the same species from a different 
172	  
subject, suggests that epigenetics may be a crucial process regulating the metabolic potential of 
173	  
heterotrophic bacteria in the human gut.   The observation that similar species reside in the gut of two 
174	  
individuals is not sufficient to propose that the metabolic capabilities of the two microbiomes are 
175	  
similar.  This work suggests that future microbiome studies should consider the methylome when 
176	  
describing the bacterial diversity in the gut.  Such analyses are no longer difficult given the latest 
177	  
sequencing technologies.   
178	  
 
Although methylation can lead to up-regulation or down-regulation of a gene (Marinus and 
179	  
Casadesus, 2009), it is impossible to say whether the GATC motif affects the expression of B. dorei 
180	  
genes in the gut.  However, the sheer number of GATC methylations found in one of these genomes 
181	  
suggests that GATC methylation is worthy of analysis in future investigations.  It is likely that gene 
182	  
expression varies widely between a strain that is fully GATC methylated versus another that is not.  
183	  
Unfortunately, unlike 16S rRNA, DamMT genes are not sufficiently conserved at the nucleotide 
184	  
level to allow rapid PCR analyses of this gene in metagenomic samples.      
185	  
 
The effect of Dam methylation on gut colonization and survival may be significant.  For 
186	  
example, Dam methylation of various transport systems affects many processes including import of 
187	  
nutrients, energy transfer, and antibiotic resistance conferred by antibiotic efflux.  All of these are 
188	  
important to gut colonization and survival.  A Dam mutant of Haemophilus influenzae is more 
189	  
susceptible to many antibiotics including erythromycin, tetracycline, kanamycin, and spectinomycin 
190	  
(Sanchez et al., 1997).  In most cases, the H. influenzae Dam mutant showed not only a lower 
191	  
minimum inhibitory concentration, but also increased susceptibility to higher concentrations of 
192	  
antibiotics (Zaleski and Piekarowicz, 2004) suggesting that Dam methylation has a role in 
193	  
upregulating expression of efflux pumps and ultimately antibiotic resistance.     
194	  
 
GATC methylation has also been implicated in pathogenesis by bacteria.  DamMT mutants in 
195	  
several species have shown attenuated pathogenesis (Balbontin et al., 2006; Heithoff et al., 1999; 
196	  
Julio et al., 2001; Julio et al., 2002; Kim et al., 2008; Mehling et al., 2007; Murphy et al., 2008; 
197	  
Robinson et al., 2005; Watson et al., 2004) and as GATC methylation is involved in many 
198	  
fundamental bacterial processes including, but not limited to, replication, DNA repair, transcription, 
199	  
and LPS composition (Marinus and Casadeus, 2009), it is not surprising that DamMT deficient 
200	  
strains would be impaired in their ability to interact with eukaryotic hosts.  Of course, given that have 
201	  
only two samples were examined here, no estimate of the effects of gut bacterial GATC methylation 
202	  
on type 1 diabetes autoimmunity outcome can be made.  
203	  
 
GATC methylation in the B. dorei 105 genome appears to be the result of a single orphan 
204	  
DNA adenine methyltransferase gene embedded within a bacterial prophage of unknown origin.  
205	  
These orphan DamMTases have been reported to protect phage DNA that include the GATC motif 
206	  
from restriction (Kossykh et al., 1995; Miller et al., 2003).  That may be the case here as the 
207	  
frequency of GATC motifs within the DamMT-containing bacteriophage region is 1.7 times higher 
208	  
than in the 105 genome overall with 6.09 and 3.59 GATC motifs per kb in the prophage and whole 
209	  
genome, respectively.   
210	  
 
These orphan DamMT-containing bacteriophage can also regulate lysogeny by the 
211	  
bacteriophage (Murphy et al., 2008).  Until this strain is cultured and a mutant is made that lacks

213	  
 
How GATC methylation affects microbiome function is unknown.  But given the many 
214	  
bacterial processes affected by GATC methylation, environmental shaping of bacterial genomes 
215	  
through environmental exposures may influence the role of the microbiome in gut diseases.  The 
216	  
surprising aspect of this work is that a single dominant species in the gut of these two subjects, B. 
217	  
dorei, can vary so radically in methylation patterns simply because of the absence of the DamMT 
218	  
gene in B. dorei in one of these subjects.  Current and future metagenomic datasets from the gut 
219	  
microbiome should be mined for DamMT genes and GATC methylation sites to determine their 
220	  
abundance and taxonomic breadth.
