Background: We set out to explore associations between the stool bacteriome proﬁles and
early-onset islet autoimmunity, taking into account the interactions with the virus component
of the microbiome.
Methods: Serial stool samples were longitudinally collected from 18 infants and toddlers with
early-onset islet autoimmunity (median age 17.4 months) followed by type 1 diabetes, and
18 tightly matched controls from the Finnish Diabetes Prediction and Prevention (DIPP) cohort.
Three stool samples were analyzed, taken 3, 6, and 9 months before the ﬁrst detection of
serum autoantibodies in the case child. The risk of islet autoimmunity was evaluated in relation
to the composition of the bacteriome 16S rDNA proﬁles assessed by mass sequencing, and to
the composition of DNA and RNA viromes.
Results: Four operational taxonomic units were signiﬁcantly less abundant in children who later
on developed islet autoimmunity as compared to controls—most markedly the species of Bac-
teroides vulgatus and Biﬁdobacterium biﬁdum. The alpha or beta diversity, or the taxonomic
levels of bacterial phyla, classes or genera, showed no differences between cases and controls.
A correlation analysis suggested a possible relation between CrAssphage signals and quantities
of Bacteroides dorei. No apparent associations were seen between development of islet autoim-
munity and sequences of yet unknown origin.
Conclusions: The results conﬁrm previous ﬁndings that an imbalance within the prevalent Bac-
teroides genus is associated with islet autoimmunity. The detected quantitative relation of the
novel “orphan” bacteriophage CrAssphage with a prevalent species of the Bacteroides genus
may exemplify possible modiﬁers of the bacteriome.

INTRODUCTION
The environmental triggers of type 1 diabetes (T1D) are still
unknown. The composition of the gut microbiome is one of the
candidates, primarily because of the metabolic and immune func-
tions of the bacterial mass. However, recent reviews1,2 pointed
out that until now, despite the growing body of knowledge, no
single organism or a well-delineated group of microorganisms has
been associated with islet autoimmunity in humans by 2 or more
independent works.
The ﬁrst studies on the human intestinal microbiome and T1D or
islet autoimmunity were published in 2011, with 4 cases and 4 con-
trols.3,4 Further 2 important studies were published in 2013, larger in
size, but with methods prone to considerable biases.5,6 Finally, the
last 2 years produced a number of new studies on the gut bacter-
iome, with no clear overlap of the results, perhaps with the exception
of changes in the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes. Changes in rel-
ative Bacteroidetes quantities, or in ratios within the phylum, were
observed in a study from the Finnish Type 1 Diabetes Prediction and
Prevention (DIPP) and VirDiab cohorts,7 from the Mexican State of
Sonora,8 and from a large sample set collected from 29 islet autoanti-
body positive subjects and 47 controls recruited in the Turku area of
Finland.9 However, Bacteroidetes is a very prevalent phylum, so some
of the observations may reﬂect changes in the diversity of the micro-
biome rather than a pathogenic involvement of the phylum itself;
moreover, the present methods of 16S proﬁling tend to aggregate
some of the closely related Bacteroides species, rather than dissect
their complex subpopulations.
None of the studies listed above investigated the virome (the
viral component of the microbiome) in parallel to the bacteriome. As
most of the human gut virome comprises phages, the virome proba-
bly bi-directionally interacts with the bacteriome proﬁles. Therefore,
investigating the quantities of known phages may in theory reveal
important associations with islet autoimmunity. The same is true
about the analysis of candidate sequences of presumably viral origin
but no clear matches found in existing virus sequence databases
(called “unknown viruses,” “uncultured metagenomics,” “dark matter
of the virome”10). Such sequences have been largely ignored in asso-
ciation studies despite their high share in human viromes.11 It is this
“dark matter” that has yielded the so far the most prevalent bacterio-
phage of the human gut, the CrAsssphage.12
The aim of the present study was to (1) assess differences in the
diversity and composition of the fecal bacteriome between children
with early-onset islet autoimmunity and tightly matched control chil-
dren within the prospective birth cohort study in Finland (DIPP
study), (2) evaluate the virus component of the microbiome in parallel
with the bacteriome proﬁles, and ﬁnally (3) explore the unassigned
“dark matter” sequences of the stool virome component.

MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1
| Subjects, case deﬁnition, and matching of
controls
We designed a case-control study nested within the DIPP birth cohort.
The DIPP cohort recruits families with a newborn infant carrying
deﬁned human leukocyte antigens (HLA) genotypes conferring suscep-
tibility to T1D. These children are prospectively followed from birth
and serum samples are regularly collected and analyzed for T1D-
related autoantibodies. Stool samples were collected every month from
3 up to 36 months of age. The DIPP study protocol has been described
in detail previously13 and has been approved by the ethical committees
of the participating University Hospitals in Oulu, Tampere, and Turku.
A written consent was obtained from all participating families.
The case series of the current study included 18 children
(11

persistently

T1D-associated autoantibodies at an early age. The mean age at the
detection of the ﬁrst islet autoantibodies was 15.2 months: 1, 7, and
10 cases turned autoantibody positive at visits scheduled at the age
of 9, 12, and 18 months, respectively. All case children progressed
eventually to T1D after a median duration of 3.2 years after autoanti-
body seroconversion (interquartile range [IQR] 1.4-4.4 years).
One non-diabetic autoantibody-negative control child from the
DIPP cohort was matched to each case child for the date and place
of birth (median absolute difference in birth dates 4.5 days, IQR 2 to
18 days), T1D risk level deﬁned by the HLA-DQB1 genotyping, and
gender. The study subjects were born in the regions of Tampere Uni-
versity Hospital and Oulu University Hospital, that is, from 2 of the
3 centers recruiting for the DIPP study.
2.2
| Stool samples
Samples for this study were collected through the years 1998 to
2007 (Table 1). Three pairs of stool samples preceding the time point
of islet autoimmunity of the case were intended for analysis in every
case-control matching pair. The samples were collected at home,
mailed overnight and then stored at −70C until analysis. Samples
were taken 3, 6, and 9 months before the date of the serum sample
where islet autoantibodies were ﬁrst detected, and corresponding
time points in matched control children. Some of the case-control
pairs contributed only with 1 or 2 pairs of samples, either because of
a very early initiation of islet autoimmunity, or because they lacked
part of the follow-up stool samples. The total number of samples was
92 (18, 15, and 13 samples at the time points 3, 6, and 9 months
before islet autoimmunity, respectively). The age at the collection of
the stool samples ranged from 2.9 to 17.3 months. This set of cases
and controls and their samples is almost identical to the set investi-
gated for the virome composition in our previous study,14 with the

2.3
| Virome sequencing
The methods of virome testing have been described in detail previ-
ously14: viruses were concentrated from stool supernatant, DNA and
RNA virome sequenced, and data deposited at Sequence Read
Archive (NCBI) under accession id PRJNA275568.
2.4
| Bacteriome proﬁling
The total DNA for amplicon sequencing was extracted from 30 to
60 mg of frozen unprocessed stool sample using the PowerSoil DNA
isolation kit (MoBio, Carlsbad, California). Negative controls (PCR
[polymerase chain reaction]-grade water) were added into each
extraction batch.
The microbiome composition was assessed by mass sequencing
of the variable region 4 of the 16S bacterial ribosomal gene.15 Sam-
ples were sequenced in duplicates differing in primer index combina-
tions. Ampliﬁcation used the AccuPrime Pfx SuperMix (Invitrogen,
Waltham, Massachusetts) with the following thermal protocol: initial
denaturation at 95C for 5 minutes, followed by 30 cycles of dena-
turation, 95C, 15 seconds, primer annealing, 55C, 30 seconds and
extension, 68C, 1 minute. Products were checked by gel electropho-
resis, diluted in water and puriﬁed with Ampure magnetic beads
(Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, California). Products were equalized
according to the results from quantiﬁcation using KAPA Library quan-
tiﬁcation kit (Kapa Biosystems, Wilmington, Massachusetts). Mock
community, a mix of bacterial DNA of a known content, was pro-
cessed and sequenced as a control along with the samples. Sequen-
cing was performed on a MiSeq machine (Illumina, San Diego,
California) with a 2 × 250 bp version 2 sequencing kit. The sequen-
cing data have been deposited at Sequence Read Archive (NCBI)
under project id PRJNA311147.
2.5
| Bioinformatic analysis of the virome
The metagenomic data from the virome sequencing were analyzed as
described previously using de novo assembly followed by querying
the contigs and singleton reads against a pan-virus database derived
from the NCBI repository.14 Because the protocol used reverse tran-
scriptase, which acts also as a DNA-dependent DNA polymerase, the
data included both DNA and RNA viromes. The resulting reference
sequences were used as scaffolds for re-mapping of the original
metagenomic reads.
Sequences unassigned to any known virus were then ﬁltered to
remove bacterial ribosomal DNA, and de novo assembled contigs
longer than 2000 bases with mean sequencing coverage higher than
100× were subjected to re-mapping from original ﬁles in order to
quantify their presence in individual samples. Their abundance was
then compared between cases and controls using conditional logistic
regression with case-control status as a dependent variable.

2.6
| Bioinformatic analysis of the bacteriome
Paired reads were merged, trimmed and ﬁltered for quality in the
Mothur suite, version 1.25.0,16 and then processed in the Qiime suite,
version
1.9.1.17
Chimeras
were
removed
by
usearch,
version
5.2.236.18 Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were constructed
based on similarity of 97% or more, and representatives of each OTU
were selected by choosing the most abundant one among the OTU.
Sequences were identiﬁed and taxonomically classiﬁed using the Silva
database, version 108.19
The resulting data were then analyzed in the R environment as a
phyloseq object.20 The high degree of identity between duplicate
sequencing of each sample was veriﬁed using principal component
analysis, and duplicates were then merged. The alpha diversity mea-
sures were determined by counting observed taxa (OTU richness),
Chao1, ACE, Shannon, Simpson, and Fisher indices; diversity was
compared between cases and controls. The beta diversity was
assessed by inspecting results of principle component analysis using
multiple distance methods (dissimilarity indices): Bray-Curtis, DPCoA,
Gower, Jaccard, Jensen-Shannon divergence, Mountford, simpliﬁed
Morisita’s index, Sørensen, unweighted and weighted UniFrac. No
formal testing of clustering was performed, as the samples apparently
did not form any clusters in either of the plots.
After ﬁltering (OTUs were retained only if they were supported
by more than 5 reads in at least 1 sample of the 92), we compared
relative abundances of taxonomic units at the level of phylum, class,
genus, and ﬁnally of OTU between cases and controls: if a signiﬁcant
distortion was found, comparisons were made also in the 3 individual
time points (3, 6, and 9 months before islet autoimmunity). No rare-
faction was employed in the analytic process, as rarefaction has been
advocated against by recent literature21; instead the differential
abundance was assessed using the DESeq2 tool22 embedded through
the phyloseq-to-deseq tool within the Phyloseq package.
Multiple models were ﬁtted—we used case-control status as the
predictor ﬁrst in a model adjusted for the matching group only, but to
compensate for the dynamic development of the infant gut micro-
biome, in further models we also added the time point (serial sample
pair number) using the DESeq2 package. The negative binomial gen-
eralized linear models were used for detecting the differential abun-
dance between the groups. The P values were corrected for multiple
testing using Benjamini-Hochberg method implemented within the
DESeq2 package, and P < .05 was considered signiﬁcant. The found
associations were then further veriﬁed in models adjusted for the
mode of delivery, and for breast-feeding. Signiﬁcant OTUs were
inspected for relative abundance in strata of samples taken at 3, 6,
and 9 months before islet autoimmunity, and for the consistence of
effect across these strata—signiﬁcance signals that lacked consist-
ency, or were supported by less than an average of 100 reads, were
removed. Only for the purposes of graphic outputs, the abundances
were transformed to a relative scale normalized to 10 000 reads.
A higher discrimination level than 97% similarity was used to
make a clear distinction between closely related species: this was the
case especially for Bacteroides dorei vs Bacteroides vulgatus whose ref-
erence sequences consistently differ only by 1 nucleotide within the
253 bases of the studied V4 amplicon of the 16S rDNA gene. To
detect this difference, the full sequencing resolution was used instead
of binning by similarity.
The signiﬁcantly associated OTUs were then put into the context
of other closely related OTUs from this study, along with related ref-
erence strains. By using phylogenetic analysis, we selected the OTUs
most closely related to the ones associated with islet autoimmunity,
and plotted them into a phylogenetic tree created from a MUSCLE
alignment23:
the
evolutionary
history
was
inferred
using
the
neighbor-joining method with evolutionary distances estimated by
Maximum Composite Likelihood analysis with 1000 bootstrapping
replicates. Analyses were performed in MEGA6 software.24 Unrelated
species were used as outgroups of the trees.
Finally, we examined the 16S proﬁles for presence of individual
pathogenic bacteria belonging to priority categories deﬁned by the
National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (NIAID).25
2.7
| Real-time assays
As 16S proﬁles are inherently relative, we wanted to analyze also
absolute concentrations of selected bacteria. Two approaches were
utilized—ﬁrst, we quantiﬁed total 16S rDNA load in the sample using
the mock community as a quantitative standard (BEI Resources, see
above). The absolute quantities of organisms were then calculated as
products of the total concentration of bacterial 16S rDNA, and the
share of the taxon among the 16S proﬁle.
Second, the 2 related Bacteroides species of interest were quanti-
ﬁed in separate PCR tests. To avoid the 16S rDNA gene as a PCR tar-
get, we computationally identiﬁed gene sequences that are present in
all publicly available sequenced strains belonging to the Bacteroides
species of interest, but in none of other Bacteroidetes nor in any of
40 other selected bacterial genomes across the taxonomic tree (for
primers, PCR conditions, and for the correlation to results from 16S
proﬁling see Figure S5, Supporting Information).
2.8
| Analysis of relation of bacteria to the phages
We constructed correlation networks visualizing the abundance data
of bacteriophages (sorted by their presumed host) vs the abundance
of the most abundant bacteria. For the sake of clarity, all quantities
were normalized to 10 000 reads. An interactive on-line tool for cus-
tom ﬁltering and visualization of the data, programmed by us using
Cytoscape26 and open source JavaScript libraries jQuery (https://
jquery.com) and DataTables (https://www.datatables.net), is available
at http://compbio.uta.ﬁ/phagenet.

RESULTS
3.1
| Bacteriome diversity was not associated with
islet autoimmunity
The measures of alpha diversity (ie, within-sample diversity) of sam-
ples from cases with islet autoimmunity did not differ from those in
matched control subjects (Figure S1A), all raw P > .30. This applied to
the whole dataset as well as to the last analyzed sample taken
3 months before the islet autoimmunity. The diversity steadily
4

increased with age (Figure S1B), without appreciable differences in
the pace of this increase between cases and controls. The plots of
beta diversity (distance) measures, and by timing of the sample rela-
tive to islet immunity (9, 6, vs 3 months before) did not reveal clus-
tering by case-control status, regardless of the distance method used
(Figure S1C).
3.2
| Taxonomic composition
Having tested diversity, we removed extremely rare OTUs that had
no samples with more than 5 reads. This criterion let to a deletion of
a minute fraction of reads (0.19%), but a substantial reduction in the
overall count of OTUs from 2275 to 926. The number of ﬁltered
reads per sample then ranged from 19 990 to 110 700 (median
32 400, IQR 27 530 to 37 260; Figure S2).
In this ﬁltered dataset, we assessed the taxonomic composition:
the most abundant phylum was Firmicutes, followed by Bacteroidetes,
Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria. Of pathogenic agents of public
health importance, only Salmonella enterica was noted in 1 sample. Its
subspecies was indeterminable using the present sequencing data,
and the bacterium disappeared in the subsequent sample taken from
the same subject 3 months later. Several children were also tran-
siently positive for Clostridium difﬁcile, whose toxigenic status was
impossible to determine from the present data.
3.3
| Association of individual OTUs with islet
autoimmunity
At the higher taxonomic levels of phyla, classes, or genera, no associ-
ation with islet autoimmunity was detectable. Then, we proceeded to
examining individual OTUs: ﬁrst such analysis was performed at the
conventionally utilized level of sequence similarity of 97%, that is,
reads similar by 97% or more were classiﬁed into 1 OTU. Here, we
identiﬁed several statistically signiﬁcant association signals. First, a
very prevalent OTU, grouping both B. dorei and B. vulgatus, was
inversely associated with subsequent islet autoimmunity, with a
binary logarithm of the fold-difference of −2.3, Padj = 4.0 × 10−4.
Another OTU comprised of a member of Bacteroidia, Bacteroides cac-
cae, was also inversely associated with the risk of islet autoimmunity,
Padj = 4.3 × 10−4. Finally, we noted a marginally signiﬁcant (Padj =
0.015) inverse association of an OTU comprised of Biﬁdobacterium
biﬁdum (Figure 1).

If these association signals are genuine, OTUs that are closest to
the associated ones should show also a decrease in cases relative to
controls, albeit statistically insigniﬁcant. The rationale is that these
OTUs have split from the main OTU either by evolution, or by
sequencing errors. Therefore, the OTUs inversely associated with
islet autoimmunity were put in sequence context of closely related
OTUs, and of reference strains from databases: the trees are pre-
sented along with the measures of association and the OTU abun-
dance in Figure 2. All but 1 related sequences are insigniﬁcantly
inversely associated with islet autoimmunity, that is, they share the
direction of the association.
3.5
| Sensitivity analysis

dates, but either the direction or the strength of the association varied
between time points (suggestive of a random effect), or the quantity of

feed of the signal between indices. Therefore, to support our main
ﬁndings, we performed a sensitivity analysis by constructing OTUs at
a decreased level of identity: 95% instead of the 97% or absolute
identity. The resolution was decreased in order to analyse the dataset
from a broader phylogenetic perspective. The inverse association of
the B. dorei-vulgatus group persisted when using the 95% identity

| Bacteriophages as modiﬁers of the bacterial

Among correlations of abundant organisms, we noted that the

| Unknown sequences and islet autoimmunity
We also tested association with islet autoimmunity for virome motifs
unassigned to any known organism: only strong signals were tested,
contigs having longer than 2000 bases with a 100-fold or higher cov-

with subsequent islet autoimmunity (uncorrected P value from .04 to
.026), but the association vanished after extending the analysis to the
clusters where the sequences belonged (Figure S4). If such associa-
tions were genuine, they would be reinforced rather than lost by
summing up the sequencing signals of additional genomic segments
of the hypothetical organism.

Our result adds to the body of evidence that an imbalance within

present ﬁnding is rather similar to what had been detected earlier in
another cohort of DIPP children who lived in a different area, Turku

associated with an imbalance between 2 most abundant species of

absent and instead indications were observable of a decrease in other
3 prevalent species of Bacteroides among case subjects.
Because the whole 16S rDNA proﬁle is based on relative abun-
dances (proportions), and there is no quantitative reference in stool
samples, it is difﬁcult to dissect a complex association like the one we
observed. Some indications come from the absolute quantiﬁcation by
real-time PCR showing an inverse association of B. vulgatus quantity
with islet autoimmunity, whereas no effect was noted for B. dorei.
When models with 16S proﬁles as predictors of islet autoimmunity
were adjusted for the total absolute quantity of 16S from real-time
PCR, the observed associations did not appreciably change: this may
indicate that the distortions associated with islet autoimmunity are in
absolute quantities, not only in the relative share within the bacter-
iome proﬁles. Admittedly, even the absolute bacterial quantities in
stool tell only little about the situation in the gut, as stool is notori-
ously known for variability in water and indigestible ﬁber content,
and there is nothing that could serve as a passive reference for bacte-
rial quantiﬁcation.
4.1
| Imbalance among species of genus Bacteroides
The imbalance within Bacteroides may be genuinely associated with
islet autoimmunity, regardless of whether B. dorei is relatively
increased, or B. vulgatus and others relatively decreased. Thus, the
previously documented increase in B. dorei in DIPP case children8
may reﬂect the same phenomenon as the decrease in several major
species of genus Bacteroides observed in the present study. The exact
nature of this imbalance could manifest in different ways depending
on many stochastic effects connected to subgroup selection within
the overall DIPP study, such as age and place. Our sample set was
collected from children with very early onset of islet autoimmunity,
and born in different geographical regions (cities of Tampere and
Oulu) compared to the Turku children in the previous report. In addi-
tion, the metabolic function of the same bacteria may differ depend-
ing on its methylation patterns as suggested earlier.27 Indeed, very
subtle differences among sequence types conferring far reaching
functional effects have been observed previously in many other ﬁelds
of pediatric infectious epidemiology—1 example is the ever-growing
classiﬁcation scheme of Burkholderia cepacia, a serious opportunistic
pathogen in cystic ﬁbrosis: here 1 species ﬁrst branched into many
genomic species to later expand further in identiﬁable epidemic
strains of dramatically diverse virulence.28
4.2
| Biﬁdobacterium and the risk of subsequent
islet autoimmunity
Two OTUs of Biﬁdobacterium were inversely associated with islet
autoimmunity (Table 2). The sequenced region of the 16S rDNA gene
is not informative regarding the exact species of 1 of them
(B. pseudocatenulatum, or catenulatum, or rarer species), the other is
B. biﬁdum. Both B. pseudocatenulatum and B. biﬁdum have been previ-
ously used in probiotic preparations, although the evidence of efﬁ-
cacy is inconclusive, if any (eg, 29), and biﬁdobacteria have been also
connected to breast-feeding.30 Both probiotics and breast-feeding
have been linked to decreased risk of developing T1D later in life.31-
33 Human humoral immunity responds to biﬁdobacteria, but this reac-
tivity does not appear to profoundly differ between children with
islet autoimmunity and controls.34 Thus, this association may be a
marker of breast-feeding and early infant diet, rather than a causative
factor that could protect from islet autoimmunity.
4.3
| Viruses and their bacterial hosts
The putative correlation of CrAssphage with the genus Bacteroides
merits further investigation. To our knowledge, this is the ﬁrst study,
which used a high-resolution 16S proﬁling to associate CrAssphage
quantity with bacterial proﬁles. Although our analysis indicated a cor-
relation between CrAssphage and B. dorei, which in turn has been
associated with an increased risk of islet autoimmunity in a previous
study 8, generalizability of such a ﬁnding is very limited. First, strong
CrAssphage signals (>3% of the virome) were found only in 4 indivi-
duals (Figure 3), second, there are apparently several distinct genetic
types (unpublished data, B. Dutilh, personal communication) of this
most widely disseminated and most abundant of all known bacterio-
phages that humans carry.11,12 Still, an interaction between a phage

4.4
| Signals of unknown origin in the virome, and
their association with islet autoimmunity
We tested only the most prominent signals of unknown origin repre-
senting the “dark matter” in the stool virome for an association with
islet autoimmunity, limiting the analysis to contiguous sequences
longer than 2000 bases, with a high coverage of 100 times or more.
Correlation analysis of the strongest signals shows that there may be
7 such organisms, but none of them was signiﬁcantly associated with
islet autoimmunity. Testing the less abundant items among putative
novel viruses would require a considerably larger data set.
4.5
| Strengths and limitations of our study design
One of the strength of this study lies in the homogeneity of the
genetic risk for T1D, very early outcome (islet autoimmunity), system-
atic collection of samples, and in the parallel evaluation of the bacter-
iome along with DNA and especially RNA virome. Such complex
information (bacterial proﬁle, DNA and RNA viruses) on serial stool
samples is unique in studies of islet autoimmunity. Also, very tight
matching was applied to controls, reﬂecting the spatial and temporal
shifts that may occur in the factors affecting the microbiome compo-
sition. We also strived for optimum resolution ability by checking our
technical performance using internationally accepted mock commu-
nity samples, and used replicates throughout the sequencing process.
In addition, we designed speciﬁc PCR assays for the 2 closely related
bacteria that target genes outside 16S and can be a simple and useful
means of their quantitative assessment in future studies. Neverthe-
less, we cannot judge their diagnostic value as the assays have not
been formally evaluated against quantiﬁed type strains.
The V4 region of the 16S rDNA is generally considered suitable
for human studies of the bacteriome proﬁles. The double indexing
strategy of the method developed by Kozich et al15 is more robust
against misidentiﬁed reads within the multiplexed set than the earlier
single-index methods, and the MiSeq 2x250 kit provides a long over-
lap between forward and reverse reads. Therefore, this bi-directional
sequencing rendered us a high degree of conﬁdence and we could
use full resolution of the sequences instead of binning by similarity.
Although binning is indispensable in ecologic studies, here we aimed
for organisms differing by only several bases or even a single base
within the sequenced region. For comparison, the usually used 97%
identity binning means that in a fragment of 250 bases sequences dif-
fering by as many as 7 bases may still end up sorted in the same
OTU. Indeed, use of V3 and V4 regions together would increase the
resolution ability (eg, B. dorei vs vulgatus differ by 3 additional nucleo-
tides in region V3), but the current bi-directional sequencing performs
better and more reliably in fragments of 250 bases (V4) as compared
to fragments having 450 bases (V3 + V4).
A limitation of the utilized sequencing strategy lies in the difﬁcul-
ties connected with distinguishing very weak positivity from com-
plete negativity for a bacterium, which is highly abundant in other
samples in the same sequencing run. Abundance estimation in low-
quantity samples is heavily inﬂuenced by misclassiﬁcation of reads
because of errors inherent to the indexing on the Illumina platform.
These issues are known,36 and even modern double indexing meth-
ods15 are not immune to such problems (own unpublished data).
Other limitations of our study are primarily related to the sample
collection protocol. First limitation is the number of samples per
individual—there have been 3 studies testing more serial samples
from each individual than we did,9,37,38 although most of the so far
published association studies investigated only 1 sample per individ-
ual. We tested 3 time points prior to ﬁrst autoantibody. This lower
count of samples was primarily because of the laborious and expen-
sive virome metagenomic sequencing. The limited count of samples
did not allow thorough analyses of independent effects conferred by
breast-feeding or by the mode of delivery. Second limitation is the
delay between collection and freezing at −70C, connected to the
overnight shipment from participants’ homes. Previous studies indi-
cated feasibility of a protocol with a 24-hours long transport at room
temperature, although the agreement on this issue is not universal.39-
41 Finally, it is the timing of samples vs islet autoimmunity. Similarly
to most other studies, we do not know when exactly the islet autoim-
munity started: blood sampling is less frequent than stool sampling
for ethical and compliance reasons, and therefore the window for
autoantibody seroconversion is relatively wide (3 months) even in this
kind of frequent follow-up scheme—still it should be covered by at
least 2 of the stool samples in every case-control pair, and our sam-
pling scheme covers also the time point where changes in the micro-
biome were noted in previous studies on islet autoimmunity.

CONCLUSIONS
An imbalance within the main species of the Bacteroides genus seems
to precede the initiation of islet autoimmunity in high-risk children.
The exact species primarily associated with the condition cannot be
ﬁrmly determined, so this evidence may presently be understood as a
marker rather than a causative factor—such a marker may be linked
to the perinatal variables, breast-feeding, or early nutrition, factors
that have long been preferential targets for T1D research. Future
studies aimed at conﬁrming our results should utilize high-resolution
sequencing strategies, as only a relatively subtle sequence difference
separates taxa with different direction of the association.
