ABSTRACT
Undernutrition remains a public health problem in the developing world with an attributable under-five death proportion of
45%. Lower gut microbiota diversity and poor metabolic output are associated with undernutrition and new therapeutic
paths may come from steering gut microbiota composition and functionality. Using a dynamic gut model, the Simulator of
Human Intestinal Microbial Ecosystem (SHIME R⃝), we investigated the effect of a lipid-based nutrient supplement enriched
with prebiotics (LNSp), compared to LNS alone and control treatment, on the composition and metabolic functionality of
fecal microbiota from three infants suffering from undernutrition. LNS elicited a significant increase in acetate and
branched-chain fatty acid production, and a higher relative abundance of the genera Prevotella, Megasphaera, Acinetobacter,
Acidaminococcus and Pseudomonas. In contrast, LNSp treatment resulted in a significant 9-fold increase in Bifidobacterium
relative abundance and a decrease in that of potential pathogens and detrimental bacteria such as Enterobacteriaceae spp.
and Bilophila sp. Moreover, the LNSp treatment resulted in a significantly higher production of acetate, butyrate and
propionate, as compared to control and LNS. Our results suggest that provision of prebiotic-enhanced LNS to

BACKGROUND
Undernutrition remains a major public health concern in devel-
oping countries, contributing to ∼45% of all-cause deaths in
children under 5 years of age (Black et al. 2013). Definitions of
undernutrition in children include wasting or acute malnutri-
tion [weight for height/length z-score (WHZ) ←2SD], stunting or
chronic malnutrition [height/length for age z-score (HAZ) ←2SD]
and underweight [weight for age z-score (WAZ) ←2SD], which
is a hybrid indicator. These conditions are considered moder-
ate when the z-score is comprised between −2SD and −3SD,
and severe when the z-score < −3SD (WHO and UNICEF 2009;
UNICEF 2013). Undernutrition stems from recurrent infections,
poor dietary patterns and malabsorption evolving into a vicious
circle in which gut microbiota have been recognized to play
a key role (Kau et al. 2011). The scientific domain often refers
to a microbiome characterized by an increase in potentially
pathogenic or detrimental bacteria (Monira et al. 2011; Smith
et al. 2013; Ghosh et al. 2014; Gough et al. 2015), or lower diversity
and decreased metabolic activity compared to healthy controls
(Monira et al. 2011; Preidis et al. 2015). Furthermore, Subramanian
et al. (2014) and Smith et al. (2013) evidenced a microbial imma-
turity in undernourished children, defined as a microbiota for
age z-score ≤−2. The microbiota immaturity was not resolved
by current nutritional therapeutics that mainly rely on the provi-
sion of ready-to-use therapeutic or supplementary foods, which
are lipid-base nutrient supplements (LNSs).
In recent years, much attention has been directed to the
relationship between gut microbiota and undernutrition, in an
effort to find new therapeutics to tackle the condition. It is
now reported that nutritional interventions that include a com-
ponent targeting gut microbiota may have a better chance of
durably resolving undernutrition (Blanton et al. 2016a; Hermann
and Foligne 2017; Pekmez, Dragsted and Brahe 2018). However,
proof-of-concept preclinical studies are needed to document the
rationale for future in vivo studies. In this regard, animal studies
have shown promising results (Subramanian et al. 2014; Blanton
et al. 2016b); nevertheless, human models are required to enable
extrapolation of results.
Diet is an important factor that influences gut microbiota (De
Filippo et al. 2010; Jumpertz et al. 2011; Wu et al. 2011) and, as
such, can be used to steer microbiota toward a beneficial com-
position. In return, a healthy gut microbiota allows additional
energy harvest from the diet, which is essential for growing
infants whose diet faces the challenge of providing the required
energy and nutrients for a healthy growth through a limited
capacity digestive system (Martorell, Khan and Schroeder 1994).
In addition, the gut microbiota actively shapes host immunity
while providing protection against potential pathogens, thus
enhancing resistance to infections (Dimmitt et al. 2010; Kau et al.
2011).
Methods to steer gut microbiota toward a beneficial composi-
tion include the provision of prebiotics, which are non-digestible
oligosaccharides that are fermented by bacteria in the colon into
short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) (Gibson and Roberfroid 1995; Gib-
son et al. 2004). SCFAs constitute additional source of energy for
colonocytes as well as for the host (Bergman 1990; Fern´andez
et al. 2016).
The present study aims to assess the effect of an LNS,
which is a complementary food supplement for the prevention
of undernutrition in children and enriched with prebiotics, on
undernourished child microbiota functionality and composition
in a dynamic human digestive tract model.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Participants and inoculum
Fecal samples were obtained from three undernourished infants
of 10 months living in rural Burkina Faso, West Africa. Infants’
demographic and anthropometric characteristics are presented
in Table 1. None of the infants was exposed to antibiotics in the
three weeks before sampling. All infants were still breastfed,
and in the community, millet gruel with added sugar is used
as complementary food in all infants, and sometimes, infants
are fed the family meal (data not yet published). The samples
were frozen at −80◦C within 1 h after collection and shipped to
Belgium on dry ice. Upon reception in Belgium, samples were
stored at −80◦C until further processing. After thawing at ambi-
ent temperature, 20 g of each fecal sample was added 100 mL
of anaerobic phosphate buffer 0.1 M, pH 6.8 (containing 4.4 g
K2HPO, 3.4 g KH2PO4, 0.5 g sodium thioglycolate as reducing
agent, and 500 mL of demineralized water) and stomached (Lab-
Blender 400, LEDTechno) for 5 min. The resultant homogenized
slurry was centrifuged for 2 min at 500 g and the supernatant
was used to inoculate the SHIME R⃝vessels.
An in-house baby SHIME R⃝feed was used as the basic
medium for all treatment conditions and was composed as fol-
lows: Nutrilon plus, a follow-on formula (Danone S.A., Paris,
France) was purchased on the market. All other compounds for
the basic baby SHIME R⃝feed were purchased from Sigma Aldrich
(Bornem, Belgium). To mimic malnutrition status, the amounts
of compounds in the basic medium were decreased by 20% (FAO,
WHO and UNU 1980). The feed thus contained Nutrilon plus
(4.0 g L−1), Pectin (0.8 g L−1), Mucin (3.2 g L−1), Starch (0.8 g L−1),
Cellobiose (0.8 g L−1), Glucose (0.8 g L−1), Proteose peptone (0.8 g
L−1), and l-Cysteine (0.2 g L−1). Mucin beads were prepared using
100 mL autoclaved distilled water containing 5 g porcine mucin
type II, 1 g agar and 350 μL of 10 M NaOH to effect a pH of 6.8.
Pancreatic juice composed of NaHCO3 (12.5 g L−1), bile salts (6 g
L−1) and pancreatin (0.9 g L−1) was also prepared for small intes-
tine digestion.
Test compounds
Small-quantity LNSs (Enov’Nutributter R⃝+) were provided by
Nutriset SAS (Malaunay, France). The following ingredients com-
posed the LNS matrix: vegetable oils (soybean), skimmed milk
powder, peanuts, sugar, vitamin and mineral complex, fully
hydrogenated mix of vegetable oils and emulsifiers mono- and
diglycerides. To account for the absorption of digested lipids that
normally takes place in the human small intestine where dietary
lipids are absorbed up to 99% (Gangl and Ockner 1975; Mu and
Porsgaard 2005), LNS was defatted using 10 mL cyclohexane for
20 g LNS prior to use in the experiment. The nutrient composi-
tion of LNS is given in Table S1 (Supporting Information).

Experimental setup
An M-SHIME R⃝(Ghent University and ProDigest, Ghent, Belgium)
setup, simulating the human gastrointestinal tract and includ-
ing both luminal and mucosal layers in the colon compartment,
was used as previously described (Van den Abbeele et al. 2012).
The setup is schematized in Fig. 1. One colon vessel per child
and treatment condition was used, and the pH in colon vessels
was maintained at 6.6–6.9 through a system of automatic pumps
connected to a probe and to acid and base supplies.
The inocula from each of the three infants were submitted
to a control treatment (SHIME R⃝feed alone), an LNS treatment
(SHIME R⃝feed + 2 g LNS) and an LNSp treatment (SHIME R⃝feed
+ 2 g LNS + 1.5 g native inulin + 1.5 g FOS). The daily amount of
LNS was 2 g, assuming that LNS is 90% digestible. Feed (140 mL,
three times per day) and pancreatic juice (60 mL, three times
per day) were supplied to the system through automatic pumps.
The experiments were run for 27 days. A stabilization period was
observed from Day 1 to Day 14 under SHIME R⃝feed only, to allow
the bacterial community to adjust to in vitro conditions. Subse-
quently, the treatment consisting of adding LNS or LNSp in the
relevant vessels was conducted from Day 15 to Day 27.
Sampling
Luminal samples were taken daily from SHIME R⃝vessels, and
mucous layer samples three times per week. Luminal samples
taken during the treatment period, i.e. from Day 15 to Day 27,
were analyzed for microbial community composition and SCFA
content. Additionally, Day 0, Day 14, Day 15, Day 21 and Day 27
samples were further analyzed for number of gene copy content.
For both analyses, 1 mL sample was taken. For DNA extraction,
the sample was centrifuged at 18 000 g for 5 min and the pellet
was used.
Microbial community analysis
16s rRNA gene sequencing
16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing analysis was performed as
described before (De Vrieze et al. 2016; De Paepe et al. 2017). In
brief, DNA extraction was performed by means of bead beat-
ing with a FastPrep-24 5G instrument (MP Biomedicals, Santa
Ana, CA, USA) and phenol/chloroform extraction. The 16S rRNA
gene V3–V4 hypervariable regions were amplified by PCR using
primers 341F (5′-CCT ACG GGN GGC WGC AG-3′) and 785Rmod
(5′-GAC TAC HVG GGT ATC TAA KCC-3′). The reverse primer
was adapted from Klindworth et al. (2013) to increase cover-
age. PCR was performed using Taq DNA Polymerase with the
Fermentas PCR Kit according to the manufacturers’ specifica-
tions (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). 10 μL of
the original genomic DNA extract was sent out to LGC genomics
GmbH (Berlin, Germany) for library preparation and sequenc-
ing on an Illumina Miseq platform with v3 chemistry, with
the primers mentioned above. PCRs were carried out for 30
cycles. To assess the sequencing quality, a mock community
was included in triplicate in the sequencing run. Read assembly
and cleanup was largely derived from the MiSeq SOP described
by the Schloss lab (Schloss, Gevers and Westcott 2011; Kozich
et al. 2013). Briefly, mothur (v. 1.38) was used to assemble paired-
end reads into contigs, perform alignment-based quality filter-
ing (alignment to the mothur-reconstructed SILVA SEED align-
ment, v. 123), remove chimeras, assign taxonomy using a na¨ıve
Bayesian classifier (Wang et al. 2007) and RDP release 14 (Cole
et al. 2009) and cluster contigs into operational taxonomic units
(OTUs) at 97% sequence similarity. All sequences that were clas-
sified as Eukaryota, Archaea, Chloroplasts and Mitochondria
were removed. Furthermore, if sequences could not be classified
at all, even at kingdom level, they were removed. For each OTU,
representative sequences were picked as the most abundant
sequence within that OTU. The raw fastq files that were used to
create the OTU table, which served as a basis for the microbial
community analysis, have been submitted to the National Cen-
ter for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database under acces-
sion number PRJNA551226.
RT-qPCR analysis
Real-time qPCR assays were performed using StepOnePlus real-
time PCR system. DNA samples were diluted 10.0 times for
further processing. Reactions were performed in a volume of
20 μL consisting of 10 μL of 2× iTaq universal SYBR Green
supermix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA), 2.0 μL DNA
template, 0.8 μL (10μM stock) of each primer (338F: ACTCC-
TACGGGAGGCAGCAG and 518R: ATTACCGCGGCTGCTGG) and
6.4 μL nuclease-free water. Amplifications were run as follows:
initial denaturation for 2 min at 95◦C, followed by 40 cycles of 15 s
denaturation at 95◦C and combined anneal/extension 1 min at
60◦C. At the end of the qPCR run, a melting curve analysis was
performed to confirm product specificity (60–95◦C, T per 15 s
= 0.3◦C). Quantification was performed using a standard curve
based on known concentrations of DNA standard dilutions from
107 copies μL−1 down to 10 copies μL−1. All qPCR analyses were
conducted in triplicate.
Short-chain fatty acid analysis
C2–C8 fatty acid analysis was performed according to Andersen
et al. (2014). Briefly, liquid samples were conditioned with sul-
furic acid and sodium chloride. An internal standard, 2-methyl
hexanoic acid, was used for quantification of further extraction
with diethyl ether. C2–C8 fatty acids (including isoforms C4–C6)
were measured by gas chromatography (GC-2014, Shimadzu R⃝,
The Netherlands) with a DB-FFAP 123-3232 column (30 m ×
0.32 mm × 0.25 μm; Agilent, Belgium) and a Flame Ionization
Detector (FID). Prepared sample (1μL) was injected at 280◦C with

Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed in R version 3.5.2 (R core
team 2018). The significance level was set at 5% for all hypothesis
tests. The analysis code can be accessed at https://github.com/L
aeticiatoe/MalnutShime.
16s rRNA sequencing data analysis was done using mainly
the packages ‘vegan’ (Oksanen et al. 2018), ‘phyloseq’ (McMurdie
and Holmes 2013) and ‘DESeq2’ (Love, Huber and Anders 2014).
Alpha diversity was computed and compared between treat-
ment conditions using linear mixed models.
A rarefaction to even depth was done to normalize sam-
pling effort before comparing communities. Beta diversity was
assessed based on the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity measure and
visualized using non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS)
and heatmaps.
A differential expression analysis based on the negative
binomial distribution (DESeq2) was performed to identify OTUs
whose relative abundance was most affected by treatment con-
dition. A Bonferroni correction was applied to correct for multi-
ple comparisons.
Real-time qPCR was used to determine the number of gene
copies per μL sample. Comparisons of numbers of gene copies
per μL were done between samples from different days using
Wilcoxon signed rank test, and between treatment groups using
Kruskal–Wallis test.
To assess the metabolic activity of the microbial commu-
nity, acetate, propionate, butyrate and branched-chain fatty
acids (BCFA) consisting of the sum of isobutyrate, isovalerate
and isocaproate concentrations were considered, as those con-
stitute >95% of the total SCFA production (Cummings et al.
1987; den Besten et al. 2013) and, as such, are a good reflec-
tion of the metabolic activity. Differences in acetate, propionate,
butyrate and BCFA production between treatment conditions
were assessed using linear mixed models. A stepwise model
selection was done using a parametric bootstrapping to deter-
mine the best model fits to the data, and when the result was
not conclusive, the Akaike information criterion was used. Mod-
els’ goodness of fit were post-hoc checked by plotting residual
Q–Q plots. Predicted marginal means were then computed con-
trasting all levels of treatment groups with a Tukey family-wise
α correction for multiple comparisons.

RESULTS
Microbial community composition
Microbiota originated from three undernourished infants and
were submitted to three treatment conditions: control, LNS and
LNSp. A total of 117 samples were taken daily from the SHIME R⃝
vessels from Day 15 to Day 27. The sequencing of three sam-
ples failed, resulting in a final dataset of n = 114 samples.
Sequencing results included 7373 OTUs at 97% sequence iden-
tity belonging to the phyla Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes,
Actinobacteria, Verrucomicrobia and 1 unidentified bacterial phy-
lum. Within these phyla, 84 different genera were identified.
Minimum, median and maximum read counts were respectively
2266, 8860 and 26825.
Identified bacterial genera that had >1% relative abundance
were visualized using a bar graph per infant and treatment con-
dition. A high inter-individual variability was observed (Fig. 2).
In the control treatment condition, the detected genera of
>1% relative abundance were mainly Megamonas, Megasphaera,
Escherichia/ Shigella, Enterobacteriaceae, Enterococcus, Veillonella
and Pseudomonas in infant 1, Megamonas, Megasphaera, Enterobac-
teriaceae, Mitsuokella, Prevotella, Acidaminococcus and Bacteroides in
infant 2 and Megasphaera, Enterobacteriaceae, Mitsuokella, Mega-
monas, Bacteroides, Bilophila and Prevotella in infant 3.
In the LNS treatment condition, infant 1 harbored mainly
bacteria of genus Megamonas that was overridden by Prevotella,
Mitsuokella, Megasphaera, Escherichia/Shigella and Enterobacteri-
aceae from Day 22 on. The same trend was observed in infants
2 and 3. Additionally, the genera Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, Aci-
daminococcus, Veillonella, Bacteroides and Dialister were detected to
a lesser extent in all infants.
In
the
LNSp
treatment
condition,
the
genera
Escherichia/Shigella, Megamonas, Megasphaera, Mitsuokella and
Enterobacteriaceae predominated in all infants. Bifidobacterium
was more detected in infants 1 and 2, and Prevotella in infants 2
and 3. Bacteroides was mainly identified in infant 3.
Bifidobacterium passed the threshold of 1% relative abundance
in the LNSp treatment condition only.
A day-by-day fluctuation in the relative abundance of
detected genera could be noticed for each infant in all treatment
conditions (Fig. 2).
Alpha diversity was computed in R using the function ‘esti-
mate richness’ of the package ‘phyloseq’. The treatment did not
result in any significant difference in alpha diversity measured
by four indexes (Species richness, Chao1 index, Shannon index
and Inverse Simpson index) that were compared between treat-
ment conditions using linear mixed models (results not shown).
After rarefaction to the minimum read count observed in
a sample, beta diversity was assessed based on Bray–Curtis
distance between samples and visualized using NMDS ordina-
tion plots. Samples were more similar within treatment groups,
with LNSp resulting in a distinct clustering pattern in infant 1
and infant 3. Samples from infant 2 presented a mixed pattern
(Fig. 3). The Bray–Curtis dissimilarity matrix was used to plot
heatmaps of the 20 most abundant genera per infant. Again,
communities’ structure stayed more similar in the first 3 days of
treatment and samples clustered by treatment group thereafter.
The 20 most abundant bacterial genera differed by infant. Bifi-
dobacteria were observed almost exclusively in the LNSp treat-
ment group (Figures S1–S3, Supporting Information).
To determine which OTUs were differentially affected in
terms of variation of relative abundance by treatment condi-
tion, a DESeq model was fitted on OTU abundances including
treatment, day and infant as predictors. OTUs with significantly
different relative abundance were then extracted and ordered
based on their Log2-fold change (LFC). OTUs with at least 2-
fold change, i.e. with doubled or halved relative abundance were
then retained for visualization (Fig. 4; Figures S4–S12, Supporting
Information).
The LNS treatment resulted in a significant variation in the
relative abundance of a large number of OTUs. That effect was
mostly infant specific (Fig. 4; Figures S4–S6, Supporting Infor-
mation). Nevertheless, some common features emerged: com-
mon OTUs with significantly increased relative abundance were
OTU15 (Acinetobacter) in all infants, OTU11 (Acidaminococcus) in
infants 1 and 3, OTU9 (Pseudomonas) in infants 2 and 3, while
several Proteobacterial OTUs’ relative abundance decreased. The
OTUs with the most strongly increased relative abundance were
OTU6 (Prevotella, 9-fold), OTU3 (Megasphaera, 8-fold) and OTU2
(Mitsuokella, 7.5-fold) in infant 1. Conversely, OTU32 and OTU13
(Veillonella) read count was significantly lower in the LNS treat-
ment condition together with that of Lactobacillus in all infants
(OTU24 and OTU50). In addition, despite an overall negative LFC
of −3.7, OTU17 (Bilophila) exhibited a relative abundance increase
from Day 24 (Fig. 4; Figures S4–S6 and Table S2, Supporting Infor-
mation).
Compared to control, LNSp resulted, in Infant 1, in a 9-
fold increase in OTU12 (Bifidobacterium), an 8-fold increase in
OTU3 (Megasphaera) while the relative abundance of OTU13
(Veillonella) decreased by 7-fold. In Infant 2, the read count of
OTU19 (Bifidobacterium) was enhanced by 7-fold, followed by
OTU9 (Pseudomonas, 4-fold). Two Bacteroides spp., OTU18 and
OTU8, decreased by 6-fold. In infant 3, OTU21 (Megasphaera) and
OTU12 (Bifidobacterium) had ∼5-fold higher relative abundance,
while OTU18 (Bacteroides) and OTU17 (Bilophila) relative abun-
dance decreased by 7- and 8-fold respectively. The temporal
trend showed that, despite a LFC varying from −4 to 4, OTU11
(Acidaminococcus), had a relative abundance fluctuating around
its initial level in the LNSp treatment condition, while its read
count did not increase in the control treatment condition (Fig. 4;
Figures S7–S9 and Table S2, Supporting Information).
Compared to LNS, LNSp resulted in 5- to 10-fold increase
OTU12 and OTU19 (Bifidobacterium) relative abundance in all
infants, and in a decrease by 6- to 7-fold of OTU15 (Acinetobacter).
A pronounced decreased relative abundance was also observed
for OTU6 (Prevotella) in infant 1. After a period of stable count
(Day 15 to Day 24), OTU4 (Escherichia/Shigella) and OTU14 (Enter-
obacteriaceae sp.) relative abundance started to decrease. The rel-
ative abundance of Veillonella was confirmed to not increase in
the LNSp treatment condition, while that of Acinetobacter was
higher under LNS alone. Again, Bilophila could take advantage
of the LNS nutrient platform and increase after 8 days of treat-
ment (from Day 24), which was not the case in the presence of
prebiotics (Fig. 4; Figures S10–S12 and Table S2, Supporting Infor-
mation).
To determine whether particular OTUs relative abundances
were related to SCFA production, we analyzed the correlation
between OTUs with abundance > 1% and acetate, propionate,
butyrate and BCFA concentration, using Pearson rho. Most cor-
relations were weak (rho < 0.5). However, OTU15 (Acinetobacter)
was strongly correlated to BCFA concentration in infants 1 and
3 (rho = 0.73 in both cases).

DISCUSSION
A long-term incubation in a dynamic gut model was performed
using fecal microbiota from infants suffering from undernutri-
tion, a condition known to be associated with lower gut micro-
biota diversity and metabolic functionality, as well as over-
all gut microbiota immaturity (Smith et al. 2013; Ghosh et al.
2014; Gough et al. 2015; Subramanian et al. 2015; Blanton et al.
2016b). The experiment showed an important effect of prebiotic-
enhanced LNS (LNSp) on both the composition and metabolic
activity of the fecal microbiota.
Microbiota from the infants, despite sharing a certain num-
ber of taxa also featured inter-individual differences, a charac-
teristic of human gut microbiota outlined in several other stud-
ies (Falony et al. 2016; De Paepe et al. 2017; Healey et al. 2017; Jones

et al. 2018). Furthermore, the three infants sampled from a larger
cohort for this experiment suffered different degrees of under-
nutrition. While all infants were moderately wasted, infants 1
and 3 were stunted, with the condition reaching the severe level
in infant 3 (z-score length for age ←3). The same infants (1 and
3) were also severely underweight, while infant 2 was moder-
ately underweight but not stunted. Wasting is strongly predicted
by gut microbiota immaturity, a condition described by Subra-
manian et al. (2014) in children from Bangladesh. The condition
is characterized, in a given child, by the lack of certain bacte-
rial genera normally present in children of the same age, while
genera more characteristic of younger children persist. Children
from whom samples were taken in our study were 10 months
old and were fed mainly millet gruel as complementary food.
The introduction of complementary food is known to promote
microbiota diversification with an increase in bacteria from
the genera Clostridium, Bilophila, Ruminococcus, Bacteroides, Pre-
votella and Veillonella (B¨ackhed et al. 2015; Tanaka and Nakayama
2017). Although not sufficient to characterize the microbiota as
immature in this study, it is noteworthy that two genera,
Ruminococcus and Clostridium, also identified as major age-
discriminatory (Subramanian et al. 2014), were not identified
in any of our samples. Furthermore, the gut microbiota of
stunted children is characterized by an increased representation
of potential pathobionts like Escherichia/Shigella, enterobacteria
and Veillonella and lack of butyrate producing clostridia (M´endez-
Salazar et al. 2018; Vonaesch et al. 2018), a feature confirmed in
the present experiment.
Despite these inter-individual differences, the shift in OTU
relative abundances when LNS was added with inulin and FOS,
was consistent: we observed more representatives of the genus
Bifidobacterium, while relative abundances of Veillonella, Acineto-
bacter, Bacteroides, Enterobacteriaceae and Bilophila decreased. The
changes in different OTUs’ relative abundance could also be
visualized in the clustering pattern of samples as shown by the
measures of beta diversity. Indeed, over time, samples from dif-
ferent treatment condition tended to cluster together, a ten-
dency with infant specific patterns. The effect of the prebiotics
was further singled out by contrasting LNS and LNSp thus tak-
ing out the effect of LNS. The bifidogenic effect of inulin and FOS

confirms findings in studies with healthy infants (Costalos et al.
2008; Stiverson et al. 2014; Ripoll et al. 2015; Wernimont et al. 2015)
and has been attributed to the presence of short-chain FOS and
to cross-feeding, thanks to primary inulin degraders (Rossi et al.
2005; Flint et al. 2014; R´ıos-Covi´an et al. 2016). Conversely, the
relative abundance of Enterobacteriaceae decreased by 2–4-fold in
the presence of prebiotics. In the absence of a significant varia-
tion in total number of gene copies between days and between
treatment conditions during the treatment period, as shown by
the real-time qPCR data, it is likely that the community actually
shifted to harboring more bifidobacteria and less enterobacteria.
Bifidobacterium spp. are one of the most studied probiotic bac-
teria and are known to provide several health benefits to the
host. Beside the production of acetate and lactate by degrad-
ing carbohydrates, bifidobacteria have been described to also
inhibit bacterial and viral pathogens such as enterotoxic and
enteropathogenic Escherichia coli, Clostridium perfringens, Clostrid-
ium difficile and rotavirus who are responsible for most diarrheal
episodes in infants (Zhong et al. 2004).
Other OTUs with significantly higher relative abundance in
the LNS and LNSp treatment conditions belonged to the Megas-
phaera and Mitsuokella genera. However, comparing LNS and
LNSp with control condition evidenced that the higher rela-
tive abundance of those OTUs was mainly due to the LNS food
matrix. LNS alone also positively affected the relative abundance
of Prevotella, Acidaminococcus, Acinetobacter, Dialister, but did not
elicit any effect on the relative abundance of bifidobacteria. With
the provision of 2 g defatted LNS in treatment vessels, 52 mg
proteins and 94 mg carbohydrates (mainly mono- and disaccha-
rides) were daily supplied to the bacterial community. The quan-
tity of lipids entering the vessels was negligible in this exper-
iment. Human gut isolates of Megasphaera have been found to
harbor a wide range of carbohydrate active enzymes alongside
with oligopeptide transport systems allowing them to use carbo-
hydrates as well as a wide range of amino acids as carbon source
(Shetty et al. 2013), thus conferring them a competitive advan-
tage, an ability that may have contributed to its increased rela-
tive abundance in our setting. Acidaminococcus, a Gram-negative
Firmicutes who can thrive on proteins alone (Kim et al. 2004;
D’Auria et al. 2011) and is associated with growth faltering in
children (Gough et al. 2015) had a significantly increased rela-
tive abundance in the LNS treatment condition in infant 1 and
infant 3 who suffered the condition. Prevotella is associated with
plant rich diet and was found to be predominant in children
from Burkina Faso (De Filippo et al. 2010, 2017). However, some
Prevotella species, together with Acinetobacter and Mitsuokella are
also found at high abundances in chronic inflammation status
(Ghosh et al. 2014; Subramanian et al. 2014; Dinh et al. 2016).
Undernutrition, especially in its chronic form, is associated with
environmental or tropical enteropathy, a condition character-
ized by chronic intestinal inflammation and impaired intestinal
barrier function (Campbell et al. 2003; Salvatore et al. 2008; McKay
et al. 2010; Korpe and Petri 2012). In this context, whether the
high abundance of genus Prevotella in African children in gen-
eral, and in the infants sampled for the present experiment in
particular, is linked to plant based diet or to a subclinical intesti-
nal inflammation status remains to be investigated.
Our study also revealed LNSp to result in a significant
decrease in the relative abundance of OTUs belonging to the gen-
era Veillonella, Bilophila and Bacteroides, and in a delayed manner,
of bacteria ascribed to the family Enterobacteriaceae. As malnu-
trition stems from poor dietary patterns and infections (UNICEF
2013), the observed effect on these potential pathogens and
pro-inflammation bacteria is of major importance for under-
nourished children, and, should it be confirmed in vivo, could
prevent further evolution toward severe acute malnutrition.
While both LNS and LNSp treatments significantly increased
the production of acetate, LNS alone lead to a significantly
higher production of BCFA compared to control and LNSp.
Acetate is the main SCFA produced in the gut and can be synthe-
sized by many gut microbes from pyruvate via the acetyl-CoA or
via the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway. These include Bacteroides, bifi-
dobacteria, Veillonella, enterobacteria, enterococci and Prevotella
(Macfarlane and Macfarlane 2012; Koh et al. 2016), many of which
were enriched under the LNS and LNSp treatments. The main
substrate for acetate synthesis is carbohydrate, and as a mat-
ter of fact, the LNS food matrix contains mono- and disaccha-
rides that could be readily used to increase acetate production.
Moreover, acetate can also be produced from proteins, mainly
when nutrient source becomes scarce (Cummings and Macfar-
lane 1991; Wall et al. 2009), which was probably the case in
the LNS treatment condition as glucose and disaccharides are
quickly consumed by the bacterial community. The increased
production of BCFA can be considered a marker for increased
microbial protein metabolism primarily. BCFAs are produced
from branched-chain amino acids like leucine, isoleucine and
valine, which are abundant in both cow milk and peanut, the two
main ingredients of LNS (Beach, Bernstein and Macy 1941; Kay-
ode Adebisi et al. 2017). Increased colonic protein metabolism
may yield metabolites (phenols, indoles and amines) that corre-
late with less favorable health effects.
Finally, the LNSp treatment significantly resulted in higher
butyrate and propionate production. This confirms the butyro-
genic effect of inulin and FOS demonstrated in previous stud-
ies (Jung, Jeon and Han 2015; Poeker et al. 2018; Baxter et al.
2019). Butyrate is produced in the gut through several pathways,
of which the acetyl-CoA butyryl-CoA transferase pathway, that
consists in the condensation of two acetyl-CoA molecules, is the
most important (Miller and Wolin 1996; Reichardt et al. 2014).
The most plausible production pathways for propionate in this
experiment are the succinate and acrylate pathways, proceeding
form carbohydrate metabolism and using succinate and lactate
respectively as substrates (Koh et al. 2016). A high SCFA produc-
tion lowers the pH in the intestinal lumen, hindering the growth
of potential pathogens (Sun and O’Riordan 2013). Furthermore,
acetate and propionate are used in lipogenesis, gluconeogenesis
and can also reduce inflammation by lowering the production
of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Bergman 1990; Macfarlane and
Macfarlane 2012; Fern´andez et al. 2016). Butyrate has been exten-
sively studied and is known to serve as the main energy source
for colonocytes, enhancing cell differentiation and tight junc-
tion proteins, thus improving intestinal barrier (Bergman 1990;
den Besten et al. 2013; R´ıos-Covi´an et al. 2016). Butyrate also plays
a major role in the modulation of immune response by stimulat-
ing the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines and inducing
the differentiation of na¨ıve T cells to regulatory T cells (Kaisar
et al. 2017; Lee et al. 2017). Although traditional major butyrate
producers were not differentially expressed in this study, the
level of butyrate was consistently higher in the LNSp treatment
condition as compared to LNS and control. Beside the produc-
tion of propionate, human gut isolates of Megasphaera, have been
shown to produce butyrate from glucose (Shetty et al. 2013). It is
thus possible that in the absence of traditional butyrate produc-
ers like Roseburia and Faecalibacterium, which are often depleted
in undernourished children (Million et al. 2016), Megasphaera
could occupy this functional niche to a certain extent. However,

the sole activity of Megasphaera is not sufficient to explain the

The microbial community used in this study was sampled
from undernourished children who need higher energy provi-
sion to recover from the condition. As gut microbiota can pro-
vide up to 15% of daily energy requirements (B¨ackhed et al. 2004;
Wolf 2006; Krajmalnik-Brown et al. 2012) from SCFAs, the signif-
icantly higher SCFA production in the LNSp compared to LNS
alone indicates that addition of prebiotics may lead to a faster
recovery. Ultimately, further in-vivo studies are needed to verify
that hypothesis.
Limitations
Our study has some limitations that warrant caution. First, the
fecal samples were frozen and transported from Burkina Faso
to Belgium on dry ice. Freezing samples and thawing them
unfortunately always results in some loss of bacteria, albeit
not significant (Kerckhof et al. 2014). Second, the in vitro condi-
tions consisted of providing on a daily basis a standard nutri-
tional medium during the stabilization period. That nutritional
medium is different from the actual diet of the subject from
whom the samples were taken and can result in additional loss
and/or shift in microbial community composition. However, it
has been demonstrated that despite this unavoidable loss in
biomass during the stabilization phase, key taxa are retained in
SHIME experiments (De Paepe et al. 2018) making SHIME systems
fairly representative of the original community.
CONCLUSION
Our study demonstrated the bifidogenic effect of inulin and FOS
in microbiota stemming from undernourished children. While
this bifidogenic effect can be attributed primarily to the presence
of short-chain FOS and cross-feeding, it is noteworthy that one
major outcome of it may be the sharp decrease in bacterial gen-
era and families containing potential pathogens and detrimen-
tal representatives such as Veillonella, Enterobacteriaceae, Desul-
fovibrio and Bilophila. Moreover, in the absence of major butyrate
producers as often observed in undernourished children, func-
tional redundancy allowed a high butyrogenic effect of the LNSp
treatment, alongside with higher production of acetate and pro-
pionate, while LNS alone was limited to a significant higher pro-
duction of acetate. Whether these effects of LNSp can be repro-
duced in vivo remains to be investigated in a much needed effort
to improve future nutritional interventions for the prevention
and/or treatment of undernutrition.
