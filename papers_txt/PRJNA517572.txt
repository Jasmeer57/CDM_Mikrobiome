Impact of Postnatal Antibiotics and Parenteral Nutrition on
the Gut Microbiota in Preterm Infants During Early Life
Jie Jia, PhD1,2,3,4,∗
; Pengcheng Xun, MD, PhD5,∗

Abstract
Background: The early-life gut microbiota, which is critically important for the long-term health of infants, is normally sensitive
to perturbations, especially in preterm infants. However, how the gut microbiota develops and what key factors affect the preterm
gut microbiota remain largely unknown. We hypothesized that preterm microbial dysbiosis exists from the beginning after birth,
and microbial alteration is associated with parenteral nutrition and antibiotic therapy interventions. Methods: Fecal samples
were collected from fifty-one preterm and fifty full-term vaginally delivered (FTVD) infants at 7 time points for 90 days after
birth. The microbial profiles of 558 fecal DNA samples were analyzed by sequencing their 16S ribosomal RNA amplicons. A
random-effects generalized least square regression was used to identify factors that influence the bacterial composition over time.
Results: The altered gut microbiota in preterm infants existed from the meconium, having significantly lower levels of Escherichia-
Shigella than those in FTVD infants. The developmental trajectories of 7 predominant bacterial groups successfully fitted with
exponential/linear function curves (R2, 0.921–0.993) in both groups. By day 90, depleted levels of Bacteroides and Parabacteroides
and an overabundance of Peptoclostridium were characteristic of the preterm group. The prolonged use of antibiotics and parenteral
nutrition had significant adverse effects on the Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium levels in preterm infants. Moreover, gestational age,
sex, and birth weight were factors impacting specific genera in preterm infants. Conclusion: The early-life microbial composition
and functions were markedly different in preterm infants, being associated with the prolonged use of postnatal antibiotics and
parenteral nutrition. (JPEN J Parenter Enteral Nutr. 2019;00:1–16)

Introduction
The early colonization and development of the microbiota
in the gastrointestinal tract (gut) of neonates is critically
important for the infant’s physiology and metabolic and
immune programming and has a profound impact on pe-
diatric and lifelong health.1,2 Disruption of the microbiota
in early life has been associated with immune and metabolic
diseases in later life, such as asthma and obesity.3-5 The gut
microbiota of infants is unstable and can be affected by a
number of environmental factors during early life, such as
feeding regimen and antibiotic therapy.6,7 However, most of
the studies have been conducted on Western populations,
and the geography could also be a critical factor shaping
the human gut microbiota.8 Therefore, studies of the early
life gut microbiota in infants from Eastern countries, such
as China, are urgently needed to confirm the generalizability
of the findings on the Western populations.
Compared with that in full-term neonates, the criti-
cal stage of initial gut colonization in preterm infants
was more likely found to occur in the hospital neonatal
intensive care unit (NICU).9 Preterm infants were also
demonstrated to have a higher prevalence of Proteobac-
teria, a delayed establishment of the normal microbiota
composition, and an alteration in the intestinal short-chain
fatty acid composition.10 The early life microbial alteration
in preterm infants is considered as a high-risk factor for
the development of infectious diseases, such as neonatal
necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC).2 Although broad-spectrum
antibiotic treatments are commonly used for reducing in-
fections in preterm infants, increasing evidence has shown
an association of antibiotic use with reductions in the gut
microbial diversity as well as with the overabundance of
multidrug-resistant bacteria (such as Escherichia, Klebsiella,
and Enterobacter) in these infants.11,12 The question of
whether long-term and short-term antibiotic use causes
different alterations in the gut microbiota needs more in-
vestigation. Parenteral nutrition (PN) is another necessary
intervention practiced in the NICU to meet the adequate
nutrition intake of preterm infants and reduce their early
weight loss.13 However, the long-term use of PN can cause
severe complications, including sepsis and liver failure. In
an animal model of PN dependency, a reduction in the
bacterial diversity of the gut microbiota was found, along
with a high level of pathogenic bacteria.14 One study of
infants with short bowel syndrome showed that PN was
associated with a lower level of Firmicutes,15 which was
presumed to be dominant in the nutrient-rich rather than the
nutrient-deprived environment caused by PN.16 However,
the influence of early PN on the establishment of the
premature gut microbiota remains largely unknown. Thus,
it is important to gain a better understanding regarding the
alterations of the gut microbiota in preterm infants and
how environmental factors disturb microbial development
in the gut in early life. In this study, we aimed to obtain
a longitudinal view of the initial colonization and early
development of the gut microbiota and of their functions in
preterm infants compared with those in full-term vaginally
delivered (FTVD) infants and then to confirm the associ-
ations of some reported factors (eg, gestational age, birth
weight, and feeding type) with the alteration of the infant
gut microbiota, especially those of 2 important clinical
interventions, that is, PN and antibiotic use.
Methods
Subject Enrollment
Preterm infants were recruited at the Department of NICU,
Xin Hua Hospital, School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiao Tong
University, China, from July 2014 to February 2015. The
inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) gestational age of
<37 weeks; (2) no gastrointestinal tract disorders, metabolic
diseases, or viral hepatitis, and (3) hospital stay of >7 days.
Healthy full-term infants were recruited at the Department
of Obstetrics, Xin Hua Hospital, during a similar period,
with the following inclusion criteria: (1) 37 ࣘgestational
age < 42 weeks; (2) vaginally born; and (3) no gut disorders,
metabolic diseases, congenital defects, or viral hepatitis. The
mothers of both the preterm and full-term infants were
tested as negative for group B Streptococcus infection and
viral diseases (human immunodeficiency virus, hepatitis)
during pregnancy. None of the mothers of the full-term
infants had received intrapartum antimicrobial prophylaxis.
All recruited infants remained free of NEC, sepsis, or
death during the sampling time. Solid foods had still not
been introduced to any of the infants by the end of the
study. The demographic, feeding, and clinical characteristics
were collected during both hospital and home stays. This
study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xin Hua
Hospital. Written informed consent for sample collection
was obtained from the patients’ parents or guardians. This
study was also registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier:
NCT03373721).
Sample Collection and DNA Extraction
Meconium (defined as the first evacuated stool within
48 hours after birth) as well as other stool samples at 7 (3–9),
14 (11–17), 21 (18–24), 42 (32–49), 70 (60–84), and 90 (80–
101) days of age were collected immediately after defecation
into a sterile tube by a nurse or parent and stored in a −20°C

freezer. The samples were placed in a cooler with ice packs
for shipment to the laboratory and stored at −80°C before
being used for DNA extraction. DNA from the fecal sam-
ples was extracted using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini
Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufac-
turer’s instructions, with the addition of a bead-beating step.
16S Ribosomal RNA Sequencing and Analysis
The V3–V4 regions of the bacterial 16S ribosomal RNA
(rRNA) gene were amplified with the barcode-indexed
primers 338F and 806R. These 2 regions of the 16S rRNA
gene were chosen because they have been widely evaluated in
previous studies,17,18 and the primers were shown to be less
biased for bacterial amplification and had a good coverage
of the bacterial groups typically found in human fecal
samples.18,19 Equimolar amounts of the purified amplicons
were paired-end sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform
(Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the standard
protocols provided by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology
Co, Ltd (Shanghai, China). All raw reads were deposited
into the National Center for Biotechnology Information
Sequence Read Archive database (accession number: PR-
JNA517572). Operational taxonomic units were clustered
with a 97% similarity cutoff using UPARSE (version 7.1),
and the chimeric sequences were identified and removed
using UCHIME. The taxonomy of each 16S rRNA gene
sequence was analyzed against the Silva (SSU128) 16S
rRNA database by the RDP Classifier algorithm, using a
confidence threshold of 70%. The α-diversity of the gut
microbiota was measured according to the Chao index,
Shannon index, and Simpson index. The functional profiles
of the microbial communities were predicted using phylo-
genetic investigation of communities by reconstruction of
unobserved states. The functional composition profiles were
demonstrated by the relative abundance of the level-2 Kyoto
Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways.
Statistical Analysis
The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare the
distribution of continuous variables, such as the relative
abundance of bacteria between the 2 infant groups. A
heat map was generated to examine the similarity of the
microbial compositions between both infant groups over
time (R package, vegan).
The random-effects generalized least square regression
model was used to examine potential factors that influ-
enced the bacterial composition and diversity over time. To
improve the normality, arcsine square root transformation
and Box-Cox power transformation were performed for the
bacterial compositions and diversity indexes, respectively.
The model was constructed separately for the preterm and
FTVD infants, in which the sex, birth weight, feeding mode,
and time point were adjusted for each group. The feeding
modes were categorized into 3 groups: breastfed (ie, breast-
fed time that accounted for ࣙ90% of the feeding time during
the 90 days of sample collection), mixed fed (breastfed time
that accounted for 10%–89% of the feeding time during the
90 days of sample collection), and formula fed (breastfed
time that accounted for <10% of the feeding time during the
90 days of sample collection). The gestational age, delivery
mode, intrapartum antimicrobial prophylaxis, PN use (7–14
or ࣙ15 days), and NICU antibiotic use (<7, 7–14, or ࣙ15
days) were considered for the preterm group only. Because
the number of days of NICU antibiotic use and probiotic
use were highly correlated (Spearman correlation coefficient
[rs] = 0.55, P < 0.0001), only the NICU antibiotic use was
incorporated into the regression model.
The succession trajectories for the predominant bacterial
groups were modeled in 2 steps. First, we determined the
estimated β coefficients for time, with time entering the
models as dummy variables. Second, we fitted the curves
of these β coefficients against time using 2-parameter expo-
nential regression or linear regression as appropriate. Unless
otherwise specified, all statistical analyses were performed
using STATA 13.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).
Results
Gut Microbial Development Over Time in the 2
Infant Groups
In total, 51 preterm infants and 50 FTVD infants providing
stool samples at >2 time points were included in the
final analysis. The demographic and clinical data of the
participants are described in Table 1. In total, 558 stool
samples (245 from the preterm group and 313 from the
FTVD group) were sequenced, and the mean values of
Good’s coverage of all samples were above 99.9%. At the
phylum level,both the meconium and fecal samples were
dominated by Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Actinobacteria,
and Bacteroidetes (>1% of the relative abundance of the
total bacterial population) in both infant groups, with differ-
ent proportions observed between the meconium and fecal
samples over the 3 months (Figure 1A). The meconium from
the preterm infants harbored significantly higher levels of
Firmicutes, but lower amounts of Cyanobacteria, compared
with those from the FTVD infants (both Padj < 0.05)
(Figure 1B, Supplementary Table S1). At the age of 90 days,
the Bacteroidetes level was significantly lower in the preterm
infants than in the FTVD infants (Padj < 0.01) (Figure 1C,
Supplementary Table S1).

terium, Klebsiella, Bacteroides, Streptococcus, and Veil-
lonella were predominant in both the preterm and FTVD
groups, with notable variability within and between the
groups over time (Figure 2A). The meconium microbiota
of the FTVD infants was enriched with Escherichia-Shigella

Difference in Gut Microbial Functions Between
the 2 Infant Groups
Comparison of the meconium samples from the preterm
and FTVD groups revealed that the relative abundance of
proteins related to 11 Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and
Genomes pathways at level 2 were notably different between
the 2 groups (q-value < 0.01). Higher levels of functions
such as amino acid metabolism and cell growth and death
and lower levels of functions like glycan biosynthesis and
metabolism were annotated for the gut microbiota from
preterm infants (Figure 5A). At day 90, the gut microbiota
from the preterm group showed increased functions like
membrane transport, cell motility, and infectious disease
but lower levels of functions responsible for the metabolism
of terpenoids and polyketides, carbohydrates, lipids, and
other amino acids (q-value < 0.01, Figure 5B).

Identification of Factors That Perturb the Gut
Microbiota in Infants
Multiple variables were adjusted with the random-effects
generalized least square regression model to analyze their
impacts on the bacterial diversity and composition in the
preterm and FTVD infants. Bacterial diversity was shown to
be positively associated with the birth weight of the FTVD
infants, as indicated by the Shannon index (P = 0.021)
(Supplementary Table S3). The level of Bifidobacterium
was highest in FTVD infants who had received mixtures
of formula and breast milk compared with those who had
been predominantly breastfed (P = 0.026) (Table 2). The
level of Streptococcus had increased positively with the
gestational age of the preterm infants (P = 0.021), whereas
that of Lactobacillus had increased with the birth weight
(P = 0.015). Unclassified Enterobacteriaceae was more
predominant in the male than in the female preterm infants
(P = 0.009). The levels of Enterococcus and Lactobacillus
had decreased significantly with the prolonged use of NICU
antibiotics. The infants with 7–14 and ࣙ15 days of NICU
antibiotic use had lower levels of Lactobacillus than that of
the preterm infants with <7 days of antibiotic use (both P
values < 0.001). Meanwhile, a prolonged duration of PN
support (ࣙ15 days) was strongly associated with a decreased
proportion of
Bifidobacterium in the preterm infants
(P = 0.021).

Conversely, in the FTVD group, Bacteroides followed the
trajectory of a linear increase over time. This bacterial genus
has been reported to be enriched in most vaginally delivered
infants but depleted in infants born by cesarean section.18
Some species of Bacteroides (eg, Bacteroides fragilis and
Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron) could mediate the immune
system by affecting the Th1/Th2 balance through their
capsular polysaccharides.18 Moreover, the Bacteroidetes can
degrade a variety of complex glycans (eg, human breast
milk), especially some Bacteroides species that contain
abundant glycosidase and lyase genes and are involved in
carbohydrate metabolism.26 Therefore, the reduced capabil-
ity of carbohydrate metabolism and immunity in preterm
infants at day 90 were likely associated with the lack of
Bacteroides during early life.
Another objective of the current study was to identify
key factors that shape early gut microbial development. Our
study found that a higher birth weight was associated with
an increased level of Lactobacillus in the preterm group
and increased bacterial diversity in the FTVD group. A low
birth weight is one of the leading risk factors for neonatal
morbidity and type 2 diabetes mellitus in adulthood.27 The
delayed colonization of commensal bacteria and the over-
abundance of Enterobacteriaceae and Enterococcus have
been observed in very low-birth-weight infants.28 A higher
level of Bifidobacterium was found in the FTVD infants
who had received mixed feeding (formula and breast milk)
than in the predominantly breastfed infants. This was likely
because there were 3 infants receiving probiotics in each
of these 2 groups, and the probiotic administration time
was longer for the mixed-fed infants than for the breastfed
babies (mean 9 days vs 5 days). The gestational age had been
reported to influence preterm bifidobacterial colonization,29
and it was strongly correlated with the increased level of
Streptococcus in the current study. Streptococcus is one of
the core members of the infant gut microbiota and normally
colonizes soon after birth.2 Whether its presence is related
to infant gut maturation needs further investigation. The
sex-related difference in the infant gut microbiota has rarely
been reported. A recent study reported that male preterm
infants were more likely to have a higher abundance of En-
terobacteriales and less of Clostridiales than female preterm
infants during the first month of life.17 We confirmed
a sex-specific bacterial difference with the finding of a
significantly higher level of Unclassified Enterobacteriaceae
in the male preterm infants during their first 3 months of
life. As the gut microbiota has been found to be involved in
sex-related autoimmune disorders and altered sex hormone
levels (by regulating testosterone production and/or utiliza-
tion) in an animal study,30 more investigations on the sex-
specific functions of the gut microbiota and their potential
associations with sex-specific diseases are needed.17
Antibiotics are the most commonly prescribed medicines
for preterm neonates in the NICU to prevent and/or reduce
neonatal infections. In our cohort, prolonged antibiotic
use in the NICU (7–14 days and ࣙ15 days vs <7 days)
was associated with decreased levels of Lactobacillus and
Enterococcus in the preterm infants. The human gut is
commonly colonized by Lactobacillus, and some members
of the lactobacilli have been used as potential probiotic
candidates in the production of fermented foods.31 Zou et al
found no Lactobacillus colonization in preterm infants with
high-intensity antibiotic exposure (>7 days).32 Prolonged
empiric antibiotic treatment (ࣙ5 days) was suggested to in-
crease the risk of NEC or late-onset sepsis in very low-birth-
weight infants.33 Significant controversy exists over the role
of Enterococcus, specifically E. faecalis, on human health.
Higher levels of enterococci have been associated with
antibiotic therapy in infants, and some strains have emerged
as multidrug-resistant and hospital-acquired pathogens.34,35
On the other hand, Enterococcus is normally present in
neonates after birth, and certain species (eg, E. faecalis) were
found to be involved in immune modulation by suppressing
the inflammatory responses of specific signaling pathways.36

PN is life saving for preterm infants with an immature
gut when adequate nutrition cannot be delivered via the
enteral route. Nevertheless, PN does cause serious adverse
effects, such as intestinal inflammation, PN-associated liver
diseases, and late-onset sepsis.13 The abundance of Pro-
teobacteria was found to be associated with prolonged
PN use in children with intestinal failure,37 and a simi-
lar PN-associated bacterial shift in an animal study was
suggested to drive the proinflammatory state and loss of
epithelial barrier function mediated by the MyD88 sig-
naling pathway.38 For the first time, to the best of our
knowledge, we have found that the duration of PN use
was associated with a decreased level of Bifidobacterium
in preterm infants. This bacterium is normally abundant
in infants, especially breastfed babies. As a saccharolytic
bacterium, Bifidobacterium produces short-chain fatty acids
and lactate, which together with the exopolysaccharides pro-
duced by certain Bifidobacterium species have been shown
to positively modulate immune function.39 High levels of
Bifidobacterium have been considered a potential microbial
biomarker for health.2 Moreover, PN is associated with a
decreased production of secretory IgA and the impaired
antimicrobial function of Paneth cells. These factors could
favor the growth of pathogenic bacteria at the expense of
beneficial commensals,13 as shown in the current study.
More research on the interaction between nutrient delivery,
the gut microbiota, and PN-associated effects may con-
tribute to enhancements in the safety and quality of life of
neonates requiring PN.
There were several limitations in the current study. First,
the study population was relatively small, and thus enlarging
the infant population would provide stronger statistical
power to investigate the impacts of multiple environmental
factors. Second, we did not determine whether different
antibiotic classes affect the microbiota differently in the long
term; this should be examined in future studies. Moreover,
although we characterized the taxonomic composition and
provided functional insights into the preterm and full-term
microbial communities by using phylogenetic investigation
of communities by reconstruction of unobserved states,
which has been proven to provide sufficient function pre-
diction for 16S rRNA datasets containing a few hundred
sequences per sample,10 it had limitations regarding the
sequencing depth and did not consider other important
parts of the gut microbiome (ie, viruses and eukaryotic
cells).40 Multiomic studies in this field would provide more
information on the functional pathways and metabolites at
the strain level.
In conclusion, our study showed that marked differences
in the gut microbial compositions and functions between
Chinese preterm and FTVD infants began from the meco-
nium, and 7 predominant bacterial groups followed specific
developmental trajectories in the first 3 months after birth.
Antibiotics and PN were identified as the critical drivers
shaping the early preterm microbiota, with the levels of
beneficial bacteria being notably reduced. Moreover, the
gestational age, sex, and birth weight also impacted specific
genera in the preterm infants. A future multicenter study
in different regions of China, incorporating the factors
found in this study, may facilitate a more sophisticated
understanding of the interaction between the early life gut
microbiota and preterm infant health.
