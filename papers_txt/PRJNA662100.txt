Abstract 
25 
 
26 
Mucosal surfaces like those present in the lung, gut, and mouth interface with distinct 
27 
external environments. These mucosal gateways are not only portals of entry for 
28 
potential pathogens but homes to microbial communities that impact host health. 
29 
Secretory immunoglobulin A (SIgA) is the single most abundant acquired immune 
30 
component secreted into mucosal surfaces and via the process of immune exclusion, 
31 
shapes the architecture of these microbiomes. Not all microorganisms at mucosal 
32 
surfaces are targeted by SIgA, therefore a better understanding of the SIgA-coated 
33 
fraction may identify the microbial constituents that stimulate host immune responses in 
34 
the context of health and disease. Chronic diseases like type 2 diabetes are associated 
35 
with altered microbial communities (dysbiosis) that in turn affect immune-mediated 
36 
homeostasis. 16S rDNA sequencing of SIgA-coated/uncoated bacteria (IgA-Biome) was 
37 
conducted on stool and saliva samples of normoglycemic participants and individuals 
38 
with prediabetes or diabetes (n=8/group). These analyses demonstrated shifts in 
39 
relative abundance in the IgA-Biome profiles between normoglycemic, prediabetic, or 
40 
diabetic samples distinct from that of the overall microbiome. Differences in IgA-Biome 
41 
alpha diversity were apparent for both stool and saliva, while overarching bacterial 
42 
community differences (beta diversity) were also observed in saliva. These data 
43 
suggest that IgA-Biome analyses can be used to identify novel microbial signatures 
44 
associated with diabetes and support the need for further studies exploring these 
45 
communities. Ultimately, an understanding of the IgA-Biome may promote development 
46 
of novel strategies to restructure the microbiome as a means of preventing or treating 
47 
diseases associated with dysbiosis at mucosal surfaces.

Introduction 
49 
Already a global epidemic, type 2 diabetes prevalence is expected to increase 
50 
precipitously in coming decades (1). Worldwide, 382 million people were living with 
51 
diabetes in 2013, but projections predict 600 million by 2035 (2). Type 2 diabetes 
52 
increases the risk of chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, stroke, neuropathy, 
53 
other diabetes complications, and certain cancers (3-11). Evidence suggests that the 
54 
composition of the gut microbiome can influence inflammation and host metabolism in a 
55 
manner consistent with what is seen at the onset of type 2 diabetes, intimating a 
56 
possible causal role of the gut microbiome in type 2 diabetes progression (12, 13). An 
57 
understanding how the gut microbiome changes across time accompanying disease 
58 
progression will provide insights into the etiology of type 2 diabetes and possibly identify 
59 
novel diagnostic strategies and therapeutics. The challenge is how to identify among the 
60 
hundred trillion (14) gut bacteria those species profiles most closely associated with 
61 
health and disease. We addressed this challenge by analyzing changes to the 
62 
immunoglobulin A (IgA) microbiome (IgA-Biome) of stool and saliva that accompany 
63 
changes in glycemia as described previously.  
64 
Secretory immunoglobulin A (SIgA) is the single most abundant acquired immune 
65 
component secreted into mucosal surfaces that shapes the architecture of mucosal 
66 
microbiomes (15, 16). Although present at all mucosal surfaces (e.g., in the gut, lungs, 
67 
mouth, eye), most SIgA is secreted into the gut (15), where it prevents direct interaction 
68 
between the host intestinal mucosa and antigens derived from food, bacteria, or other 
69 
sources (16). This process, known as immune exclusion (17-21), prevents antigen 
70 
attachment through the agglutination of SIgA-bound targets that are subsequently

removed by peristaltic and mucociliary movements (22-24). SIgA also mediates immune 
72 
inclusion, a process that involves linking commensal microbiota constituents to the gut 
73 
mucosa via glycans present on the SIgA secretory component (16).  
74 
The present study examines the IgA-Biome of the stool and saliva of 
75 
normoglycemic, prediabetic, and diabetic individuals as a means of identifying microbial 
76 
SIgA signatures associated with these diabetes phenotypes. Remarkably, while altered 
77 
serum IgA responses have been observed in those with type 2 diabetes (7, 8, 25, 26) 
78 
little is known about SIgA in the gut in the context of diabetes or hyperglycemia. An 
79 
understanding of the SIgA-coated repertoire will identify microbial constituents that 
80 
stimulate the host’s immune response in the context of either health and disease. Using 
81 
flow cytometry, we analyzed the IgA-Biome of stool and saliva using 16S rDNA 
82 
sequencing. Characterization of the fecal and salivary IgA-Biome microbial signatures 
83 
may yield diagnostic markers of diabetes and represent novel targets for the 
84 
manipulation of the microbiome to reduce chronic inflammation and reverse or prevent 
85 
diabetes progression.

We next sought to determine whether discriminant taxa identified by LEfSe 
170

171 
measures enrichment of each genus in the IgA-coated versus the uncoated fraction, 
172 
thereby accounting for inter-subject variation in taxonomic abundance variation readily 
173 
apparent in both stool and saliva (Table S3). Of those stool bacteria differentiating IgA 
174 
sorted fractions, none varied with diabetes status (Table S3). In contrast, ICI scores of 
175 
the discriminant salivary bacteria Oribacterium (p=0.035) and Selenomonas (p=0.007), 
176 
and to a lesser extent Selenomonas_3 (p=0.068), increased with dysglycemia. 
177

178 
among presort samples, which underscores the value of the IgA-Biome to inform 
179 
previously unrecognized microbial relationships in the context of type 2 diabetes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS 
181 
 
Study population and sample collection. Stool and saliva samples were 
182 
collected from age matched normoglycemic, prediabetic, and diabetic individuals (8 
183 
participants from each group) from Starr County, TX (Table 1). Subjects are 
184 
participating in a larger study designed to identify changes in the nasal and gut 
185 
microbiomes during the progression from normoglycemia to prediabetes to diabetes. 
186 
This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Texas 
187 
Health Science Center and informed written consent was obtained from each 
188 
participant. Following enrollment, saliva samples (approximately 2-3 ml) were collected 
189 
at the field office and immediately frozen at -80ºC. Approximately half of participants 
190 
were able to provide a stool sample at time of enrollment.  Stool was collected using a 
191 
Feces Catcher (Abbexa, Houston, TX), and a portion of the sample was placed in a 
192 
Covidien Precision™ Stool Collector and stored at -80ºC. Participants unable to provide 
193 
a stool sample at the field office collected samples in a similar fashion at home and 
194 
contacted a Starr County Health Studies staff member when the sample was ready for 
195 
pickup. Samples were stored at -20ºC until they were transported to the field office 
196 
where they were stored at -80ºC. Samples were shipped to the Houston laboratories on 
197 
dry ice and stored at -80ºC until processed for flow cytometry. 
198 
Diabetes classifications. Participants were defined as normoglycemic, 
199 
prediabetic, or diabetic based on their fasting glucose, 2-hour post load glucose levels, 
200 
or percent glycated hemoglobin (%HbA1c) (Table 1). “Normoglycemic” classification 
201 
required all 3 measures to be within normal range while an abnormal value on any one 
202 
test was sufficient to classify a subject as “diabetic.”

Sample preparation for flow cytometry. Stool and saliva samples were 
204 
prepared as described by Palm et al. with minor modifications (31). Stool samples were 
205 
resuspended at a concentration of 0.1 g/ml in sterile filtered, cold phosphate buffered 
206 
saline (PBS, pH 7.4). Stool samples were allowed to rehydrate for 30 min on ice, and 
207 
saliva samples were allowed to thaw at room temperature. One mL of either stool or 
208 
saliva were then added to Lysing Matrix D tubes (MP Biologicals, Santa Ana, CA) and 
209 
homogenized for 7 sec using a BeadBeater (Biospec, Bartlesville, OK). Lysing Matrix D 
210 
tubes were then centrifuged at 730 rpm for 15 min and 100 µl were collected for 
211 
immunostaining. Each sample was washed 3X with 1 ml cold PBS/1% bovine serum 
212 
albumin (BSA, Sigma, St. Louis, MO). Between washes, samples were centrifuged at 
213 
9,230 rpm. After the first wash, 20 µl were removed and frozen at -80ºC for use as the 
214 
presort sample. After the last wash, samples were blocked in 25 µl of 20% mouse 
215 
serum for 20 min on ice. Fluorophore-conjugated anti-IgA and anti-IgM antibodies were 
216 
added to respective samples at the concentrations specified by the manufacturers 
217 
(listed below) for 30 min on ice. Samples were washed 3 times in PBS/1%BSA. After 
218 
the last wash, samples were resuspended in 1 ml PBS/1% BSA and transferred to a 
219 
flow tube. All centrifugation steps were carried out in a cold room at 4ºC. Samples were 
220 
maintained on ice until sorted.

277

283

284

285

287 
mapped against an optimized version of SILVA Database v132 (35) containing only 
288

289

290 
sheath fluid samples were removed from the dataset if their average relative abundance

Statistical analysis. Agile Toolkit for Incisive Microbial Analyses 2 (ATIMA2), a 
296 
stand-alone tool for microbial data exploration, was used as an integrated solution for 
297 
analyzing and visualizing microbiome data (atima2.jplab.net/). ATIMA2 uses 
298 
PERMANOVA to evaluate differences in overarching community composition (beta 
299 
diversity), while comparisons of community dispersion were determined via the Mann-
300 
Whitney U test. Alpha diversity analyses were performed in STATA 16 using repeated 
301 
measures ANOVA and the paired t-test as appropriate. Differentially abundant taxa by 
302

303 
minimum LDA=2.0. LEfSe analysis was limited to taxa identified in at least 10% of 
304 
samples. Finally, ICI was calculated as relative abundance (IgA+) /relative abundance 
305

306 
pseudoabundance of 0.0008 (the limit of detection) was applied to the denominator. 
307 
Samples in which taxa were not detected in either fraction were excluded from analysis.

results are considered significant at p<0.05.

Discussion 
311 
Recent efforts highlight the promise of the gut and oral microbiome as targets in 
312 
the development of novel prognostic, diagnostic, and therapeutic interventions. These 
313 
microorganisms represent the bulk of the human microbiota (37) and as residents of 
314 
mucosal microenvironments they also face the additional selection pressures imposed 
315 
by that of the host’s adaptive and innate immune systems (15). In the present study, we 
316 
examined the stool and salivary microbiomes in the context of the host immune 
317 
response by characterizing the IgA-Biome, which profiles bacteria based on their SIgA-
318 
bound status. Specifically, we investigated differences in the IgA-Biome in type 2 
319 
diabetes, a chronic inflammatory disease with known alterations to both the intestinal 
320 
and oral microbiota (13, 37-41).  
321 
The mucosal immune system functions beyond simply protecting the host from 
322 
infection. It also effectively serves as a ‘bouncer,’ selecting the residents of the intestinal 
323 
microbiota as a means of maintaining gut homeostasis (16, 42, 43). To do this, it must 
324 
decide between an active inflammatory response driven by either pathogen- (e.g., 
325 
lipopolysaccharide, peptidoglycan) (44, 45) or self-derived danger signals (e.g., S100 
326 
proteins, heat shock proteins) (44-46) or a non-inflammatory maintenance response 
327 
governed by safety signals delivered by commensal bacteria and their by-products, 
328 
healthy epithelial cells, and dietary components (42). Part of this decision-making 
329 
process is established in early life, paradoxically through interactions between the 
330 
developing immune system and the gut microbiota. This process is essential to both the 
331 
development of CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells as well as to the maturation of B-cells into IgA-
332 
secreting plasma cells (43).

Unlike traditional gut and oral microbiome sequencing approaches, the IgA-
334 
Biome offers a different perspective of the microbial communities colonizing mucosal 
335 
surfaces. Specifically, it provides an understanding of host immune awareness to the 
336 
myriad targets present in these ecological niches. Although bacteria found in stool are 
337 
frequently used as a surrogate for the mucosal microbiome, data demonstrate these two 
338 
populations are distinct (47, 48). SIgA primarily targets microbes that come in contact 
339 
with host tissues, therefore the SIgA-coated fraction of the IgA-Biome is likely to be 
340 
more similar to the mucosal microbiota than the microbiota colonizing the lumen of the 
341 
intestine (49, 50). We hypothesized therefore that IgA-Biome profiles may be a better 
342 
indicator of the mucosal-associated microbiota since these organisms will, by definition, 
343 
have come in contact with the host’s immune system and are thus more likely to have 
344 
been in closer proximity to mucosal surfaces (16). The specificity of SIgA in the gut and 
345 
at other mucosal sites (e.g., the oral mucosa, lungs, eyes) dictates and shapes 
346 
microbial community architecture, topography, invasiveness profiles, and the 
347 
immunometabolome via antibody-mediated immune exclusion and inclusion (16, 49). 
348 
The IgA-Biome signature therefore provides a snapshot of the organisms that have the 
349 
attention of the host’s immune system or, in the context of disease, organisms that 
350 
should have the host’s attention but for unknown reasons do not. Therefore, further 
351 
characterizing IgA-Biome at the species level will identify the microbial constituents that 
352 
stimulate and interact with the host immune system to promote type 2 diabetes (31, 51-
353 
54). 
354

355

384 
Human studies of the gut microbiome and diabetes have largely focused on 
385 
differences between individuals of varied glycemic status. Bifidobacterium, the most 
386 
consistently identified taxa, showed an inverse relationship with hyperglycemia 
387 
(60). Other bacteria negatively associated with prediabetes or diabetes are Roseburia, 
388 
Faecalibacterium, and other Clostridiales members (61-64). Bacteria with frequent 
389 
positive associations with type 2 diabetes include Ruminococcus, Lactobacillus, and 
390 
various members of the Proteobacteria phylum (61-65). These relationships are not 
391 
surprising given that Proteobacteria may result in loss of gut barrier function, and 
392 
hyperglycemia is itself associated with intestinal permeability (66). Of these taxa, we 
393 
identified Escherichia_Shigella and Pseudomonas (Proteobacteria) to be associated 
394 
with the IgA+ and IgA- organisms, respectively, in addition to members of the Order 
395 
Clostridia (Dorea and Ruminococcus).

397

398 
associated with diabetes (67-70), while Prevotella has additionally been linked to poor 
399 
oral health and increased body mass index (55, 71). Alloprevotella, also preferentially 
400 
bound to SIgA, has by contrast been inversely associated with diabetes (58, 72). Taxa 
401 
found to be differentially coated with SIgA included Capnocytophaga, Leptotrichia, and

410 
Identifying changes to the gut IgA-Biome that can be linked to transitions across 
411 
glycemic profiles and to changes in IgA-Biome profiles at other mucosal sites i.e., the 
412 
oral IgA-Biome, may help identify subtle fluctuations associated with diabetes 
413 
phenotypes and potentially identify IgA-Biome signatures that are shared between 
414 
mucosal microenvironments. The presence of Dorea, for example, a genus associated 
415 
with gut microbiota changes in those with diabetes was identified in this study at both 
416 
anatomic sites and in both the IgA-coated (stool) and -uncoated (saliva) compartments 
417 
(62, 77). Dorea (and potentially other genera with similar profiles) may represent 
418 
candidate taxa that can be used to track changes to the SIgA response at different 
419 
anatomic sites in the context of changing glycemia profiles. 
420 
The salivary IgA-Biome may provide a different avenue for the early detection of 
421 
chronic disease states and serve as a potential alternative to the gut microbiome since 
422 
immune responses originating in the gut have implications at distant mucosal surfaces. 
423 
For example, it was demonstrated that gut dysbiosis negatively impacted immune 
424 
responses in the lung in response to a Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection (78). Due

426 
IgA-Biome data should be interpreted with caution. However, even in this limited sample 
427

428 
Future studies will employ whole genome sequencing to identify organisms at the 
429 
species level associated with changes in glycemia status in both the gut and saliva. 
430 
These analyses will allow us to also test the hypothesis that changes in the gut IgA-
431

432

433

434 
 Characterizing the IgA-Biome provides an awareness of taxa targeted by the 
435 
host immune response and, equally important, those taxa not targeted in the context of 
436 
specific disease states. Such studies can only further our understanding of disease 
437

438

439 
mucosal surfaces. Continued research of these unique signatures is greatly warranted.
