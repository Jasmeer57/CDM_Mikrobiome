Different Types of Short Bowel Syndrome: Implications

Abstract
Background: The gut microbiome and metabolome may significantly influence clinical outcomes in patients with short bowel
syndrome (SBS). The study aimed to describe specific metagenomic/metabolomics profiles of different SBS types and to identify
possible therapeutic targets. Methods: Fecal microbiome (FM), volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and bile acid (BA) spectrum
were analyzed in parenteral nutrition (PN)-dependent SBS I, SBS II, and PN-independent (non-PN) SBS patients. Results: FM in
SBS I, SBS II, and non-PN SBS shared characteristic features (depletion of beneficial anaerobes, high abundance of Lactobacilaceae
and Enterobacteriaceae). SBS I patients were characterized by the abundance of oxygen-tolerant microrganisms and depletion of
strict anaerobes. Non-PN SBS subjects showed markers of partial FM normalization. FM dysbiosis was translated into VOC and
BA profiles characteristic for each SBS cohort. A typical signature of all SBS patients comprised high saturated aldehydes and
medium-chain fatty acids and reduced short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) content. Particularly, SBS I and II exhibited low protein
metabolism intermediate (indole, p-cresol) content despite the hypothetical presence of relevant metabolism pathways. Distinctive
non-PN SBS marker was high phenol content. SBS patients’ BA fecal spectrum was enriched by chenodeoxycholic and deoxycholic
acids and depleted of lithocholic acid. Conclusions: Environmental conditions in SBS gut significantly affect FM composition and
metabolic activity. The common feature of diverse SBS subjects is the altered VOC/BA profile and the lack of important products of
microbial metabolism. Strategies oriented on the microbiome/metabolome reconstitution and targeted delivery of key compounds
may represent a promising therapeutic strategy in SBS patients. (JPEN J Parenter Enteral Nutr. 2019;00:1–14)

Clinical Relevancy Statement
Intestinal microbiome and metabolome may rank among
important factors affecting intestinal adaptation in short
bowel syndrome (SBS). We identified alterations in mic-
robiome composition as well as the lack of important
products of microbial metabolism that specifically depends
on the type of SBS (ie, endostomy or jejunocolic). Strategies
oriented toward the microbiome/metabolome reconstitu-
tion and targeted delivery of key compounds may represent
a promising therapeutic strategy in SBS patients.
Introduction
Short bowel syndrome (SBS) develops in patients who
undergo an extensive resection of small bowel and is a
most common cause of intestinal failure (IF) and long-
term parenteral nutrition (PN) dependency in adults.1
However, as in much as 50% of cases, adaptive pro-
cesses of intestinal mucosa compensate for the mal-
absorption resulting in partial or even total indepen-
dence from PN.2 Among other factors, the extent of
intestinal adaptation following resection depends on gut
microorganisms.3
The gut environment in SBS patients is altered in sev-
eral ways. First, fecal pH is lower in SBS relative to the
healthy gut (5.6 vs 6–7).4 Secondly, because of the short
length of the remaining small intestine and colon, the
intraluminal oxygen concentration may be elevated. Thirdly,
the disruption of enterohepatic circulation may result in
disturbed bile acid (BA) metabolism and a preference for
microorganisms tolerant to BAs. Finally, because of im-
paired digestion, the residual lumen of the gut is supplied
with abundant but poorly digested substrates, that is, simple
sugars, amino acids, peptides, and others. All these factors
may lead to significant alterations in the gut microbiome.
Gut microbiome is considered 1 of the most metabolically
active organs of the human body, with the production
of a wide range of metabolically active compounds 5.
Hence, alterations in the microbial profile may have signif-
icant consequences for the host physiology.6 Particularly,
metabolites like short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), BA deriva-
tives, or products of protein fermentation attract growing
attention.7-10
The aim of our study is to show how different eco-
logical conditions in the short bowel impact the micro-
biome and metabolome composition and how these changes
depend on the type of resection and level of mucosal
adaptation (ie, PN-dependent vs PN-independent SBS).
Specific aims of our study included (1) description of
gut microbiome composition, (2) identification of volatile
organic compound (VOC) profile, and (3) determination of
BA spectrum in feces and serum in all 3 groups of SBS
patients.
Material and Methods
Patients and Controls
SBS patients were recruited at 4 centers for home PN
in the Czech Republic. All patients after extensive bowel
resection (remaining short bowel <200 cm) during the
recruitment period (1 year) were assessed for eligibility.
Exclusion criteria were active cancer, IF-associated liver
disease, and refusal to participate. Thirty eligible patients
with SBS were recruited and separated into 3 groups. Two
groups with IF were defined as dependent on PN: (1)
jejunostomy (SBS I), n = 10, and (2) jejunocolic (SBS II),
n = 14. The third group comprised of jejunocolic patients
without dependency on PN (non-PN SBS; n = 5). SBS I and
SBS II patients were receiving PN since the SBS diagnosis.
The medium length of PN duration in the non-PN SBS
group was 10 (7; 15) months; the medium interval between
weaning off PN and sampling was 41 (41; 52) months.
Healthy controls (HCs; n = 40) were recruited from body
mass index–matched and age-matched individuals without
any known gastrointestinal pathology. The characteristics
of study cohorts and individual patients are reported in
Tables S1 and S2. The study was conducted in accordance
with the Helsinki declaration and Good Clinical Practice
guidelines. All patients and controls signed an informed
consent approved by the respective ethical committees at
each center.
Fecal Sample Collection and Manipulation
Stool collected at home was immediately stored at −20°C
until transported in the frozen state to the laboratory.
Samples collected at the hospital were transported on ice to
the laboratory directly, frozen, and stored at −50°C. Once
thawed, the samples were homogenized using stomacher
(BioPro, Czech Republic); 1 aliquot was used for DNA
extraction, and the rest was lyophilized.
Gut Microbiome Analysis
DNA from fecal samples was isolated by QIAmp Pow-
erFecal DNA Kit (Qiagen), and the V1-V3 region of the
bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified by polymerase
chain reaction. Sequencing analysis was performed on
MiSeq (Illumina, Hayward, CA, USA). Sequencing data
are available at the Sequence Read Archive database un-
der the accession number SRP132835 (https://www.ncbi.
nlm.nih.gov/sra/SRP132835). Raw sequences were pro-
cessed using standard bioinformatic procedures within the
QIIME 1.9.1 package.11 The data were treated as compo-
sitional (proportions of total read count in each sample,
nonrarefied), and prior to all statistical analyses were trans-
formed using centered log-ratio transformation.12 All reads
from high-throughput sequencing of 16SrRNA encoding

gene genes of all the samples were processed together. In
total, we obtained 4143.823 reads, and 1375.888 passed
the quality control. The median was 10,106 (5395; 32,591)
reads per sample. In total, we identified 4735 operational
taxonomic units (OTUs).
BA Analysis in Feces
The samples were prepared as described in Supplemen-
tary Material S3. Liquid chromatography separation was
performed using 1290 Infinity LC (Agilent Technologies)
followed by mass spectrometry using 6550 iFunnel LC-
Q-TOF-MS (Agilent Technologies) equipped with a Dual
AJS ESI probe in negative-ion mode. System control and
data acquisition were performed by Agilent MassHunter
Quadrupole Time of Flight Acquisition Software (B.06)
with Qualitative Analysis (B.07 SP2) Software. The BA
concentrations were transformed by logarithmic transfor-
mation; to avoid the problems of the logarithm of 0, we
added a constant of 0.1 to all records of the data matrix.
Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) and glycoursodeoxycholic
acid (gUDCA) profiles were removed from the analysis since
UDCA was supplemented to part of the patients per os.
BA Analysis in Serum
The sample was prepared as described in Supplementary
Material S3 and separated on a high-performance liquid
chromatography system (Dionex Ultimate 3000, Dionex
Softron GmbH, Germany) equipped with a Pinnacle DB
C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 3 μm, Restek, USA) and
appropriate guard column. BAs were detected on a triple
quadrupole mass spectrometer (TSQ Quantum Access Max
with H-ESI II probe, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) op-
erating in selected reaction monitoring mode. Data acqui-
sition was performed using XCalibur SW (V.2.2. Thermo
Fisher Scientific). Peaks in resulting chromatograms were
manually integrated, and peak areas were used to calculate
concentrations of individual BAs using respective calibra-
tion curves. The BA concentrations were transformed by
logarithmic transformation, and data were prior transfor-
mation adjusted by addition a constant of 0.1. UDCA
and gUDCA profiles were removed from the analysis, since
UDCA was supplemented to part of the patients per os.
Volatile Compounds Analysis of Feces
Volatiles fingerprinting of lyophilized fecal samples was
performed using an Agilent 7890 gas chromatograph
(Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with a multifunc-
tional autosampler Combi Pal (CTC Analytics, Switzer-
land) and coupled with a TruTOF (LECO, USA) time
of flight mass spectrometer. Volatiles were collected us-
ing solid-phase microextraction fiber with a divinylben-
zene/carboxen/polydimethylsiloxane coating from Supelco
(USA).
Data Analysis
For each data type (BAs, microbiome, VOCs), principal
component analysis (PCA) was applied to visualize the main
trends between the groups. We applied analysis of variance
with Tukey’s honest significance test for testing group pair-
wise differences. For gut microbiome, the statistical analyses
were performed on each of the 7 taxonomy levels (Phylum,
Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species, and OTUs) separately.
Where applicable, the resulting P-values were adjusted for
multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg
procedure. Results were considered significant at P ࣘ0.05
(Tukey’s honest significance test) or at false discovery rate ࣘ
0.1 (all the other statistical tests). Spearman’s rank correla-
tion coefficient and t-test of the correlation coefficients were
used to assess the correlation between the studied variables.
All analyses were performed in R, version 3.4.2.13 The inte-
grative analysis of data was performed using partial least
squares with sparse discriminant analysis using function
block.splsda(), and integrative heat map was created using
function cimDiablo of the mixOmics R package.14
Results
The Composition of Fecal Microbiome
The number of OTUs and Shannon index were significantly
lower in SBS I and SBS II patients but not in the non-PN
SBS group compared with HC (Table S4). The main phyla
contributing to the microbiome in all groups were Bac-
teroidetes, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria. In HCs and non-
PN SBS patients, the median Bacteroidetes to Firmicutes
(B/F) ratio was 0.7 and 1.0, respectively. In both SBS I and
SBS II patients, Bacteriodetes was significantly depleted,
resulting in the B/F ratio of 0.04 and 0.16, respectively. The
third most abundant phylum, Proteobacteria, contributed
to total microbiome by 3 ± 0.7% in the HC group, but its
representation was higher in the non-PN SBS group (11 ±
1.5%; P = 0.103), in the SBS II group (29 ± 4.2%; P =
0.011), and in the SBS I cohort (33 ± 6.9%; P = 9.1E-05).
β-Diversity is visualized in a PCA plot (Figure 1A).
The main observed effect was the distinct separation of
microbial communities in HC and SBS I + SBS II sam-

according to individual SBS cohorts. Non-PN SBS samples
did not form a separate cluster, but the differential abun-
dance analysis showed significant quantitative differences
among the groups. Figure 1B and C show SBS group-
specific patterns of fold changes to HCs based on all
bacterial families with significantly different representation
among at least 2 groups and abundance ˃1%. Compared
with HC, the common feature shared by all SBS cohorts
was the significantly higher abundance of Lactobacillaceae
and Enterobacteriaceae and depletion of Ruminococcaceae.

VOC Profile in Feces
We identified 307 different analytes in the fecal samples.
Among all VOCs identified, none were unique for any
group, but the concentration of 156 analytes differed signif-
icantly among at least 2 of the groups. Within this subset,
98 VOCs were of very low (˂0.1%) abundance, 44 VOCs
represented 0.1%–1% of the total, 10 VOCs belonged to the
abundant compounds (1%–5%), and 4 were highly abundant
(˃5%). PCA scores plot (Figure 2A) shows 2 distinctly
separated clusters, SBS I cluster with SBS II and HC cluster
with non-PN SBS. The distribution pattern of abundant
(˃1%) metabolites with significantly different representation
among at least 2 groups is shown in Figure 2B and C. Two
saturated aldehydes (hexanal, nonanal) were significantly
higher in all SBS groups compared with controls. Ten com-
pounds significantly contributed to the separation of SBS I
and SBS II groups from non-PN SBS and HCs. Saturated
aldehyde octanal, medium-chain fatty acids (decanoic and
dodecanoic acid) and heteroaromatic compound 2-pentyl
furane were higher in both SBS I and SBS II groups, whereas
products of amino acid metabolism (p-cresol and indole),
SCFAs (butanoic and pentanoic acids), fatty alcohol (1-
nonanol), geranyl acetone, and γ -undecalactone were lower.
SBS I and SBS II differed in indole and γ -undecalactone
content. Elevated phenol fecal content was specific for non-
PN SBS patients. The statistics are given in Table S6, and
the box plots showing the abundance of abundant VOCs are
shown in Figure S2.
BA Profile in Feces
The total amount of BAs in feces was significantly higher
in all SBS patients (Table S7). According to PCA, BA
profiles of different cohorts separated into different clusters,
with the exception of SBS I vs SBS II, which partially
overlapped (Figure 3A). Figure 3B and C shows that
each SBS cohort follows its own distribution pattern and
quantitative contribution of individual BAs, all of them
being different from HCs. The common marker of all SBS
groups is significantly elevated chenodeoxycholic (CDCA)
and deoxycholic (DCA) acid content in feces. Cholic acid
(CA) content tends to be higher as well, but this trend
reached statistical significance only in the non-PN SBS
group. Lithocholic acid (LCA) content is significantly lower
in SBS I and SBS II patients, and a similar trend was
observed in the non-PN SBS cohort. The statistics are given
in Table S8, and the box plots showing the abundance of
abundant VOCs are shown in Figure S3.
BA Profile in Serum
PCA showed that HC samples and all SBS patients sep-
arated into 2 independent clusters (Figure 4A), but there
were no significant differences in individual BA distribution
patterns among SBS groups (Figure 4B and C). Virtual
absence of DCA and its conjugates, as well as elevated CA,
glycocholic acid, and CDCA serum content, is the dominant
characteristic of all SBS cohorts. Higher glycochenodeoxy-
cholic acid concentration was observed in SBS I and SBS
II patients but not in the non-PN SBS group. DCA and its
glycin- and taurine-conjugate concentrations were under the
detection limit in all SBS groups. The statistics are given
in Table S9, and the box plots showing the abundance of
abundant VOCs are shown in Figure S4.

Metabolome Data
The OTUs, VOCs, BA feces, and BA serum data were
compiled into a single database and processed using mul-
tivariate analysis. The heat map of all these data allows
for visualizing the hierarchical clustering of individual
patients’ samples according to the common characteristics
(Figure 5). All HC subjects fall into 1 cluster, characterized
by the specific combination of microbiome, VOC, and BA
profiles (low saturated aldehydes, medium-chain fatty acids,
fecal DCA, fecal CDCA, serum CA, serum CDCA, serum
DCA, Lactobacillaceae, Enterobacteriaceae, Fusobacteri-

Rikenellaceae, Odoribacteraceae, Lachnospiraceae, and Ru-

further tend to aggregate into 2 clusters, first comprising of

correlations between variables identified as significantly
different between at least 2 groups. Some bacterial families
(ie, Enterobacteriaceae, Lactobacillaceae, Streptococcaceae,

Bacteroidaceae, Prevotellaceae, and Barnesiellace) correlate
positively with HCs and negatively correlate with SBS

relations follow a similar pattern as VOCs (Figure 7A
and B).

shared some characteristic features. The OTU number and
α-diversity were comparably reduced in both groups. PCA
clearly distinguished between SBS patients and HCs, but
no specific grouping occurred for the SBS I versus SBS II

genus Lactobacillus and potential pathogens from the En-

of Firmicutes or Bacteroidetes were significantly underrep-
resented. These data are in accordance with previously
published results.3,15-17 Nevertheless, we identified some
characteristics that distinguish SBS I patients from the SBS
II group, including high abundance of facultative anaerobes
from Streptococcaceae and Staphylococcaceae families and
severe depletion of obligate anaerobe Bacteroides spp. in
SBS I. We hypothesize that the underlying cause of the ob-
served phenomena is the higher oxygen tension in residual
gut of SBS I compared with SBS II subjects.
The gut microbiome of SBS patients without IF still
share several important features with PN-dependent SBS
phenotypes, including overrepresentation of Lactobacil-
laceae or Enterobacteriaceae or Fusobacteriaceae. On the
other hand, in some aspects it resembles the HC micro-
biome, that is, α diversity normalization and an increasing
tendency in Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, or Bac-
teroidaceae representation. Based on our data, we cannot
conclude which microbiome is “healthier,” as we cannot
decide whether particular microbiome composition really
causes disease or dysfunction in the host or is just an adapta-
tion to different physiological conditions. Nevertheless, our
data show that the successful intestinal adaptation is at least
associated with the microbial composition closer to HCs.
Metabolome
The human microbiome is characterized by a significant
degree of functional redundancy. Therefore, not only the
composition but also the functional capacity of the in-
testinal microbiome is highly important regarding the final
metabolome profile.5
Recent research indicates that fecal VOC profiles in
healthy subjects and in those suffering from specific gas-
trointestinal diseases differ and have the potential to serve
as a noninvasive biomarker.18 In our study, fecal concen-
tration of 156 VOCs was significantly different between
SBS patients and HCs, but only 14 of them contributed
by ˃1% to the total profile. These abundant VOCs be-
long to long-/medium-chain aldehydes, medium-chain fatty
acids/SCFAs, fatty alcohols, acyclic monoterpenoids, and
amino acid–derived bacterial metabolites.
Saturated aldehydes are formed by lipid peroxida-
tion, and their accumulation is associated with oxidative
stress.19,20 In vitro, these compounds may also be produced
by Lactobacillus helveticus21 and Lactobacillus paracasei.22
We found that the fecal content is significantly higher in SBS
patients and corresponds with the observed dominance of
Lactobacillaceae. The presence of these compounds in feces
may serve as both a marker of Lactobacillus abundance as
well as a general marker of oxidative stress in the gut.
Indole is a product of microbial tryptophan metabolism
recognized by intestinal epithelial cells and used to
strengthen host cell–barrier properties and maintain con-
trolled inflammation.23 Several microbial metabolites de-
rived from tryptophan, including indole, act as ligands
of aryl hydrocarbon receptor, which is a critical regula-
tor of immunity and inflammation.24 Activation of aryl
hydrocarbon receptor stimulates interleukin-22 transcrip-
tion the gut, which in turn regulates the release of an-
timicrobial peptides and affects the homeostatic balance
between immunity and the microbiota by regulating micro-
bial composition.25-27 Furthermore, indole can weaken the
invasion and colonization capabilities of enteric bacteria
by reducing the expression of Salmonella Pathogenicity
Island-1 genes, which facilitate bacterial invasion into host
cell28 and strengthen the intestinal barrier by inducing the
expression of tight junction proteins through the pregnane
X receptor.23,29,30 In inflammatory cells, indole can suppress
the activation of NF-kB and proinflammatory chemokine
production and simultaneously increase anti-inflammatory
cytokine production.31 We hypothesize that its shortage may
be 1 of the factors contributing to unfavorable conditions
like the compromised intestinal permeability and enhanced
translocation of bacterial toxins in the remaining intestine
in SBS patients. Indole supplementation thus may represent
a therapeutic mean for regulation of intestinal inflammation
and promotion of epithelial cell function.
SCFAs, particularly butanoic acid, belong to the most
important metabolites produced by strictly anaerobic
Firmicutes in the cecum and colon.32 The most im-
portant butanoic acid producers belong to Ruminococ-
caceae and Lachnospiraceae families, and the dysbiosis of
SCFA producers is associated with several gastrointestinal
pathologies.33 Our data show a severely reduced amount
of fecal SCFA in SBS I patients. Similar shortage was
found in SBS II patients, regardless of the presence of
a part of the colon. This observation is in accordance
with observed underrepresentation of Ruminococcaceae
and Lachnospiraceae.
Bile Acids
Transformation of primary BAs in the intestine solely
depends on gut microbiome activity. Only 1 study34 ad-
dressed the fecal BA composition in human SBS patients.
In children with SBS and intractable diarrhea, the total
BA content in feces was >10 times higher than in the
control group, with primary BAs accounting for >95%.

13
In a piglet SBS model, the disturbance of the BA profile
with an increase in the proportion of primary BAs and a
decrease in secondary BAs was observed, and these changes
were associated with the loss of bacteria belonging to
the Clostridiales.35 We confirmed altered representation of
individual BAs in feces of SBS patients, though we did
not find an unambiguous enrichment of primary BAs at
the expense of their secondary metabolites. We found the
elevated content of CDCA and its glycine and taurine
conjugates, but CA and glycocholic CA content was similar
in SBS patients and controls. Regarding secondary BAs,
all SBS patients exhibited significantly reduced fecal LCA
content (SBS I = SBS II ˂ non-PN SBS) but elevated
amounts of DCA. This observation could be explained
by a specific block in LCA synthesis. LCA is a product
of CDCA biotransformation, whereas DCA is synthesized
from CA. The accumulation of LCA precursor (CDCA)
and a normal level of DCA precursor (CA) support this
hypothesis. Up to now, only a few bacterial species capable
of 7α-dehydroxylation of primary BAs were reported, all
of them belonging to Clostridium and Eubacterium.9 We
confirmed underrepresentation of Clostridium in SBS I
and SBS II patients but not in the non-PN SBS group,
which corresponds to the amount of LCA in respective
groups. We have no explanation based on our results for
the observed elevation of DCA content in SBS patients, but
we can speculate that some bacterial strains prefer CA over
CDCA in 7α-dehydroxylation. The physiological concen-
tration of secondary BAs is important for maintaining an
anti-inflammatory status in the intestine.36 UDCA has well-
established anti-inflammatory functions and is often used
in the treatment of inflammatory bowel diseases. Ward et
al showed that the therapeutic effect of UDCA depends on
its microbial transformation to its first metabolite, LCA,
in the colon.36 We could hypothesize that a lack of LCA
content in the gut lumen of the patients contributes to the
proinflammatory status associated with the SBS syndrome.
Limitations
Our results are limited by several factors, the most impor-
tant being sample selection. SBS with IF is a rare diagnosis.
Its incidence in the Czech Republic is about 14 patients per
million per year.37 We were able to recruit 30 patients, po-
tential candidates for home PN, over a 1-year period, which
we supposed to be representative at the nationwide scale.
The samples showed high variability, which is an inherent
bias of such a type of clinical research. We were unable to
enroll any patient with jejuno-ileo-colic anastomosis (SBS
type III). These patients are less common37; in the Czech
Republic they account for only 6.6% of all SBS patients
receiving home PN,38 so we could not include them in the
study. Another limitation of our study is the absence of
oral food intake records as the nutrients given per os may
differ among patients. Composition of oral/enteral diet is
a major factor influencing the intestinal microbiome, yet it
can vary considerably among patients with SBS. Moreover,
we can speculate that in SBS, residual lumen of the gut
is supplied with abundant but poorly digested substrates,
including simple sugars, amino acids, and proteins. Unfor-
tunately, based on our data records, we cannot comment on
dietary habits of patients enrolled to the current study. How
protein-caloric intake in SBS patients relates to microbiome
composition clearly deserves further investigation.
Conclusion
In conclusion, new findings presented in this study could be
summarized as follows. First, microbiome composition in
SBS type I and SBS type II patients with IF share several
important features, regardless of the presence of colon. Sec-
ond, dysbiosis in SBS is associated with altered metabolome,
particularly with SCFA, indole, and LCA shortage. Third,
microbiome and VOC profile in SBS patients without IF is
similar to HCs, but alterations in BA metabolism persist.
Even though the design of the study is purely observational
and we cannot draw any conclusions about the causal role
of identified predictors of intestinal adaptation, our data
suggest that this process could be influenced by the com-
position of gut microbiome. Furthermore, the therapeutic
approaches focused on the microbiome/metabolome recon-
stitution in SBS, that is, fecal transplantation, prebiotic
supplementation, or targeted delivery of microbial products
into the intestine may support the adaptation process or at
least relieve the dependence on PN.
