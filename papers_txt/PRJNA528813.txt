children’s gut microbiota in relation to diet and body composition

ABSTRACT
Background: Gut microbiota data obtained by DNA sequencing
are complex and compositional because of large numbers of
detectable taxa, and because microbiota characteristics are described
in relative terms. Nutrition researchers use principal component
analysis (PCA) to derive dietary patterns from food data. Although
compositional PCA methods are not commonly used to describe
patterns from complex microbiota data, this approach would be
useful for identifying gut microbiota patterns associated with diet and
body composition.
Objectives: To use compositional PCA to describe the principal
components (PCs) of gut microbiota in 5-y-old children and explore
associations between microbiota components, diet, and BMI z-score.
Methods: A fecal sample was provided by 319 children aged 5 y.
Their primary caregiver completed a validated 123-item quantitative
FFQ. Body composition was determined using DXA, and a BMI z-
score was calculated. Compositional PCA identified characterizing
taxa and weightings for calculation of gut microbiota PC scores at
the genus level, and was examined in relation to diet and body size.
Results: Three gut microbiota PCs were found. PC1 (negative
loadings on uncultured Christensenellaceae and Ruminococcaceae)
was related to lower BMI z-scores and longer duration of breast-
feeding (per month) (β = −0.14; 95% CI: −0.26, −0.02; and β =
0.02; 95% CI: 0.003, 0.34, respectively). PC2 (positive loadings
on Fusicatenibacter and Bifidobacterium; negative loadings on
Bacteroides) was associated with a lower intake of nuts, seeds, and
legumes (β = −0.05 per gram; 95% CI: −0.09, −0.01). When
adjusted for fiber intake, PC2 was also associated with higher BMI
z-scores (β = 0.12; 95% CI: 0.01, 0.24). PC3 (positive loadings on
Faecalibacterium, Eubacterium, and Roseburia) was associated with
higher intakes of fiber (β = 0.02 per gram; 95% CI: 0.003, 0.04) and
total nonstarch polysaccharides (β = 0.02 per gram; 95% CI: 0.003,
0.04).
Conclusions: Our results suggest that specific gut microbiota com-
ponents determined using compositional PCA are associated with
diet and BMI z-score. This trial was registered at clinicaltrials.gov
as NCT00892983.

Introduction
Interest in the role the human gut microbiota plays in
nutritional health is growing (1, 2), with dysbiosis (imbalance)
of the gut microbiota community linked to obesity in adults (3,
4). However, less is known about relations with childhood obesity
(5). Although it is generally considered that an adult-like, stable,
and more diverse gut microbiota is attained by ∼3 y of age
(6–9), further changes appear to occur during childhood (10).

Associations between the gut microbiota and health outcomes,
particularly obesity, could therefore be different in growing
children than in adults.
In recent years the structure and statistical analysis of gut
microbiota data obtained from DNA sequences have come under
scrutiny. Due to the nature of sequencing technology these
data are commonly expressed as relative abundance, which is
the abundance of each taxon relative to the total abundance,
expressed as a percentage or proportion. These data are therefore
compositional because the bacterial taxa are codependent (i.e.,
if the percentage of 1 bacterial taxon increases, the percentage
of at least 1 other bacterial taxon has to decrease because by
definition the sum must always be 100%). Traditional methods
of statistical analysis, such as regression modeling, are not
appropriate for such data because compositional data violate their
assumptions, in particular the assumption that variables are not
colinear (11–14).
Nutrition researchers commonly use principal component
analysis (PCA) to determine dietary patterns in an attempt to
describe the complexity of human diets, rather than investigating
single foods or nutrients (15). PCA is a dimension reduction
technique that uses a linear combination of all inputted variables.
With dietary pattern analyses, all participants receive a “score”
for each pattern indicating how strongly they adhere to that
pattern. In infants, we have previously identified a “vegetables
and meat” pattern characterized by higher intakes of “vegeta-
bles,” “meat, eggs, and beans,” and “fruit,” and lower intakes
of “baby and toddler foods” (16). Although PCA has been used
to investigate gut microbiota configuration, the compositional
nature of these data has not always been appropriately handled
(14), and the taxa characterizing each principal component
(PC) are not generally stated. Instead, results of PCA are
commonly interpreted by visualizing the variables (e.g., relative
abundance of genera) in the same space using a biplot (17),
without calculating scores for the components to undertake
further statistical analyses. This could be a missed opportunity
to discover whether different combinations of bacterial taxa are
associated with diet or body composition.
The aim of this study was to use compositional PCA to de-
scribe gut microbiota in children aged 5 y and to explore relations
between gut microbiota PCs and diet, BMI (kg/m2), and body
composition. Secondary objectives included investigation of the
relations between diet, BMI z-score, and body composition with
alpha diversity (Shannon index) and the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes
(F/B) ratio.
Methods
Study design
The study described is a cross-sectional analysis that used gut
microbiota, dietary, anthropometric, and body composition data
collected at 5 y of age from a randomized controlled trial (RCT),
the Prevention of Overweight in Infancy (POI) study (18, 19).
Participants
Because protocol (18, 19) and main outcome (20, 21) articles
have been published, only relevant information is provided in
brief here. POI was a 4-arm RCT undertaken in Dunedin, New
Zealand, from 2009 to 2015. The study aimed to determine
whether additional education and support for parents regarding
infant sleep (sleep group); food, activity, and breastfeeding (FAB
group); or both (combination group), reduced excessive weight
gain compared with usual care (control group) at the end of the
intervention (2 y of age) and after 3 y of follow-up (5 y of age).
All mothers who had booked into the only maternity hospital
in Dunedin, New Zealand (>97% of all births), were invited to
participate in mid to late pregnancy. Healthy, full-term infants
were eligible, and 802 women agreed to participate (58% of those
invited). At 5 y, participant retention was 69% (n = 557). Of
these, 324 participants provided fecal samples, of whom 319 also
provided complete dietary data and so were investigated in this
study. The participant flowchart can be found in Supplemental
Figure 1.
The New Zealand Lower South Regional Ethics Committee
(LRS/08/12/063) and University of Otago Human Ethics Com-
mittee (12/274) approved the study, and written informed consent
was obtained from all adult participants before randomization and
before commencing the first follow-up visit at 3.5 y of age.
Information on infant sex, birth weight, mode of delivery,
parity, level of household deprivation (22), self-reported maternal
prepregnancy height and weight, maternal education, and age
were obtained from baseline questionnaires in pregnancy, and at
birth.
Gut microbiota data
Parents were asked to collect ∼5 g of fecal sample from
the child participant at 5 y of age. The sample was stored in
the home freezer in study-provided freezer containers before
collection by the study team. Samples were stored at −80◦C until
DNA extraction. Fecal bacterial DNA was extracted from 250
mg feces according to the manufacturer’s instructions (12,855–
100 PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit; Mo Bio). Amplification
of the 16S rRNA gene V4 region, library preparation, and
sequencing were carried out at Argonne National Laboratories
(University of Chicago) using 2 × 250 base paired-end reads on
an Illumina MiSeq instrument. After quality filtering, sequences
were analyzed using a combination of QIIME version 1.9.1 (23)
and VSEARCH version 1.9.5 (24). Sequences were clustered into
operational taxonomic units (OTUs) by using closed-reference
OTU picking against the SILVA version 123 database (25) using
97% identity threshold. Relative abundance at the genus level was
calculated by the summation of the raw OTU table based on the
7-level taxonomy strings from the SILVA version 123 database.
Alpha diversity for both richness and evenness was described
using the Shannon index. The DNA sequence data have been
deposited in the NCBI SRA database under BioProject accession
number PRJNA528813.
Dietary data
Early dietary data on weeks of “exclusive” breastfeeding [i.e.,
no other liquids or solids since birth (26)] and “any” breastfeeding
were determined from feeding questionnaires completed by the
mothers every 4 wk from when the infant was aged 3 to 27 wk.
This allowed the determination of breastfeeding status and age at

Body composition, BMI, and fat mass index z-score data
At the 5-y time point, child height and weight were measured
by trained researchers following the WHO protocols (28).
BMI-for-age z-scores were calculated using the WHO growth
standards (29), with overweight defined as a BMI z-score ≥1.036
to <1.645, and obesity as ≥1.645 (cutoffs correspond to the
85th and 95th percentiles of the study population). Fat mass
and percentage of body fat were assessed by DXA. All DXA
measurements were performed and analyzed by 1 experienced
operator with a Lunar Prodigy scanner (software package 16.0;
Lunar) using standard procedures (30). Fat mass index (FMI)
was calculated as fat mass divided by height in meters squared.
The FMI z-scores were then calculated for the study sample
(31).
Statistical analysis
Data were analyzed using Stata software (Stata Statistical
Software: Release 13; StataCorp LP). Figure 1 was generated us-
ing GraphPad Prism (version 8; GraphPad Software). Statistical
significance was indicated by P < 0.05, 2-sided for tests where
this is an option.
Student t tests (assuming equal variance) and chi-square tests
were used to compare baseline variables between those who
provided complete microbiota and dietary data, and those who
did not (Table 1).
To generate the gut microbiota PC scores, compositional PCA
was used to determine the weightings given to each taxon in the
calculation of the scores. These weightings are referred to as the
loadings. There were 364 genera in the dataset and many of these
had very low prevalence. To reduce the number of variables in
the PCA, only those that were present in ≥75% of the participants
were analyzed, resulting in 167 genera from the initial 364 genera
for the 319 participants. The median (25th, 75th percentile)
percentage of counts removed in this step was 0.09% (0.03%,
0.3%). Because gut microbiota data are compositional in nature
(11–14), centered log-ratios of each genus-level OTU to the
geometric mean of all genus-level OTUs were used. Because this
transformation does not work for zero values, zeros were replaced
using the geometric Bayesian multiplicative method on actual
counts in the zCompositions R package (32, 33). Sensitivity
analyses of this zero-replacement method were carried out by
comparing scree plots, characterizing taxa, loadings, and PC
scores with the simpler method of adding a set value to all
genus-level OTU counts (we used values ranging from 0.2 to
2). PCA of the centered log-ratios was carried out, and a scree
plot of eigenvalues was examined (Supplemental Figure 2) to
determine how many components were most strongly indicated
(3 were indicated). Orthogonal rotation of the components was
undertaken. After this, each genus-level OTU had a “loading” for
each component—simply, the loading represents the correlation
with the component so that high loadings identify the variables
that characterize the component. We described the 3 components
with the centered log-ratio variables of the top 10 highest loading
genera (positive or negative). The centered log-ratios describe
the composition in terms of a genus relative to the geometric
mean of all the genera in the sample. The centered log-ratios with
the highest loadings were considered to identify the genera that
characterized the patterns found using PCA, which were used in
the further analysis.
Scores for the 3 PCs were calculated by using the loadings
for the (centered log-ratios of the) genera to create weighted
scores of the original relative abundance measures (before zero-
replacement). Every child participant was given a score for each
of the 3 gut microbiota PCs. All 167 genera were used in the
calculation of each gut microbiota PC, which is the sum of
the loadings for each variable (genus) multiplied by the relative
abundance of that genus. Hence, a child participant’s score
for PC1 = (their loading of genus 1 on PC1 × their relative
abundance of genus 1) + (their loading of genus 2 on PC1 × their
relative abundance of genus 2) + … + (their loading of genus
167 on PC1 × their relative abundance of genus 167). Similar
calculations were done for PC2 and PC3, enabling scores for all
3 PCs for every child. PC scores were standardized, resulting in
the mean and SD for each PC being 0 and 1, respectively. All PC
scores were consistent with a normal distribution.
To describe the characterizing genera for each PC, the median
relative abundance of the genera that had the top 10 largest
absolute loadings along each PC for the corresponding centered
log-ratio variables (positive or negative) for all (n = 319)
participants was calculated and is displayed in Figure 1.

associations between diet and body composition variables
and gut microbiota components (Table 2). Variables with the

were found to be confounders (P < 0.25) (34); the third model
(data reported in the text only) included both BMI z-score
with each of the dietary components to investigate whether
any associations with the gut microbiota components were
influenced by including dietary component variables in the
model and vice versa. All models were adjusted for the 4 POI

Discussion
Our study identified 3 gut microbiota PCs in 5-y-old New
Zealand children: 1 associated with BMI z-score (PC1); 1
associated with BMI z-score and 1 aspect of diet (PC2); and
1 associated with several aspects of dietary intake, particularly
those relating to fiber (PC3). The use of compositional PCA
enabled the specific bacterial taxa characterizing each component
to be identified, and the strength of their contribution to the PC to
be used in the calculation of PC scores. In this rapidly growing
area, we advocate for a statistical approach that nutritionists
are familiar with: the use of PCA to describe patterns in
complex dietary data. Although compositional PCA has been
used previously (14), we propose an approach to determining
gut microbiota components analogous to how dietary patterns
are derived while also accounting for the compositional nature

of relative abundance data. To the best of our knowledge, our
analysis of PCs in relation to diet and body composition has not
been previously examined. We propose 3 key advantages to this
approach.
The first key advantage is the provision of meaningful
summaries from large datasets that are sparse and complex in
their correlation structure and require summation (36). Because
gut microbiota data are compositional (12–14), a change in
abundance of 1 bacterial taxon influences the relative abundance
of other taxa. Such data violate the assumptions of traditional
statistical methods. To be able to use such methods, log-ratio
transformations must be performed. Centered log-ratios are an
appropriate transformation for PCA because the vertices are no
longer constrained to a constant summation but still present the
proportion of the taxon in relation to the mean composition.
When this transformation has taken place, PCA can be under-
taken as usual and loadings for each component determined.
The resultant PC scores produce convenient, noncompositional
summary variables for gut microbiota.
The second key advantage is that assigning every child
a score for each component allows every child to be con-
sidered in each analysis, compared with methods such as
enterotyping, which assign a child to a single enterotype
thereby limiting assessment to group comparisons. Using
continuous PC variables allows assessment of relations with
other variables across all participants, which increases statistical
power and means that relations are being examined across
the same participants rather than in different groups of people
(37).
The third key advantage is that our statistical approach was
able to demonstrate associations between gut microbiota PCs
and diet and BMI z-score. PC1 was negatively associated with
BMI z-score even after adjustment for dietary variables, and
PC2 was positively associated with BMI z-score after adjustment
for fiber intake. This suggests that higher BMI z-scores were
associated with lower relative abundance of Bacteroides and
higher relative abundance of uncultured Christensenellaceae and
Ruminococcaceae. Existing data examining these relations are
mixed (38). Earlier work in children (39–41) has indicated
that BMI z-scores are negatively associated with Bacteroides.
Christensenellaceae has been associated with leanness in adults
(42), but not in infants (43).
PC3 was positively associated with a number of dietary
components (fiber, total NSP, and “meat, fish, poultry” intake),
independent of BMI z-score. Therefore, a higher intake of these
dietary components is associated with more Faecalibacterium,
Eubacterium, and Roseburia, independent of body weight. These
3 genera are butyrate-producing bacteria (44), with butyrate being
1 of the 3 main SCFAs produced by the gut microbiota. Butyrate
from microbial sources is considered important for host colonic
health because it acts as an energy source for the epithelial
cells and has anti-inflammatory properties (45). The positive
association with fiber and total NSP can probably be attributed
to Faecalibacterium, Eubacterium, and Roseburia being genera
that metabolize dietary plant polysaccharides (46, 47).
There was a weak negative association between alpha diversity,
as quantified by the Shannon index, and intake of “lower fiber,
more healthy cereals” (which consists of food items such as

−0.01, −0.00), and because food groups are defined differently
in different studies, direct comparisons cannot be made with the
literature. However, assuming that a higher intake of “lower fiber,
more healthy cereals” (associated in our data with lower alpha
diversity) is associated with lower fiber intake in the diet overall,
our finding agrees with other studies where lower fiber intake has
been associated with lower alpha diversity (48, 49). However,
our study does not support the relation between the commonly
examined F/B ratio and BMI z-score. This reflects the findings of
a recent meta-analysis that reported no significant difference in
the F/B ratio between obese and normal weight individuals (50),
but conflicts with some earlier work in children (51).
The proportion of variance explained by the 3 components is
low (22%), suggesting that although this method of summarizing
microbiota data into “patterns” is a convenient way to summarize
communities of taxa to explore relations with other variables,
these patterns do not explain a large majority of the variation seen
in gut microbiota data. This is a limitation of this data-driven
method, and although our sample size (n = 319) is larger than
most in this field (36) and is considered “good” for PCA, we have
<2 observations per variable (n = 167), which is considered low
(52).
Strengths of the study include the use of an FFQ that has
been validated for the nutrients and food groups of interest
to the gut microbiota and the age group studied. In addition,
the use of compositional analyses for the PCA reflects recent
developments in statistical approaches to this type of data.
There are 2 potential limitations of the compositional approach
in this context. First, because the microbiota data sat in a
different sample space to the rest of the data before the
compositional transformation, there is the possibility of spurious
results. Second, the large number of zeros in the dataset (mean
percentage of zeros = 37 ± 9%) undermines the compositional
approach to some degree, although we undertook sensitivity
analyses for the zero-replacement. Inferences from this study are
limited by the nonrepresentative nature of the sample (5-y-old
New Zealand children from higher socioeconomic families were
overrepresented so caution could be needed when generalizing to
less advantaged families). In addition, a small number of potential
confounders were investigated. Hence, we cannot rule out other
potential confounding, including suppression effects, explaining
our results. Because we used 16S rRNA gene sequencing,
definitive species identification and the functionality of the gut
microbiota present were not determined.
Our study highlights that the gut microbiota in 5-y-old
children can be described in terms of 3 different PCs, which are
associated with diet and BMI z-score. Because our current data
demonstrated that the proportion of variance explained by the PCs
is low, we anticipate that there are other factors in relation to the
components that we have not been able to identify. We encourage
fellow researchers to use compositional PCA as a method for
identifying further links between the gut, diet, and obesity, and
for developing the next generation of research in which the impact
on body composition of dietary interventions that modify the gut
microbiota is determined.
