besity and metabolic syndrome comprise one of the greatest 
health epidemics of the 21st century1–3, with current medi-
cal strategies having limited efficacy, poor tolerance and 
high cost4–8. Continued lack of progress in curbing this epidemic has 
diverted growing interest into new approaches such as fecal micro-
bial transplantation (FMT), which targets the gut microbiome9–14. 
Recent landmark studies in which patients with metabolic syn-
drome received FMT from lean healthy donors have indeed demon-
strated metabolic improvements, but effects have been inconsistent 
and short term, possibly due to progressive loss of donor microbe 
engraftment15–17. Although promising, only one research group has 
demonstrated these benefits to date, however, these studies were 
performed solely in male participants with mild metabolic dysfunc-
tion and used invasive FMT delivery techniques18–21.
To establish FMT as a pragmatic therapy for obesity and metabolic 
syndrome, novel strategies that enhance efficacy using non-invasive 
delivery methods in both male and female patients with a diverse 
spectrum of metabolic dysfunction are needed22–26. The concept of 
using dietary fiber supplementation to maintain healthy microbial 
communities introduced by FMT is one strategy that has been pro-
posed, but no human trials have examined this concept to date27–30. 
The aim of our work was thus to evaluate oral encapsulated FMT 
with adjunctive fiber supplementation in a representative North 
American population with severe obesity and metabolic syndrome, 
the most rapidly escalating form of obesity worldwide31.
Dietary fibers are non-digestible carbohydrates that exert 
a variety of health benefits dependent on their physicochemi-
cal properties32. High-fermentable (HF) fibers, for example, are 
readily fermented by select microbial strains and produce ben-
eficial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), while 
low-fermentable (LF) fibers are largely resistant to gut microbial 
fermentation and can act through fecal bulking and the modula-
tion of gastrointestinal transit33. Together, these diverse properties 
provide opportunities with which to influence engraftment of an 
FMT and microbiome assembly in the recipient to enhance meta-
bolic benefits34.
We propose that orally administered FMT would alter recipient 
microbial ecology, thereby improving insulin sensitivity, and that 
dietary fiber supplementation would enhance or maintain these 
effects. The primary outcome was the evaluation of changes in insu-
lin sensitivity between baseline and after 6 weeks of treatment using 
the homeostatic model assessment (HOMA2-IR/IS). Secondary 
outcomes included evaluation of the safety and tolerability in addi-
tion to changes in anthropometrics, glycemic markers, lipid profile, 
quality of life measures, dietary intake, serum inflammatory mark-
ers, fecal SCFAs and fecal microbiota composition.
Fecal microbial transplantation and fiber 
supplementation in patients with severe 
obesity and metabolic syndrome: a randomized 
double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial
Valentin Mocanu1, Zhengxiao Zhang2,3, Edward C. Deehan2, Dina H. Kao4, 
Naomi Hotte2, Shahzeer Karmali1, Daniel W. Birch1, Kalutota K. Samarasinghe   5, Jens Walter6 and

Fecal microbial transplantation (FMT) from lean donors to patients with obesity has been associated with metabolic benefits, 
yet results so far have been inconsistent. In this study, we tested the application of daily fiber supplementation as an adjunct 
to FMT therapy to modulate cardiometabolic outcomes. We performed a double-blind randomized trial in patients with severe 
obesity and metabolic syndrome receiving oral FMT, to test high-fermentable (HF) and low-fermentable (LF) fiber supplements 
(NCT03477916). Seventy participants were randomized to the FMT-HF (n = 17), FMT-LF (n = 17), HF (n = 17) and LF (n = 19) 
groups. The primary outcome was the assessment of change in insulin sensitivity from baseline to 6 weeks using the homeo-
static model assessment (HOMA2-IR/IS). After 6 weeks, only patients in the FMT-LF group had significant improvements 
in HOMA2-IR (3.16 ± 3.01 at 6 weeks versus 3.77 ± 3.57 at baseline; P = 0.02). No difference in HOMA2-IR was observed 
over this period for those in the FMT-HF group (3.25 ± 1.70 at 6 weeks versus 3.17 ± 1.72 at baseline; P = 0.8), the HF group 
(3.49 ± 1.43 at 6 weeks versus 3.26 ± 1.33 at baseline; P = 0.8) or the LF group (3.76 ± 2.01 at 6 weeks versus 3.56 ± 1.81 at 
baseline; P = 0.8). Interventions were safe and well-tolerated with no treatment-attributed serious adverse events. We provide 
proof of concept for the use of a single-dose oral FMT combined with daily low-fermentable fiber supplementation to improve 
insulin sensitivity in patients with severe obesity and metabolic syndrome.

Results
Overview of trial enrollment. From 7 May 2018 to 9 October 2019, 
5,200 patients were electronically screened, 780 were assessed for 
eligibility, 70 were randomized, and 68 received study intervention 
(Fig. 1). A total of 61 patients completed their week 6 visit and were 
analyzed using a modified intent-to-treat protocol. Seven patients 
withdrew before the primary outcome (FMT-HF group, n = 2; 
FMT-LF group, n = 3; HF group, n = 2), with three patients citing 
time-commitment concerns, two requiring prolonged antibiotic 
courses (leg cellulitis, n = 1; prostatitis, n = 1) and two being offered 
early surgery. Of those who reached the primary week 6 endpoint, 
five (FMT-HF group, n = 1; FMT-LF group, n = 1; HF group, n = 1; 
LF group, n = 2) withdrew due to time commitments prior to com-
pleting their week 12 visit.
Participants were consecutively enrolled, block randomized and 
stratified by sex into one of four groups: FMT and high-fermentable 
fiber (FMT-HF), FMT and low-fermentable fiber (FMT-LF), pla-
cebo FMT and high-fermentable fiber (HF), and placebo FMT and 
low-fermentable fiber (LF). All patients underwent concurrent 
medical therapy but were asked to discontinue alternative prebiotic 
or probiotic therapies. High-fermentable fiber supplementation 
consisted of an equal mixture of soluble corn fiber, resistant wheat 
starch type 4 and acacia gum, while low-fermentable fiber supple-
mentation consisted of microcrystalline cellulose at doses of 33 g d−1 
for male participants and 27 g d−1 for female participants. Further 
details about the trial design, including inclusion and exclusion cri-
teria, are available in the Methods.
Characteristics of the study population. The study participants 
who completed our 6 week primary endpoint had a mean age of 
47.8 ± 10.0 years, a mean body mass index of 45.3 ± 7.0 kg m−2, 
and a female sex predominance (83.6%) (Table 1). The majority 
of patients were taking the anti-hyperglycemic medications either 
glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) (n = 39, 63.9%) or sodium–glucose 
co-transporter 2 inhibitors (n = 3, 4.9%), while others received 
conventional metformin (n = 15, 24.6%) therapy. No differences in 
basic characteristics, degree of metabolic dysfunction, medications 
or dietary intake were observed between the groups at baseline.
Insulin sensitivity and HOMA2. From baseline to 6 weeks, par-
ticipants in the FMT-LF group had significant improvements in 
HOMA2-IR (3.16 ± 3.01 at 6 weeks versus 3.77 ± 3.57 at baseline; 
P = 0.02), while no differences were observed for those in the 
FMT-HF group (3.25 ± 1.70 at 6 weeks versus 3.17 ± 1.72 at base-
line; P = 0.8), the HF group (3.49 ± 1.43 at 6 weeks versus 3.26 ± 1.33 
at baseline; P = 0.8) or the LF group (3.76 ± 2.01 at 6 weeks versus 
3.56 ± 1.81 at baseline; P = 0.8). Similarly, when comparing the 
FMT recipients with their fiber control groups, only participants 
receiving FMT and low-fermentable fiber supplementation had 
significant improvements in the mean difference for HOMA2-IR 
(−24.0% ± 12.0%; P = 0.02) and for HOMA2-IS (27.6% ± 12.3%; 
P = 0.02) versus participants receiving low-fermentable fiber alone 
(Fig. 2a,b).
Secondary metabolic outcomes. We then evaluated whether the 
improvements in insulin sensitivity seen at 6 weeks were driven by 
changes to serum insulin or by differences in fasting plasma glu-
cose. This revealed significant improvements in serum insulin levels 
(mean difference, −25.4 ± 12.3%; P = 0.02, Fig. 2c) for patients in the 
FMT-LF group compared with those in the LF group. No differences 
in fasting glycemia or glycated hemoglobin were observed either 
within groups or between interventions at 6 weeks. Furthermore, 
these findings occurred in the absence of any significant changes 
in anthropometric values (Extended Data Fig. 1), lipid parameters 
(Extended Data Fig. 2) or dietary intake (Supplementary Table 1).

lin sensitivity were not maintained in the absence of daily fiber 
intake (Extended Data Fig. 3). We did, however, observe significant

Serum inflammatory markers and fecal short-chain fatty acids. 
Given that systemic inflammation has been linked to altered gut 
barrier function and the development of insulin resistance35,36, 
we measured serum markers of inflammation and endotoxemia 
(Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Although participants receiv-
ing high-fermentable fiber had reduced levels of tumor necrosis 
factor-α and interleukin-8 at week 6, these changes did not trans-
late to observed benefits in glycemic outcomes and the markers 
returned to baseline levels after fiber cessation. Circulating plasma 
lipopolysaccharide, which is an established marker of gut barrier 
integrity and is associated with insulin resistance, was also not 
altered by treatment in any of the groups at either 6 or 12 weeks. 
Notably, fecal SCFAs, which are reasoned to improve gut bar-
rier function and insulin resistance, were not influenced by either 
FMT or fiber intervention throughout the course of the study 
(Supplementary Tables 3 and 4).
Oral glucose tolerance test and enteroendocrine response. 
Blunted enteroendocrine responses to glycemia have long been 
implicated in the pathophysiology of insulin resistance in patients 
with obesity and metabolic syndrome37. To elucidate potential 
mechanisms through which FMT-LF attenuated insulin resistance, 
we compared the responses of serum GLP-1 and insulin to an oral 
glucose tolerance test at baseline with those at week 6 (Fig. 3). After 
6 weeks, the FMT-LF intervention was associated with restoration 
of the physiologic patterns of GLP-1 secretion, as evidenced by the 
significant increase between 0 and 1 h and the decrease between 
1 and 2 h (P < 0.05). Peak 1 h serum GLP-1 levels in the FMT-LF 
group at week 6 also significantly increased compared with the 
FMT-HF group (P = 0.04) and approached significance in the LF 
group (P = 0.08). Marked reductions in peak insulinemia were also 
demonstrated at 1 h (baseline to week 6, P < 0.05) in the FMT-LF 
and LF groups, with these effects notably more pronounced in the 
FMT-LF group (P = 0.052).

to which gut microbiota correlated with the observed metabolic 
improvements. An alpha diversity analysis showed that the FMT-LF 
intervention was associated with increases in bacterial richness 
(Chao1 index) from baseline to week 6 and also from baseline to 
week 12 (Fig. 4a, q < 0.01). There were no differences in evenness 
(Shannon index) over the same time period for any group. At week 6, 
beta diversity for both FMT groups demonstrated significant shifts 
in microbiota composition (Fig. 4b, q < 0.02), with the FMT-LF 
group having a greater degree of compositional change (Fig. 4c) 
than at baseline. Shifts in beta diversity were no longer significant 
at 12 weeks. When focusing on specific taxa, the FMT-LF inter-
vention led to changes in seven genera and 12 amplicon sequence 
variants (ASVs), several of which were detectable at week 6, includ-
ing increases in the relative amounts of Phascolarcobacterium,

Baseline microbial taxa as predictors of insulin sensitivity. To 
assess whether the abundance of particular bacterial taxa at baseline 
was able to independently predict improvements in HOMA2-IR 
and insulin sensitivity in patients receiving FMT-LF after adjust-
ing for baseline characteristics, we conducted a repeated linear 
mixed model analysis. This showed that Phascolarctobacterium, 
Bacteroides stercoris and Bacteroides caccae (Fig. 4d) were signifi-
cantly associated with HOMA2-IR and insulin sensitivity. Given 
that these taxa have been associated with insulinemic and entero-
endocrine benefits in metabolic syndrome and obesity38–40, they 
may reflect an adaptive host microbial response that is particularly 
responsive to microbial biotherapeutic intervention, and may be 
used as treatment.
Insulin sensitivity and clinical covariates. We also examined 
whether the results could have been confounded by factors unre-
lated to the study intervention, such as differences in basic charac-
teristics, medication regimen or degree of metabolic dysfunction. 
Two multivariable logistic regression models were developed to 
evaluate changes between baseline and week 6 in insulin resistance 
and insulin sensitivity (Table 2). After adjusting for these clinical 
covariates, the greatest independent predictor of improvements in 
both insulin resistance and insulin sensitivity was allocation to the 
FMT-LF intervention (P = 0.003). Interestingly, baseline hyperin-
sulinemia was also an independent predictor of improved insulin 
resistance and sensitivity, which suggests that patients with more 
severe perturbances in glycemic regulation may be more likely to 
respond to the FMT-LF intervention.

Discussion
In this phase 2 trial, we show that low-fermentable fiber supple-
mentation following oral FMT improved insulin sensitivity from 
baseline to 6 weeks in patients with severe obesity and metabolic 
syndrome. The fact that these metabolic benefits were indepen-
dent of factors such as diet or medications but were associated with 
improved enteroendocrine responses, altered microbial ecology and 
increased engraftment of donor microbes provides further support 
for gut microbial modulation as a promising strategy to ameliorate 
metabolic dysfunction.
Understanding of the efficacy of FMTs in pathologies other than 
Clostridioides difficile infections is still very much in its infancy. 
However, there is increasing evidence that targeting the microbi-
ome can affect metabolic health. Regarding this study, key questions 
remain as to the underlying mechanisms of the improvement in 
insulin sensitivity and the modulation of microbial engraftment in 
patients receiving low-fermentable microcrystalline cellulose fiber 
supplementation after FMT therapy.
Possible explanations include the ability of cellulose to act as a 
bulking and binding agent, which could alter metabolite luminal 
concentrations, influence gastrointestinal transit and modulate the 
donor microbe–host mucus layer interface. Cellulose supplementa-
tion may also directly alter the function of specific taxa, including 
cellulose-degrading H2-producing methanogens, leading to changes 
in gut microbial fermentation efficiency and by-products44,45. 
Together these factors might constitute mechanisms through which 
the FMT-LF intervention increases microbial diversity and richness 
while also potentially inducing functional changes in taxa associ-
ated with host HOMA2-IR/IS improvements.
The limitations of this study are in keeping with the nature of 
the proof-of-concept study design. Given the relatively small sample 
size, the study was not sufficiently powered to evaluate small dif-
ferences across the full spectrum of metabolic outcomes or other 
plausible mechanisms associated with the two fiber therapies. The 
study was also powered to evaluate only changes occurring from 
baseline to week 6, limiting our ability to identify why the effects 
were not maintained following fiber cessation at 12 weeks. Given 
that we did not conduct any ‘omics’ analysis, the complex functional 
implications of the microbial modulation could not be fully evalu-
ated. Hence, caution should be borne in mind while interpreting the 
results from the inferential analysis. It is also possible that factors 
such as medications, diet and exercise could have confounded the 
findings. All patients, however, were managed by the same clinical 
care team, which provided standardized nutritional, exercise, and 
psychosocial guidance, thereby minimizing the potential confound-
ing effects of these variables. Last, due to scarcity, donors could not 
be stratified across groups. Nevertheless, the allocation of particular 
donors did not differ significantly between groups, arguing against 
the possibility of the results being confounded by a super-donor 
phenomenon.
In conclusion, this phase 2 trial provides novel evidence that 
daily low-fermentable fiber supplementation can improve insulin 
sensitivity following FMT by differentially modulating engraft-
ment of select bacterial taxa and the enteroendocrine axis. We also 
provide proof that a single-dose oral FMT can be safely and fea-
sibly engrafted in patients with severe obesity and metabolic syn-
drome. Results from our trial will serve as a basis for the ongoing 
development of novel microbial biotherapeutic strategies aimed at 
combatting the growing worldwide epidemic of severe obesity and 
metabolic syndrome through the future delivery of safe, effective 
and affordable designer bacterial consortia.

Methods
Trial design overview. This pragmatic proof-of-concept phase 2 trial was approved 
by the University of Alberta Health Research Ethics Board (Pro00076642) and 
Health Canada (control 212903), and was registered with clinicaltrials.gov 
(NCT03477916). Informed consent was obtained from all participants and safety 
monitoring was conducted independently by the University of Alberta Quality 
Management in Clinical Research Department (QMCR). The study complied 
with all relevant institutional and national regulatory body requirements. 
The trial utilized an exploratory four-arm parallel double-blind randomized 
placebo-controlled study design. All patients with severe obesity and metabolic 
syndrome who provided informed consent were consecutively enrolled through 
the Weight Wise Bariatric Clinic (Edmonton, Alberta, Canada) from 10 July 2018 
to 30 October 2019 inclusive. Participants underwent a 2 week screening and 
clinical baseline assessment prior to being randomized to one of four groups: FMT 
and HF, FMT and LF, placebo FMT and HF, and placebo FMT and LF.
Randomization was performed at a 1:1:1:1 ratio in blocks of four and stratified 
by sex via computer-generated codes using REDCap cloud (version 1.6). The 
individual who generated the random allocation sequence (N.H.) did not take part 
in the enrollment or assessment of patients. Participants and all study personnel 
were blinded to study allocation for the duration of the trial. The interventions 
consisted of a single FMT dose (or placebo) followed by a 6 week study period 
during which high-fermentable or low-fermentable fibers were added daily to 
the participant’s normal diet. All patients were provided with concurrent medical 
therapy by the bariatric team through the duration of the trial.
In total, participants attended five clinic appointments (baseline, FMT, 2, 6 
and 12 weeks) for anthropometric measurements, assessment of vital signs, and 
completion of assessment questionnaires (dietary intake, hunger and satiety, 
quality of life), as well as blood and stool sample collection. Telephone interviews 
were conducted at 1 and 4 weeks to assess for adverse events and compliance. A 2 h 
75 g oral glucose tolerance test was performed only at baseline and at 6 weeks due 
to the reluctance of participants to undergo a further procedure at the 12 week time 
point. Compensation was provided in the form of cash stipends (CAN$10) at each 
in-person clinic appointment.
Bariatric management. Edmonton’s Weight Wise Bariatric Clinic is a diverse 
multidisciplinary clinic staffed with bariatricians, endocrinologists, psychologists, 
physiotherapists and dieticians. Clinical teams were blinded to study allocation, 
and all patients underwent concurrent bariatric therapy including medical 
pharmacotherapy, nutritional modification and mental health counseling.
Study population. The inclusion criteria were age between 18 and 65 years; 
body mass index (BMI) > 30 kg m−2; total body weight fluctuation over the last 
6 months < 10%; fasting plasma glucose > 5.6 mmol l−1 or glycated hemoglobin 
(HbA1c) ≥ 5.5% or anti-diabetic medication use; and at least one of fasting 
triglycerides ≥ 1.7 mmol l−1, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) 
<1.03 mmol l−1 in male participants or <1.29 mmol l−1 in female participants, 
known diagnosis of hypertension or systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg 
or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 85 mmHg, dyslipidemia medication use or 
antihypertension medication use.
The exclusion criteria were systolic blood pressure ≥ 180 mmHg or diastolic 
blood pressure ≥ 110 mmHg at screening, fasting triglycerides ≥6 mmol l−1, acute 
infection or inflammatory condition over the preceding 4 weeks, antibiotic use in 
the past 4 weeks, current or use of insulin in the past 6 months, prior weight loss 
surgery, dysphagia, inflammatory bowel disease, colon cancer, colonic polyps with 
high-grade dysplasia, history of autoimmune or chronic inflammatory conditions 
(rheumatoid arthritis, chronic or active hepatitis B or C, human immunodeficiency 
virus, pancreatitis, advanced non-alcoholic steatohepatitis or liver cirrhosis), active 
malignancy, pregnancy, and active substance or alcohol abuse (more than two 8 oz 
drinks per d).
Sample size and recruitment. Assuming a treatment allocation factor of 1:1 and 
accepting a type 1 error of 0.05 and a correlation between repeated measurements 
of 50%, a sample size of 15 subjects per arm gave the study a power of 80% to 
detect a change of 0.8 log (s.d. 1) in the logHOMA2-IR value, or 80% power to 
detect a minimum difference of 1.1 (s.d. 1.1) in HbA1c levels between groups. 
Accounting for an expected 10% dropout rate, we enrolled a total of 68 subjects for 
this feasibility study. All randomized subjects who completed 6 weeks of follow-up 
were included for analysis.
Study endpoints. The primary outcome was a change in insulin sensitivity 
between baseline and 6 weeks as estimated using the HOMA2-IR (HOMA2 
Calculator, University of Oxford). Secondary outcomes included changes in 
vital signs (blood pressure, heart rate), anthropometric parameters (BMI, hip 
and waist circumference), glycemic parameters (fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, 
serum insulin, GLP-1), fasting lipid profile (total cholesterol, HDL-C, low-density 
lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglycerides), quality of life assessed with the 
EQ-5D-5L questionnaires, perceived satiety assessed with the Satiety Labeled 
Intensity Magnitude (SLIM) questionnaire, gastrointestinal tolerance assessed 
with a gastrointestinal tolerance questionnaire, dietary habits assessed with the 
dietary history questionnaire III (DHQ3), gut hormones (leptin, ghrelin), serum 
inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein (CRP), TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, LPS), 
fecal SCFAs, and fecal microbiota composition between baseline, 6 and 12 weeks.
Interventions. FMT manufacture. Four lean healthy volunteer stool donors 
with no metabolic comorbidities provided stool for the study participants. 
The limited number of donors did not allow for equal stratification of donors 
across all four groups (Supplementary Table 1). Donor selection was done by 
availability and screening for potential pathogens or antimicrobial resistance and 
was in compliance with Health Canada regulations46. Oral FMT capsules were 
manufactured as previously described47. Placebo FMT pills consisted of cellulose 
and were identical in appearance to ensure the blinding of patients and study staff.
FMT administration. The day prior to FMT delivery, patients were instructed to 
undergo a 24 h clear fluid fast and a bowel preparation involving two doses of 
Pico-Salax, a routine colonoscopy preparation. On the day of transplantation, 20 
FMT capsules (weighing 50 g in total) from one of four universal donors or 20 
placebo capsules were administered orally to each patient. Patients were instructed 
to restart a solid diet 1 h following the procedure as tolerated and to stop any other 
prebiotic or probiotic supplementation until the completion of the trial.
Fiber selection, manufacturing and administration. Fibers were chosen by 
expert consensus based on criteria of metabolic efficacy, tolerability and safety. 
High-fermentable fiber supplementation consisted of an equal mixture by weight 
of soluble corn fiber (PROMITOR, Tate&Lyle, 114 kcal per 100 g), resistant wheat 
starch type 4 (Fibersym, MGP Ingredients, 35 kcal per 100 g), and acacia gum 
(Pre-Hydrated Gum Arabic, TIC GUMS, 17 kcal per 100 g). In addition to the 
previous factors, these components were selected due to their ability to promote 
beneficial gut microbial and immune modulation48–53. Low-fermentable fiber 
supplementation consisted of microcrystalline cellulose (Microcel MC-12, Blanver 
Farmoquimica, 0 kcal per 100 g). The fibers were weighed and provided in foil 
packets, in portions of 27 g (for female participants) or 33 g (for male participants). 
The first three sachets (d 1–3) contained half the dose by weight to minimize 
gastrointestinal side-effects. Three week batches were provided on the day of 
FMT and at the time of the 6 week visit. Participants were instructed to start fiber 
supplementation on the day following FMT and were given dietary instructions 
from registered dietitians to optimize adherence. All sachets and fibers were 
identical in appearance to ensure blinding.
Anthropometry and serology collection. Anthropometric assessment was 
performed by trained personnel following the recommendations made by the 
Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and published in the National 
Health and Nutrition Examination Survey Anthropometry Procedures Manual54. 
Body weight was measured using a validated, calibrated bariatric scale (Scale 
Tronix) and recorded to the nearest 0.1 kg. Height was measured to the nearest 
0.1 cm using a wall-mounted stadiometer. Waist and hip circumferences were 
measured following the recommendations of the CDC Anthropometry Procedures 
Manual54 and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines55.
Blood samples were collected after an overnight fast (>8 h) and aliquots of 
plasma and serum were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. 
Routine laboratory investigations were completed using standardized laboratory 
techniques, and included a cell blood count and differentials, an electrolyte panel 
(sodium, potassium, chloride and carbon dioxide), a renal panel (creatinine, blood 
urea nitrogen), a liver panel (aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, 
alkaline phosphatase, albumin, international normalized ratio of prothrombin 
time), a glycemia panel (HbA1c, blood glucose, insulin), a lipid panel (total 
cholesterol, HDL-C, LDL-C and triglycerides), and CRP.
ELISA analysis. All assays were conducted in singlet, following the manufacturer 
protocol and storage recommendations. Immunologic evaluation included 
IL-1β (R&D Systems DuoSet ELISA, catalog DY201-05), IL-6 (R&D Systems 
DuoSet ELISA, catalog DY206-05), IL-10 (R&D Systems DuoSet ELISA, catalog 
DY217B-05), TNF-α (R&D Systems DuoSet ELISA, catalog DY210-05) and LPS 
(Abbexa Endotoxin (ET) ELISA Kit, catalog ABX514093). Glycemic markers were 
assessed using insulin assays (R&D Systems DuoSet ELISA, catalog DY8056-05) 
with a twofold serum dilution. Enteroendocrine markers were evaluated using 
total GLP-1 (EMD Millipore Corporation, catalog EZGGLP1T-36K). Hormones 
influencing hunger and satiety were analyzed using a ghrelin assay (R&D Systems 
DuoSet ELISA, DY8149-05) and a leptin assay (R&D Systems DuoSet ELISA, 
DY398-05) with a 20-fold serum dilution.
Stool collection, short-chain fatty acid and microbiome analysis. Participants 
were given stool collection kits and instructed to collect stool samples within 2 d of 
their scheduled visits. The written instructions directed the participants to preserve 
at least 30 g of stool, store the sample at 4 °C prior to transport, and to deliver the 
sample on ice. On arrival, samples were subsequently aliquoted and flash frozen at 
−80 °C for storage.
Stool SCFA concentrations were analyzed using gas chromatography at 
the Agricultural, Food and Nutritional Science chromatography core facility

as previously described56. In brief, 800 µl 0.1 N hydrochloric acid and 200 µl 
25% phosphoric acid were added to approximately 0.2 g stool. The contents 
were vortexed until fully homogenized and centrifuged at 5,000g for 15 min or 
until a clear supernatant was obtained. An internal standard solution (150 mg 
4-methyl-valeric acid, S381810, Sigma-Aldrich), 5% phosphoric acid and 
supernatant were then added to glass chromatography tubes and stored at −80°C 
prior to analysis. Samples were analyzed with a gas chromatograph (Bruker SCION 
456-GC, Bruker Corporation) using a 30 m × 0.53 mm inner diameter × 0.5 µm film 
thickness capillary column (Stabilwax-DA, Restek Corporation).
Fecal DNA extraction for microbiome analysis was conducted using a 
modified MultiTarget Pharmaceuticals protocol. Bleached beads were added 
to tubes in combination with 200 µl AquaStool (MultiTarget Pharmaceuticals) 
and approximately 100 mg thawed stool, and homogenized. The resultant 
homogenate was centrifuged (14,000g for 5 min) followed by the addition of 
100 µl AquaRemove (MultiTarget Pharmaceuticals). After re-centrifugation, the 
supernatant was collected, and isopropanol was added prior to precipitation on 
ice for 10 min. The DNA pellet was collected and washed three times with 70% 
ethanol. A total of 100 µl EB Buffer (Qiagen) was then added to solubilize the DNA, 
followed by 1 µl RNASE A (Qiagen, catalog 1007885). The mixture was incubated 
at 37 °C for 1 h and then re-centrifuged. DNA precipitation was obtained with 
10 µl 5 M NaCl, 100 µl ice cold 100% ethanol, and a 30 min incubation at −20 °C. 
The mixture was re-centrifuged and the pellet was rinsed three times with 70% 
ethanol. An additional 50 µl EB buffer was added after removal of excess ethanol, 
and the solution was left overnight at 4 °C for solubilization. After ensuring 
appropriate extraction quality using a Nanodrop 1000 Series device (Thermo 
Fisher Scientific), samples were sent for 16s rRNA gene amplicon sequencing 
(Microbiome Insights). Microbial composition was characterized using 16S rRNA 
gene amplicon sequencing of the v4 region with MiSeq Illumina technology 
(2 × 300 bp) and the following primers: 515F, GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTA; 
and 806R, GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT. QIIME2 and DADA2 were used 
for quality control and construction of the feature table57. Taxonomic assignment 
(from kingdom to genus level) of the representative sequences of each sample 
was performed using the Silva 132 pre-trained Naive Bayes classifier and the 
q2-feature-classifier plugin in the QIIME2 pipeline.
Statistical analysis. General analyses. A modified intent-to-treat analysis was 
performed to assess the effect of intervention from baseline to week 6. Continuous 
variables are reported as mean ± s.d. if normally distributed or as median and 
interquartile range if non-normally distributed. Normality was evaluated using the 
Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical data are reported as proportions and were analyzed 
using the Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test. The data in the Figures are reported as 
mean ± s.e.m. unless otherwise stated. The percentage change in variables was 
calculated by dividing the difference between the week 6 and baseline values by the 
baseline value. The reported mean difference of primary outcomes was calculated 
using the difference in percentage change between interventions. Within-group 
paired changes between baseline and week 6 were conducted using the two-tailed 
Wilcoxon signed-rank test while between-group comparisons were conducted 
using the Mann–Whitney U-test. When more than two measures were compared 
over time, repeated measures linear regression was used to compare within-group 
differences relative to baseline. Data that were non-normally distributed were 
transformed using the cube root method before analysis with the linear regression 
model. Data greater than 5 s.d. from the mean were removed as outliers.
A non-parsimonious logistic regression model was developed using a 
prospective hypothesis-driven methodologic approach to identify factors that 
independently predict changes in our primary outcomes. HOMA2-IR and 
HOMA2-IS outcomes were dichotomized into responder and non-responder 
groups. Responders were defined as having improved HOMA2 per cent change 
parameters while non-responders were defined as having worsened per cent 
change in HOMA2 markers. Variables that were significant at P < 0.1 were included 
in the main effects models unless otherwise deemed clinically relevant. Analysis 
for clinical, biochemical, and immunologic data was conducted using STATA 15 
(StataCorp 2017) and the Figures were designed using Prism 9.0.0 (GraphPad 
Software). Statistical significance was defined using a two-tailed P value of <0.05.
Microbiome bioinformatics and statistical analyses. Alpha diversity was calculated 
using Chao1 and Shannon indices with QIIME2. To compare microbial beta 
diversity between samples, Bray–Curtis distance matrices were measured using the 
vegan package in R (ref. 58), and Unifrac distance matrices were measured using 
QIIME2. Principal coordinate analysis was applied on the resulting distance matrices 
to generate two-dimensional plots using the ape R package59. Significant differences 
in beta diversity were assessed using permutational multivariate analysis of variance 
(PERMANOVA, n = 999) with the Adonis function in vegan. For the microbiota 
compositional features (alpha diversity index and centered log-ratio-transformed 
bacterial taxa counts), repeated measures linear regression and paired t-tests were 
applied to compare within-group differences relative to baseline. Between-group 
differences were assessed using linear regression, and pairwise comparisons 
(FMT-HF versus HF; FMT-LF versus LF) were assessed using unpaired t-tests.  
P values were adjusted using the Benjamin–Hochberg false discovery rate method. 
The microbiome analysis was conducted using R (version 3.5.1).
