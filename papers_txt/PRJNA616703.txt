Running title: Butyrate-forming populations in infant gut 
 
Key words: butyrate, endospores, infant gut microbiota, gut microbiota development

Significance/Originality statement 
In this study, we determined succession and development of the butyrate-forming population 
during infant gut development with a focus on endospore formers. We thereby present an 
approach that characterized and quantitatively profiled endospore formers through a 
combination of heat treatment and cultivation, 16S rRNA gene sequencing and short chain fatty 
acid analysis. We could show that in infants, the endospore community contributed about 
0.001% to total cells, and was mainly composed of the pioneer butyrate-producing Clostridium 
sensu stricto. In comparison, butyrate production of representative Clostridium sensu stricto 
was lower than of butyrate-producing Lachnospiraceae, which might explain low fecal butyrate 
levels during the first year of life. This study contributes to our understanding of roles and 
contributions of specific functional groups during gut microbiota development. 
Summary 
The acquisition of the infant gut microbiota is key to establishing a host-microbiota symbiosis. 
Microbially produced metabolites tightly interact with the immune system, and the 
fermentation-derived short chain fatty acid butyrate is considered an important mediator linked 
to chronic diseases later in life. The intestinal butyrate-forming bacterial population is 
taxonomically and functionally diverse and includes endospore formers with high transmission 
potential. Succession, and contribution of butyrate-producing taxa have been little investigated. 
We determined abundance of major butyrate-forming groups and fermentation metabolites in 
feces, isolated, cultivated and characterized the heat-resistant cell population, which included 
endospores, and compared butyrate formation efficiency of representative taxa in batch

Introduction 
The relationship between a human host and its microbiome is a mutualistic symbiosis with the 
host providing nutrients and a niche for the microbial community, while the microbial 
community contributes to digestion and energy gain, supports immune system development and 
provides defense against enteric infection (Arrieta et al., 2014). This mutual interaction is 
crucial during the first year of life when immune system and gut microbiota rapidly develop. 
The infant gut microbiome evolves from an initially diverse and heterogeneous composition, to 
a simpler, and finally a complex adult like configuration (Ferretti et al., 2018). This transition 
is nonrandom and is likely driven by the establishment of an anaerobic environment, nutrient 
availability and microbial interactions (Bäckhed et al., 2014). First colonizers are transmitted 
vertically during birth or breastfeeding, or are obtained from the hospital environment (Jost et 
al., 2014; Raveh-Sedka et al., 2016). Immediately after birth, facultative anaerobes, mainly 
Enterococcus, Staphylococcus, Streptococcus spp., and members of Enterobacteriaceae reach 
high densities (Jost et al., 2012, Pham et al., 2016), while the consumption of breast milk favors 
colonization of Bifidobacteriaceae (Lawson et al., 2020). Initial colonization and concurrent 
oxygen depletion allow the growth of strict anaerobic bacteria. The uptake of endospores may 
contribute to the acquisition of members of the families Clostridiaceae, Ruminococcaceae, 
Peptostreptococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae and Erysipelotrichaceae (Browne et al., 2016; 
Kearney et al., 2018; Nayfach et al., 2016). Endospores are a metabolically dormant and highly 
resistant microbial lifeform enabling survival during exposure to environmental stressors such 
as oxygen, acidic pH, and heat. The sporulation process can be initiated after exposure to 
unfavorable environmental conditions including nutrient deprivation, oxygen exposure or low

pH (Al-Hinai et al., 2015). Recently, Browne et al. identified several new endospore-forming 
taxa among the Firmicutes, and suggested that 50-60% of gut bacterial genera found in adults 
are able to form endospores (Browne et al., 2016).  
Diet has major impact on bacterial composition and fermentation activity, which leads to the 
formation of lactate and short chain fatty acids (SCFA) succinate, formate, acetate, butyrate, 
and propionate. These microbiota-generated metabolites are important energy sources for 
intestinal epithelial cells, have diverse regulatory functions, and impact host physiology and 
immunity (Rooks et al., 2016). Gut microbiomes rely on trophic interactions to ferment dietary 
and endogenous glycans. Acetate, the most abundant SCFA in feces, is the fermentation product 
of most intestinal bacteria, while propionate and butyrate are produced by specialized 
subpopulations (Louis and Flint, 2017). The major share of butyrate is formed from 
carbohydrates through glycolysis (Vital et al., 2017). There are two pathways, for the formation 
of butyrate from butyryl-CoA, (1) the butyryl-CoA:acetate CoA-transferase (butCoAT)

rely on the butCoAT-pathway (Louis and Flint, 2017). In contrast, members of the endospore-
forming Clostridium sensu stricto (Slobodkin et al., 2014; Vital et al., 2014; Wiegel et al., 
2006), the core group of the Clostridiaceae (for e.g. Clostridium butyricum, Clostridium 
paraputrificum) and the Peptostreptococcaceae for e.g. Clostridioides difficile) employ the 
buk-pathway. The role of endospore formers in intestinal butyrate formation during the first

months of life has been little investigated. Infant fecal butyrate levels are low, and depend, 
besides the availability of dietary and endogenous glycans, on the presence of taxa able to form 
butyrate (Pham et al., 2016). Nevertheless, butyrate formation during infanthood seems 
beneficial for health in later life and has been negatively linked to the occurrence of chronic 
diseases including allergy (Cait et al., 2019; Roduit et al., 2019; Sandin et al., 2009; Trompette 
et al., 2014). We therefore employed a holistic approach that determined fecal microbiota 
composition, the abundance of major butyrate-forming groups, and fecal concentrations of 
fermentation metabolites in samples of 69 infants were collected 90, 180 and 360 days after 
birth by the CARE (Childhood AlleRgy, nutrition and Environment)-study. CARE is a birth 
cohort conducted at the cantonal hospital and the children’s hospital in St. Gallen, Switzerland, 
which aims to elucidate the impact of gut microbiota development on the occurrence of allergic 
diseases. Concurrently, we used cultivation to estimate the most probable number (MPN) and 
fermentation activity of heat-resistant cells in fecal samples. We additionally compared butyrate 
formation capacity of representative strains of Clostridiaceae, Peptostreptococcaceae, 
Erysipelotrichaceae, Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae in single batch cultures.  
Material and Methods 
Samples. Fecal samples were collected in the context of the CARE-study, which was approved 
by the ethical committee of the canton St. Gallen (EKSG 15/005). All parents gave informed 
consent for sample collection and analyses. Feces were collected by the mothers directly from 
the diapers using sterile spoons and containers, and were sent overnight with cooling elements 
to the CARE-study team, samples arrived within 12-48 h. Samples were aliquoted and kept at 
-80 °C until further processing. The samples analysed in this project were collected from 69

in the gas phase. All experiments were conducted with 3-4 biological replicates. Optical density 
at 600 nm was monitored immediately after inoculation and after 24 h incubation using a WPA 
CO 8000 Cell Density Meter (BIOLABO Scientific Instruments, Switzerland). 
Most-Probable-Number (MPN) estimation of heat-resistant bacteria cells including endo-
spores in feces. The number of anaerobic heat resistance bacteria cells, which includes 
endospores, was estimated after heat treatment and cultivation using the MPN method and

YCFA containing glucose, cellobiose and starch with sodium taurocholate (0.1%) to enhance 
spore germination (Browne et al., 2016; Duncan et al., 2002; Mukhopadhya et al., 2018) as 
growth medium. YCFA was recently shown to be a suitable medium for broad range culturing 
(Browne et al., 2016). Approximately 100 mg of feces was suspended in 500 µL of 1% peptone 
water (Universal peptone M 66, Merck) by vortexing. To inactivate vegetative cells, the mixture 
was heated to 80 ˚C for 20 minutes, according to conditions that were previously used to isolate 
endospores from feces (Harmon et al., 1986; Lister et al., 2014). After heating, samples were 
centrifuged at 13’000 rpm for 5 minutes and the supernatant was discarded. All following steps 
were carried out in an anaerobic chamber (10% CO2, 5% H2, 85% N2, Coy Laboratories, USA). 
Samples were resuspended in 500 µL of 1% anaerobic peptone water and were serially diluted 
10-fold. The MPN was determined using 96-well cell culture plates (SPL Lifesciences Co, 
Switzerland) and a five-replicate design. YCFA medium (180 µL) was inoculated with 20 µL 
diluted sample. Plates were incubated under anaerobic conditions at 37 ˚C and were visually 
assessed for turbidity after 24 h incubation.  
For further analyses (16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing and metabolite analysis), all 
replicates of the 10-2 dilution (2 % inoculum) were collected in a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube and 
centrifuged for 5 minutes at 13’000 rpm. Supernatant and pellet were stored at -20 ˚C. 
Metabolite analysis using high pressure liquid chromatography with refractive index 
detection (HPLC-RI). Metabolites present in fecal samples, and in the supernatants of the 
MPN test and single strain batch cultures, were quantified using HPLC-RI (Merck-Hitachi, 
Switzerland) and a Fermentation Monitoring Column (BioRad, Switzerland). H2SO4 (10 mM) 
was used as eluent at a flow rate of 0.6 mL min-1 at a column temperature of 40 ˚C.

Illumina adaptors, gene specific primers were removed using Atropos (Didion et al., 2017). 
Sequences were then processed using the DADA2 pipeline which allows inference of exact 
amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) (Callahan et al., 2016). Briefly, reads were truncated after 
170 and 160 nucleotides for forward and reverse reads, respectively. Then reads with expected 
error rates higher than 3 and 4 for forward and reverse reads were removed. After filtering, error 
rate learning and ASV inference and read pairs merging were performed on each sequencing

merged, and chimeric sequences were identified and removed using “consensus” method. An 
additional clustering step was performed using the collapseNoMismatch() function provided by 
the DADA2 package in order to confirm that ASV generated from each run were not artificially 
identified as distinct (Supplementary Data File 1). Taxonomy was assigned using the SILVA 
(v.132) database (Glöckner et al., 2017) using the functions assignTaxonomy() and 
addSpecies() functions provided by the DADA2 package (Supplementary Data File 2).  
To quantitatively assess microbiota composition of feces, heat resistant cells and endospores, 
cell counts of major bacterial families were calculated from relative abundance data and total 
bacteria cells counts determined using qPCR, or MPN essentially as described recently (Jian et 
al., 2020).

cell and spore counts among different age groups. To compare two groups, Mann-Whitney rank

ASV count table, nucleotide sequences, and taxonomical path were loaded into a phyloseq

Discussion 
Butyrate is considered a key metabolite during infant gut development, which interacts with the 
immune system and is linked to chronic diseases, such as allergies (Roduit et al., 2019). We 
show that there is a succession within the butyrate-producing community, which is pioneered 
by endospore-forming butyrate producers. 
Heat-treatment enriches for endospore-forming taxa 
We used an approach combining heat treatment and cultivation to determine the abundance and 
composition of heat resistant cells within the infant microbiota, which encompassed the 
endospore 
community. 
Our 
approach 
successfully 
enriched 
for 
Clostridiaceae, 
Peptostreptococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae, and Erysipelotrichaceae, 
bacterial families that are known to include endospore-forming taxa and to colonize the 
intestinal tract of mammals (Browne et al., 2016; Galperin, 2013).  
Recent studies employed ethanol resistance or a series of enzymatic and chemical lysis 
treatments as selection criteria to enrich for stress-resistant cells including endospores (Browne 
et al., 2016; Kearney et al., 2018). Similarly, we observed major shifts in microbial composition 
after heat treatment and cultivation in the presence of sodium taurocholate, and the successful 
enrichment of potential endospore-forming taxa including the newly identified Turicibacter, 
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
 
 
 
 
 
Anaerostipes spp., and Flavonifractor plautii (Browne et al., 2017) A. caccae, was not 
identified as a spore former when it was originally characterized, while other species of 
Anaerostipes have the ability to sporulate (Browne et al., 2017; Bui et al., 2014). However, each 
approach also selected for bacterial groups lacking the ability to sporulate, for example, a high 
proportion of Actinobacteria survived lysis treatment likely due to intrinsic lysozyme resistance 
(Kearney et al., 2018). In our study, there was a high proportion of Enterococcaceae, and to a 
lesser extent, of Enterobacteriaceae, especially in samples collected at 90 days, and a high 
likelihood for shared Enterobacteriaceae and Enterococcaceae ASV between fecal microbiota 
and its heat resistant subpopulation. Selected strains of Escherichia/Shigella and Enterococcus 
possess multiple regulatory systems to counteract heat stress, the fecal matrix might have 
provided additional protection (Bradley and Fraise, 1996; Li and Gänzle, 2016).  
The infant gut environment enriches for a specific community of endospore-forming 
bacteria  
The ability to sporulate seems to be preserved among the phylum Firmicutes (Browne et al., 
2017; Galperin, 2013). In infant fecal samples, Bacillaceae and Paenibacillaceae, which have 
been mainly associated with environmental habitats such as soil, were low abundant with 
Clostridiaceae contributing most endospores. Members of Clostridium sensu stricto such as 
Cl. butyricum are considered human and animal gut commensals (Ghoddusi et al., 2010) and 
have been recovered from infant feces together with Cl. paraputrificum and Cl. ramosum 
(Ferraris et al., 2012; Mountzouris et al., 2002).  
Vertical transmission of commensal intestinal bacteria is initiated during birth and remains 
selective and persistent during the first year. In later childhood, strains are also taken up from 
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
 
 
the environment (Korpela et al., 2018). It was reported before that the vertical transmission rate 
of endospore formers was lower than of non-endospore formers, and that members of the class 
Clostridia were depleted in vertical transmission events (Korpela et al, 2018; Nayfach et al., 
2016) suggesting uptake from the environment. Indeed, the hospital environment as a source of 
Clostridia has been discussed (Ferraris et al., 2012). A recent study investigating endospore 
populations likewise suggested a low level of taxa sharing in mothers and infants (Avershina et 
al., 2020). 
There is a succession of endospore-forming bacteria in the infant gut 
Transition processes during infant microbiota development were suggested to occur 
systematically (Bäckhed et al., 2015). Nayfach et al. (2016) identified endospore formers as 
late colonizers detectable at one year of age using Ruminococcus bromii as indicator taxon. 
However, our data together with others suggests that the colonization of endospore-formers 
starts during the first months or even days (Jost et al., 2012; Vlkova et al., 2005) of life, and 
that there is successional acquisition of potentially endospore-forming taxa, with Clostridiaceae 
and 
Lachnospiraceae 
as 
initial 
colonizers 
followed 
by 
Peptostreptococcaceae, 
Erysipelotrichaceae, and Ruminococcaceae, which was concurrently reflected by the 
composition of the endospore community.  
In infant feces, endospore abundance and diversity were low 
Sporulation requires certain triggers, which are not known for all species (Al-Hinai et al., 2015), 
but might be initiated during passage through the gastrointestinal tract (Mukhopadhya et al., 
2018). Browne et al., (2016) estimated that in adults, about 30% of the fecal microbiota were 
spore formers, and that the diversity of spore-formers was higher than of non-spore formers. In 
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
 
 
 
 
 
infants, proportion and diversity were likely much lower, endospores were in general low 
abundant representing about 0.001% of total bacterial cells, or approximately 0.1% of the 
Clostridiaceae population. These results together with Browne et al. (2016) and Nayfach et al. 
(2017) might suggest that endospore transition processes indeed increase at age one. 
Spore forming clostridia are pioneer butyrate producers with low butyrate-producing 
capacity 
During the first months of life, fecal butyrate detection rates and concentrations are low and are 
gradually increasing until an ‘adult-like’ microbiota has developed (Pham et al., 2016). In 
agreement with previous data (Vital et al., 2017), we concurrently observed low diversity of 
butyrate producers and identified Clostridium sensu stricto as butyrate-forming pioneers in the 
infant gut. Members of the Clostridiaceae are anaerobes but can tolerate oxygen to various 
extent, and are, less oxygen sensitive than F. prausnitzii and butyrate-producing 
Lachnospiraceae (Browne et al., 2016). Higher oxygen resistance together with the ability to 
form endospores might be beneficial in the infant gut ecosystem with frequently changing 
environmental conditions (Browne et al., 2016). 
The amount of primary metabolites formed depends on substrate availability, cell density, 
and/or the efficiency of a microbe to produce butyrate in the presence of unlimited substrate 
supply. At the test conditions used in this study, representative clostridia strains showed an 
overall reduced efficiency to produce butyrate from glucose possibly due to low Buk activity 
(Louis et al., 2004), which might relate to lower fecal butyrate levels if an intestinal butyrate-
forming population is dominated by Clostridium sensu stricto. In addition, butyrate formation 
capacity of a microbiota relies on the presence of individual strains, as even within strains of 
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
 
 
the same species, fermentation profiles might differ. Cell counts of the butCoAT employing F. 
prausnitzii, E. rectale /Roseburia spp. and A. hallii  were stronger related to fecal butyrate levels 
than of Clostridium sensu stricto, suggesting members of this family as a driving force in 
intestinal butyrate formation already at infant age.  
Butyrate-forming Clostridiaceae, Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae co-exist in the 
infant gut ensuring functional redundancy 
In the gut, bacteria compete for substrates but also rely on trophic interactions to obtain energy 
from carbohydrate fermentation. During the first months of life, the diet of infants consists of 
breast milk or formula. Available glycan sources are endogenous mucin, lactose, breast-milk 
oligosaccharides or galacto- and fructo-oligosaccharides in infant formula. With the 
introduction of solid food, the diversity of the complex plant-derived carbohydrates expands to 
cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin and starches (Flint et al., 2012). Occurrence and abundance of 
the Clostridium sensu stricto seemed not or little affected by the changes in diet; members are 
often saccharolytic and proteolytic and therefore have access to a wide a variety of substrates 
(Wiegel et al., 2006). In contrast, prevalence and abundance of E. rectale/Roseburia spp. and 
F. prausnitzii increased after the introduction of solid food, which starts in Switzerland at 
approximately month five, in agreement with previous studies (Vital et al., 2017). Strains of E. 
rectale and Roseburia are able to hydrolyze and ferment structurally and compositionally 
different carbohydrates while F. prausnitzii can use pectin (Lopez-Siles et al., 2012; Scott et 
al., 2014). The ability of A. hallii to utilize complex dietary carbohydrates is limited, but this 
species can cross-feed on fermentation intermediates such as lactate, acetate and 1,2-
propanediol (Schwab et al., 2017). Clostridiaceae, Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae 
This article is protected by copyright. All rights reserved.
 
 
 
 
 
thus not necessarily compete for resources while functional redundancy in butyrate formation 
ensures functional stability.  
Conclusion 
Taken together, we present an approach to successfully enrich and quantitatively analyse 
endospores from (infant) feces. The infant gut microbiota harbored a distinct endospore 
community, whose diversity and abundance were low but was dominated by taxa able to form 
butyrate. There was a succession of butyrate producers pioneered by the endospore-forming 
Clostridiaceae. Our data suggest that changes within the butyrate-forming population, as well 
as an overall increase in abundance of butyrate producers, are related to an increase of fecal 
butyrate levels during the first year of life.
