Abstract
Objectives Studies suggest that exercise affects the composition and function of the human gut microbiota, yet this has not
been investigated in a randomized controlled trial. The primary aim of this study was to assess if exercise alters the diversity,
composition and functional potential of the gut microbiota in free-living humans. A secondary aim was to test whether alpha
diversity was associated with phenotypical outcomes.
Methods Eighty eight participants with overweight or obesity completed a 6-month randomized controlled trial with 4 arms;
habitual living (CON), active commuting by bike (BIKE) and leisure-time exercise of moderate (MOD) or vigorous intensity
(VIG). Faecal samples for 16 s rRNA gene amplicon sequencing were collected prior to randomization and again after 3 and
6 months, with simultaneous registration of phenotypical outcomes and diet.
Results Shannon’s diversity index increased by 5% in VIG (CI95 1–9%, P = 0.012) at 3 months compared with CON. No
associations were observed between alpha diversity and phenotypical outcomes. Beta diversity changed in all exercise
groups compared with CON, particularly the participants in VIG showed decreased heterogeneity. No genera changed
signiﬁcantly. The inferred functional potential of the microbiota in the exercise groups was increased, primarily at 3 months
and in MOD.
Conclusion Structured exercise induced subtle changes to the human gut microbiota. Cardiorespiratory ﬁtness and fat mass
were not associated with alpha diversity.
Introduction
The role of gut microbiota in metabolic health and disease
has been a subject of increasing interest [1]. Human and
rodent studies indicate that the gut microbiota might play a
role in the pathogenesis of lifestyle related metabolic dis-
turbances such as obesity and type 2 diabetes [2–4], in part
by impacting on adiposity and insulin sensitivity [5–7]. The
complex structure of the human gut microbiota is modu-
lated by several extrinsic and intrinsic factors, including
host genetics [8], age [9], diet [10] and medication [11, 12].
Physical activity is a powerful modiﬁer of metabolic
traits such as insulin sensitivity [13], adiposity [14] and
cardiorespiratory ﬁtness [15], and constitute a key compo-
nent in primary and secondary prevention of a number of
lifestyle related diseases [16]. Physical activity affects
human metabolism through numerous mechanisms; via
direct effects on target organs such as the skeletal muscles
[17] and via less direct mechanisms such as ﬁne-tuning
energy intake [18]. Recent studies have raised the question

Materials and methods
Participants and study protocol
The analysis is based on per protocol completers from a 6-
month randomized controlled trial, active commuting to
improve health and well-being in everyday life (ACTIWE).
The trial was designed to investigate the health beneﬁts of
either 6 months of active commuting by bike or leisure-time
exercise of two different intensities and has been described
in detail elsewhere [38]. The results for body composition
and dietary intake have previously been published [39]. In
short, physically inactive, normotensive, non-diabetic and
non-smoking women and men age 20–45 years with a body
mass index of 25–35 kg/m2 were recruited. Use of pre-
scription medicine (not including oral contraceptives) or ﬁrst
degree relatives with type 2 diabetes were exclusion criteria.
Following baseline testing, 130 participants were rando-
mized in a 1:2:2:2 ratio to either: habitual living (CON),
active commuting by bike (BIKE) or leisure-time exercise of
either moderate (MOD, 50% of VO2peak-reserve) or
vigorous intensity (VIG, 70% of VO2peak-reserve). All
exercise groups were prescribed an exercise frequency of 5
training days per week with a weekly exercise energy
expenditure of 1600 kcal for women and 2100 kcal for men.
No other lifestyle modiﬁcations were included in the inter-
vention. All tests were repeated 3 and 6 months after
initiation of exercise interventions. Only participants with at
least one faecal sample and completing the study per pro-
tocol (achieving at least 80% of the prescribed exercise
energy expenditure in the exercise groups) were included in
this analysis (Total: n = 88, CON: n = 14, BIKE: n = 19,
MOD: n = 31, VIG: n = 24). The trial complies with the
Helsinki declaration and was approved by the ethical com-
mittee of The Capital Region of Denmark (H-4-2013-108),
and is registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT01962259) and at
the Danish Data Protection Agency.
Phenotypical outcomes and diet
Body composition was determined by dual-energy X-ray
absorptiometry scanning (DPX-IQ X-ray bone densitometer
4.7e, Lunar Corporation, Madison, WI, USA). Cardior-
espiratory ﬁtness was measured as the peak oxygen uptake
assessed by indirect calorimetry (Oxycon Pro, Jaeger,
Würzburg, Germany) during a graded exercise test on an
electronically braked bicycle (Lode Excalibur, Groeningen,
Netherlands). Macronutrient composition and total energy
intake was calculated (Dankost 3000, Dankost, Copenha-
gen, Denmark) based on food registrations from 3 weekdays
and 1 weekend day on which the participants were asked to
meticulously weigh and register intake of food and
beverages.
Faeces collection and analysis
Participants were provided with a home stool collection kit
and asked to collect one stool sample at baseline, and at 3
and 6 months, following a standardized procedure including
antiseptic handling, collection in sterile tubes and immedi-
ate freezing at −18 °C in the participant’s freezer. The
samples were transferred in cooling bags with freezer packs
to the lab and stored at −80 °C until further processing.
Bacterial DNA was isolated from a total of 295 faecal
samples, using the NucleoSpinSoil kit (Macherey-Nagel,
Germany) following the manufacturer’s instruction. Bac-
terial cells were lysated using SL2 + Enhancer buffer SX.
DNA yield, purity and integrity were assessed using a Qubit
2.0 ﬂuorometer, a NanoDrop 2000 spectrometer (Thermo
Fisher Scientiﬁc Inc., MA, USA) and agarose gel electro-
phoresis, respectively. 16S rRNA genes were ampliﬁed
using the 515F/806R primer set targeting the V4 hyper-
variable region. Each primer consists of the appropriate
Illumina adaptor, an 8-nt index sequence i5 and i7, a 10-nt
T. Kern et al.
pad sequence, a 2-nt linker and the gene-speciﬁc primer
[40, 41]. The PCR reactions were carried out in total
volumes of 25 μl, containing 20 ng DNA, 0.2 μM of each
primer and HotMasterMix (5Prime) solution under the
following conditions: 3 min at 94 °C, followed by 28 cycles
of 20 s at 94 °C, 30 s at 56 °C and 54 s at 72 °C on an
Eppendorf thermocycler (Eppendorf AG, Germany). The
samples were puriﬁed with a magnetic-bead based clean-up
and size selection kit (Macherey-Nagel GmbH & Co. KG,
Germany). A master DNA pool was generated from the
puriﬁed products in equimolar ratios. Sequencing was car-
ried out using 500-cycles paired-end sequencing chemistry
on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform, generating a total of
14,071,570 reads.
16S rRNA gene sequence data processing
Using the DADA2 software package v1.6 [42], raw
sequences were trimmed, ﬁltered, de-replicated and merged.
Expected error ﬁltering (Emax = 1.0) was used to exclude
low-quality reads. Chimeras were identiﬁed and removed
leaving a total of 7,567,489 read counts (median counts per
sample 24,118, iqr [21,705; 26,991]) from 2746 unique
amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomical classiﬁ-
cation of sequences was performed using the RDP classiﬁer
(bootstrap support > 80) and RDP train set 16/release 11.5
[43]. A phylogenetic tree was constructed with the phan-
gorn package v2.4.0 [44]. Analyses of 16S sequencing data
were performed in R version 3.5.1 (www.r-project.org)
using the phyloseq package v1.22.3 [45]. ASVs unchar-
acterized at phylum level were discarded. Of the 295 faecal
samples, 245 originated from per protocol completers and
were used for further analysis. Prior to alpha diversity
analyses, ASV counts were rareﬁed to an equal sequencing
depth of 14,721. For beta diversity analyses, raw data were
normalised by total-sum scaling and Bray–Curtis dissim-
ilarities and weighted UniFrac distances were calculated
using the vegan package v2.4.4. Differentially abundant
genera were analyzed after rarefying and ﬁltering out genera
present in less than 50% of samples in each group across all
time points, retaining 40 genera which accounted for 77%
of total rareﬁed reads.
Functional analysis
Bacterial metagenomic content was predicted based on rar-
eﬁed ASV counts by direct inference using Piphillin [46] and
imputation of 6470 KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes
and Genomes; version May 2017) orthologous groups (KOs).
Metabolic modules were inferred using GOmixer module
table v1.07 with a minimum coverage cutoff of 0.6 based on
the coverage distribution of all modules [47].
Statistical analysis
The statistical analyses were performed in SAS 9.4 (Cary,
NC, USA) and R version 3.5.1. Uncorrected P-values <
0.05 were regarded as statistically signiﬁcant and false
detection rates [48] were calculated for the analyses of
differential abundance and inferred functional modules. For
all parametric tests, normality and homogeneity of the data
were visually inspected using diagnostics plots.
Descriptive data are presented as means and standard
deviations for normally distributed data or as medians and
25th and 75th percentiles for non-normally distributed data.
Between-group comparisons of compliance with the
exercise prescription were performed using analysis of
variance with the outcome as a function of group. In the
case of a signiﬁcant F-test, post hoc t-tests were performed
to evaluate differences between groups. Right skewed data
were log-transformed to achieve normality. Data for the
exercise frequency did not meet the assumption of nor-
mality after transformation and was analysed using a one-
way ANOVA on ranks (Kruskal–Wallis H-test).
Statistical tests for longitudinal changes to phenotypical
outcomes, diet, alpha diversity, functional modules and
differential abundance were analysed as repeated measures
by using linear mixed models (phenotypical outcomes, diet
and alpha diversity) or permuted generalized linear mixed
models with a binominal distribution and a logarithmic link
function (differential abundance and inferred functional
modules) with the mean vector as a function of factorial
time and a group-by-time interaction. Adjustment for sex
effects at baseline were included for outcomes where sex
differences were expected, see the individual ﬁgures and
tables for information on this. No group effect was included
at baseline. The linear mixed models included a repeat
statement (time) on the participant level. The restricted
maximum likelihood estimation method was used. An
unstructured covariance structure was speciﬁed and the
Kenward and Rogers denominator degrees of freedom
approximation applied to compute the denominator degrees
of freedom. A similar model was used to investigate
potential associations between key phenotypical outcomes
and alpha diversity; including alpha diversity and an inter-
action between alpha diversity and time, but no group level.
The generalized linear mixed models were speciﬁed with a
random intercept on the participant level. The maximum
likelihood with adaptive quadrature (50 quadrature points)
estimation method was used. The data for most genera and
some functional modules did not ﬁt the assumptions for the
negative binominal distribution; 1000 replicates of the
datasets with permuted group allocation were created and
analyzed for null-hypothesis testing. Subsequently the
estimates obtained using the original and the replicate

permutation test (vegan R package).
Results
Participants
A total of 88 participants were included in the analysis;
of these 70 had a full set of faecal samples, 17 had
samples from two time points and one had only 1 sample.
Baseline characteristics of the participants are available
in Table 1.
Exercise compliance and key phenotypical outcomes
All exercise groups had comparable total exercise energy
expenditures during the intervention (median 93–94% of
the prescribed) (Supplementary Table S1). As intended by
study design, VIG exercised at a higher intensity compared
with MOD. Cardiorespiratory ﬁtness increased and fat mass
decreased in all exercise groups after 3 and 6 months
intervention compared with CON (Table 2).
Diet
The daily energy intake did not change signiﬁcantly in any
of the exercise groups, though a numerical reduction was
observed in MOD at 6 months, which was driven by a
decreased intake of fat (Table 2). Likewise, the relative
daily energy intake from fat was lower in MOD compared
with CON at 3 months and tended to be so at 6 months,
whereas the relative daily energy intake from non-ﬁbre
carbohydrates was increased in MOD at 3 months.
Gut microbiome composition
A total of 6,269,045 16S rRNA reads were generated from
faecal samples of protocol completers, with a median of
24,599 (21,382; 26,978) reads for CON, 23,931 (21,314;
26,581) for BIKE, 23,801 (21,345; 26,582) for MOD and
24,529 (22,160; 27,270) for VIG. The baseline microbiome
composition is shown in Supplementary Table S2.
In order to investigate the impact of the exercise inter-
ventions on the gut microbiome, alpha diversity was ﬁrst
assessed. A pattern emerged showing a potential increase in
observed richness of ASVs in the leisure-time exercise
groups, especially MOD at 3 months (13%, CI95 −0.5–26,
P = 0.062) and VIG at 6 months (10%, CI95 −1–21, P =
0.065) (Fig. 1) (Supplementary Table S3). Taking the
evenness of the distribution of ASVs into account using
Shannon’s diversity index further enhanced the pattern for
the VIG group, resulting in a statistically signiﬁcant
increase at 3 months (5% CI95 1–9%, P = 0.012), indicat-
ing that the changes in alpha diversity in this group could be
somewhat driven by a redistribution of ASVs within the
participants. No associations were found between alpha
diversity
and
cardiorespiratory
ﬁtness
and
fat
mass,
respectively, (P > 0.1 for all tests).
The principal coordinates analysis of community struc-
ture assessed by Bray–Curtis dissimilarity revealed large
intrapersonal variations over time, with no apparent uniform
group-speciﬁc trend (Fig. 2) (Supplementary Fig. S1).
When community structures were assessed by Bray–Curtis
dissimilarity, all exercise groups showed greater change
over time when compared with CON; both from baseline to
3 months and from baseline to 6 months (Fig. 3). This effect
was less pronounced when the community structure was
determined using weighted UniFrac (Fig. 3), indicating that
the changes were primarily driven by changes in relative
abundances rather than changes in the phylogenetic com-
position. Based on the PERMANOVA we found no sig-
niﬁcant temporal changes to the average community
structure in the exercise groups when compared with CON
(Supplementary Table S4). The dispersion of the data was
comparable across time both for Bray–Curtis dissimilarity

94% (Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion
Our results suggest that structured exercise in previously
physically inactive free-living humans can induce subtle
changes to the community structure of the gut microbiota,
with decreased heterogeneity following vigorous intensity
exercise. Furthermore, leisure-time exercise, but not active
commuting by bike, might induce minor changes to the
alpha diversity and an upregulation of the metabolic
potential of the gut microbiota. The changes to the

From a microbiome centric point of view, the few sig-
niﬁcant changes in the BIKE group are interesting, espe-
cially considering the effects observed for the non-
microbiome outcomes and the similar exercise intensity
and dose compared with MOD. One potential explanation
for the diverging results lies within the effects on diet of the
leisure-time exercise interventions, especially within the
MOD group. Diet is a known driver of changes in the gut
microbiota [53] and seems to explain part of the differences
in the gut microbiome observed between physically active
and inactive people [27, 29, 30]. Exercise intensity and
modality have been suggested to affect appetite and dietary
intake [18], which could explain our ﬁndings and also
indicate that the effects of exercise on the gut microbiota in
free-living humans might depend on changes in diet. Our
aim was to assess the impact of prescribing exercise to
people with overweight or grade 1 obesity in an everyday
life setting and we deliberately did not standardize dietary
intake, neither during the intervention nor in relation to test
periods. While this has increased the external validity of our
ﬁndings, it also made it difﬁcult to disentangle the potential
effects of exercise per se from the effects caused by exercise
induced changes in diet. In the future, it would be inter-
esting to perform controlled exercise interventions with and
without clamped diets.
The rigorous exercise intervention and controlled design
mean that our study provides important information, in
particular given the paucity of data on the effects of exercise
on gut microbiota in combination with the interest that this
ﬁeld generates. Still, our study is not without limitations;
being the ﬁrst randomized controlled trial investigating the
effects of habitual exercise on the human gut microbiome
meant that sample size calculations were not possible for the
microbiome related outcomes, but rather the trial was
powered to detect changes in other outcomes [38]. Our
results indicate that more participants are needed when
assessing exercise induced changes to the human gut
microbiome than what is needed for more traditional
outcomes.

Conclusion
Our ﬁndings indicate that exercise may induce minor
changes to the human gut microbiota, and that the effect
depends on the exercise regime and could be secondary to
changes in diet. In addition, the exercise related improve-
ments in key phenotypical outcomes were not dependent on
changes to the microbiota. Larger studies with and without
clamped diets are needed to further elucidate the effect of
exercise on the human gut microbiota.
