ABSTRACT: Lipoprotein subfractions are biomarkers for the
early diagnosis of cardiovascular diseases. The reference method,
ultracentrifugation, for measuring lipoproteins is time-consuming,
and there is a need to develop a rapid method for cohort
screenings. This study presents partial least-squares regression
models developed using 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)
spectra and concentrations of lipoproteins as measured by
ultracentrifugation on 316 healthy Danes. This study explores,
for the ﬁrst time, diﬀerent regions of the 1H NMR spectrum
representing signals of molecules in lipoprotein particles and
diﬀerent lipid species to develop parsimonious, reliable, and
optimal prediction models. A total of 65 lipoprotein main and
subfractions were predictable with high accuracy, Q2 of >0.6, using
an optimal spectral region (1.4−0.6 ppm) containing methylene and methyl signals from lipids. The models were subsequently
tested on an independent cohort of 290 healthy Swedes with predicted and reference values matching by up to 85−95%. In addition,
an open software tool was developed to predict lipoproteins concentrations in human blood from standardized 1H NMR spectral
recordings.
■INTRODUCTION
Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are still the leading cause of
mortality and morbidity.1,2 Although the trend in CVD
mortality has plateaued in many countries,1 ∼18 million
people die annually worldwide due to CVD.3 Blood lipoprotein
particles (LP) play an important role in the development of
this pathology and serve as diagnostic markers. Total
cholesterol (chol) in blood and in low-density lipoprotein
particles (LDLchol) have long been used as markers of risk for
CVD.4,5 Lipoproteins are micellar-like particles with heteroge-
neous density and size, made up primarily of lipids and
proteins.6 The inside of the typical LP is composed of
triglycerides (tg) and cholesterol esters (chole). The outer shell
is composed of free cholesterols (fchol), phospholipids (phosl),
apolipoprotein A (apoA), or apolipoprotein B (apoB). During
fasting, LP in human blood can be divided into four main
fractions based on density and size: very low-density
lipoproteins (VLDL), intermediate-density lipoproteins
(IDL), low-density lipoproteins (LDL), and high-density
lipoproteins (HDL). VLDL particles are the largest with the
lowest density, and HDL are the smallest particles with the
highest density. The analytical methods used to determine LP
in human blood include ultracentrifugation (UC), gel
electrophoresis, high-performance liquid chromatography
(HPLC), and numerous assays. However, the deﬁnition of
subfractions diﬀers between the various separation techniques.
The LP are not clearly distinct groups of particles, but rather
form a distribution of particles diﬀering in size and density, and
in lipid and protein composition.7 For example, UC can
separate seven subfractions of LDL, while HPLC can separate
ﬁve subfractions, and these subfractions are not directly
comparable. Ultracentrifugation has become the clinical
reference method thanks to its capacity to better separate LP
subfractions.6 However, not all aspects of density and/or size
of all subfractions are fully determined, and their biochemical
function and physiological roles in human metabolism are only
partly understood.
A promising analytical method for the rapid quantiﬁcation of
LP in human blood plasma is proton (1H) nuclear magnetic
resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. In 1991−1992, Otvos et al.
demonstrated that plasma 1H NMR spectra and curve ﬁtting

subfractions of LP.8 This seminal work was greatly expanded
by Ala-Korpela et al., who investigated multiple NMR eﬀects
that can inﬂuence the quantiﬁcation of LP, including spectral
regions and time-domain versus frequency domain solu-
tions.9,10 In 2005, Petersen et al. applied a more pragmatic
multivariate data analytical approach, partial least-squares
regression (PLSR),11 for quantifying LP main and subfractions
by regressing the reference values measured by UC against the
lipid region (5.7−0.2 ppm) of the 1H NMR spectra (600
MHz).12 This approach was soon applied by others, and there
are now more than 20 studies demonstrating LP quantiﬁcation
by combining
1H NMR spectroscopy with a reference
measurement method, predominantly UC.6 More recently, it
has become clear that optimization and standardization of the
1H NMR measurement protocols have paramount importance
for reliable, accurate, and comparable LP quantiﬁcations across
diﬀerent laboratories and allow data fusion across cohorts.13
Consensus regarding standardization is that a high-resolution,
typically 600 MHz, NMR spectrometer has to be used for the
measurement of one-dimensional (1D) nuclear Overhauser
eﬀect spectroscopy (NOESY)
1H NMR spectra of blood
plasma or serum samples. The main advantage of using the 1D
NOESY pulse sequence is that it is fast, robust, simple and thus
suitable for standardization.
This study shows the development and extensive validation
of PLSR models to predict concentrations of LP main and
subfractions using blood plasma
1H NMR spectra and
corresponding LP data measured by UC for more than 300
volunteers. Furthermore, the study demonstrates a newly
developed software, namely, Signature Mapping (SigMa) to
predict LP in human blood plasma or serum from 1H NMR
spectra in future studies. The datasets and the software have
been made freely available to the public. The 1H NMR spectra
were measured using the most recent standard operating
procedures (SOP) covering blood collection, sample handling,
and NMR data acquisition, which have been published
previously.13,14 To the best of our knowledge, this study is
the ﬁrst to illustrate the complexity and prediction perform-
ances of PLSR models for a wide range of LP subfractions. In
addition, this study, for the ﬁrst time, explores and compares
the performance of prediction models using the entire 1H
NMR spectra as well as diﬀerent spectral regions with the
purpose of identifying optimal spectral interval(s) to develop
the simplest, most robust, and best-performing PLSR
prediction models. The prediction of nearly 100 parameters
from a single NMR spectrum raises concerns about the
covariations among the independent variables (reference
parameters) and about the rank of the dependent variables
(the spectra). This study therefore investigated the rank of the
NMR and LP datasets for better understanding the level of
intercorrelations “cage of covariance” between the individual
LP and the information content in the NMR spectra. Finally,
the PLSR models developed were validated by the prediction
of LP concentrations in an independent cohort of 290 healthy
Swedes.15
■EXPERIMENTAL SECTION
The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committees of
the Capital Region of Denmark in accordance with the
Helsinki declaration (H-15008313) and the Danish Data
Protection Agency (2013-54-0522). The Danish cohort
included 316 subjects recruited from the COUNTERSTRIKE
cohort. Subjects included 206 females (51 ± 20 years old) and
110 males (57 ± 20 years old) with a mean body mass index
(BMI (kg/m2)) of 25 (±4) for females and males. The mean
values for systolic and diastolic blood pressures (mm Hg) were
124 (±15), and 76 (±10), respectively, for females, and 130
(±17) and 77 (±11), respectively, for males. All subjects were
apparently healthy and without a diagnosis of any form of
cardiovascular disease or diabetes, reporting no chronic
gastrointestinal disorders, and not receiving antibiotic treat-
ment within 3 months of starting the study, or using pre- or
probiotic supplements within 1 month of starting the study. All
subjects visited The Department of Nutrition, Exercise and
Sports at the University of Copenhagen, where blood samples
were taken and anthropometric and clinical parameters were
recorded. Fasting blood samples were collected in vacutainers
containing the anticoagulant reagent ethylenediaminetetra-
acetic acid (EDTA). Plasma was separated after blood sample
collection and 500 μL aliquot was stored in cryovials at −80
°C until analysis.
Details of the UC-based LP quantiﬁcation (milligram per
deciliter, mg dL−1),16 measurement of 1H NMR spectra on
human blood plasma samples,13 and details of the spectral data
processing and PLSR model development are given in the
Supporting Information. One-dimensional 1H NMR spectra
were measured at the Department of Food Science (University
of Copenhagen) using a Bruker Avance III 600 MHz NMR
spectrometer equipped with a 5 mm broadband inverse RT
(BBI) probe, automated tuning, and matching accessory
(ATMA) and cooling unit BCU-05. The spectrometer was
equipped with an automated sample changer (SampleJet,
Bruker BioSpin) with sample cooling (278 K) and preheating
stations (298 K), where samples were stored at 278 K and
measured within 72 h. Phase and baseline-corrected 1H NMR
spectra were then imported to the SigMa software,2 scaled to
the electronic reference to access in vivo concentrations
(ERETIC) signal,17 and aligned toward the alanine’s doublet
(1.507−1.494 ppm) using icoshift.18 Prior to PLSR analysis,
subjects with LP concentrations below the limit of detection
(LOD) or with missing values were removed. This led to
diﬀerent numbers of subjects used in diﬀerent PLSR models.
An optimal number of latent variables (LVs) was selected by
ﬁtting 1−20 LV models to the training samples (70%) using
10-fold cross-validation and 10 times Monte Carlo repetitions.
Then, the optimal number of LV, which provided the lowest
root-mean-square error of cross-validation (RMSECV), was
selected for each PLSR model and used for the subsequent
predictions. Thus, a total of 640,200 PLSR models (33 NMR
spectral intervals × 97 LP variables × 20 LVs × 10 cross-
validations) were developed in this study. It should be noted
that a few subjects (up to seven) whose LP values were
predicted with a large error were regarded as “X−Y relation”
outliers and were removed from the training models. A PLSR
calibration model was then recalculated and tested on
independent subjects (30%) that were not used in the training
model optimization. The ﬁnal PLSR calibration models were
also tested to predict LP variables, plasma tg and chol, in the
independent Swedish cohort including 290 subjects.15 All data
analyses were performed in MATLAB (version R2016b, The
Mathworks, Inc.).

■RESULTS
Overview of the Implemented Workﬂow. Figure 1
shows an overview of the workﬂow implemented to predict LP
in human blood using 1H NMR spectra and UC data as
response variables by applying PLSR modeling. As described
previously,13 the NMR spectra were scaled to the ERETIC
signal positioned at 15 ppm, equivalent to 10 mmol L−1
protons, which minimizes experimental variations across
laboratories, and aligned toward the doublet of alanine
(1.507−1.494 ppm) using icoshift.18 It should be noted that
any additional spectral alignment aﬀecting the methyl (0.92−
0.8 ppm) and methylene (1.4−1.2 ppm) regions may
deteriorate the original chemical shifts of lipid signals and
impair LP quantiﬁcation. Based on a priori knowledge,19 13
spectral regions representing signals of blood lipids and
triglycerides were identiﬁed and their predictive performance
was compared. Each of these datasets, and combinations
thereof, were subsequently used to develop PLSR models to
identify the optimal region for LP prediction (Figure 1). An
individual training PLSR model was developed for each LP
variable and evaluated on test samples and externally validated
using the independent Swedish cohort.
Optimal Regions of the
1H NMR Spectra for Lip-
oprotein Quantiﬁcation. When performing PLSR on a large
1H NMR spectra (>16k variables), the selection of an optimal
spectral region is important for developing parsimonious
models with less noise and fewer interferences. Therefore, the
entire 1H NMR spectral was used for PLSR models either as is
(after removing residual water or EDTA signals) or divided
into 13 regions representing resonances of LP molecules.
These regions include the signal of the methyl group (C18) of
cholesterol (0.75−0.65 ppm), methyl (0.92−0.8 ppm),
methylene (1.4−1.2 ppm), and methine (5.40−5.24 ppm)
protons of diﬀerent LP molecules (Figure S1). Each spectral
region was explored either alone or in combination with other
regions, with and without interfering signals, resulting in a total
of 33 NMR datasets to be tested for regression performance.
This approach, testing one region alone and as a part of
combination with other regions, was performed to identify the
spectral interval which provides the most reliable, accurate, and
simple PLSR model for predicting LP particles. The smaller
spectral region often results in better PLSR models due to less
interfering compounds, while larger regions experience more
complex interactions of signals, thus complicating the
information retrieval.
The concentrations of 65 of the 97 LP were found to be
predictable from 20 of the 33 investigated NMR datasets
(Figure 2a and Table S1). A criterion for an acceptable
prediction performance of the PLSR model was a prediction
accuracy, Q2 > 0.6, for test set samples. Q2 is a statistical
measure of prediction accuracy often used in PLSR modeling20
and deﬁned as Q2 = (1 −PRESS/SS), where PRESS is the
predicted residual error sum of squares and SS is the sum of
squares of actual LP concentrations.
The ﬁve spectral regions (regions 7−10, 14 in Figure 2b)
representing the main signals of the LP, including methyl and
methylene protons as well as methyl of cholesterol, showed the
best prediction results, with Q2 > 0.6 for all 65 LP. Overall, the
prediction performances of all ﬁve regions were similar, with a
mean Q2 of ∼0.84 and a coeﬃcient of variation (CV) of ∼13%
for the test set prediction. However, for a few LP, the

Despite comparably high LP prediction performances of
regions 7−10 and 14, region 9 (1.4−0.6 ppm, later referred to
as the LP region) proved to be an optimal region, showing
consistently high prediction performances for all 65 LP. The
LP region is also the simplest one to extract from the entire
spectra, and unlike other regions, it does not require the
removal of interferences (non-LP molecules). Thus, the use of
the LP region minimizes complications that may arise in new
cohorts dealing with a large number of spectra with possible
chemical shifts, unforeseen signals, or a high spectral
complexity, which may impair reliable LP predictions across
laboratories. Thus, the LP region was the ﬁnal dataset used for
the development of PLSR models for LP prediction.
Prediction Performance of PLSR Models Using the LP
Region (1.4−0.6 ppm). Concentrations of 65 LP including
main and subfractions were predictable from the 1H NMR
spectral region of 1.4−0.6 ppm (region 9; Figure 2b) with Q2 >
0.6 (Table S2). The PLSR models varied in terms of their
prediction performance and complexity. From 4 to 17 LV were
required to obtain optimal models. More than 40 models
require 10 or less LV. Only one LP (LDL4chol) needed 15 LV,
and three LP (apoB in LDL3, LDL4, and LDL6) needed 17
LV. The PLSR models for total concentrations of LP
molecules in plasma and of the main fractions were found to
be less complex than the corresponding models for the
subfractions. Models for phoslp were generally less complex
than the models for chol or apoB. For example, in the same
subfraction, LDL1, the phoslp predicting PLSR model required
7 LV while the chol and apoB models required 15 and 17 LV,
respectively. No clear trend explaining model complexity by LP
molecular type, particle type, or particle size was observed.
However, it is assumed that the number of LV’s in PLSR
models is mainly determined by three factors: the chemical
complexity of the LP (number of representative NMR signals
and their resolution), the variation range present in a cohort
(concentration span of the individual LP), and the presence of
spectral interferences (overlap of signals of LP and non-LP
molecules).
Figure 3 shows the PLSR predictions of tg, chol, phoslp, and
apoA (see Figure S2). Concentrations of all seven LP
molecules, tg, chol, fchol, chole, phoslp, apoA, and apoB in
blood plasma were predictable and showed test set Q2 and
cross-validation (CV) values of 0.87−0.97 and 3−6%,
respectively (Table S2). A cumulative concentration of all
molecules in main LP fractions (e.g., chol_main_fraction =
VLDLchol + IDLchol + LDLchol + HDLchol) was also
predicted with high accuracy (Q2 = 0.70−0.97, CV = 4−
8%). Models performed slightly worse in predicting LP
molecules in each main fraction separately (e.g., VLDLchol)
than for cumulative concentrations. For example, Q2 and CV of
PLSR models for chol in VLDL, IDL, LDL, and HDL ranged
between 0.87−0.94 and 7−20%, respectively, while for total
chol in main fractions, Q2 was 0.95 and CV was 5%. Similar
trends were observed for tg, chole, phoslp, apoA, and apoB
molecules. Consistent for all seven LP molecules, the
prediction performances of the models for the main fractions
were better than those for their corresponding subfractions.

In summary, a total of 65 of 97 LP were predictable with
reasonable performance (Q2 > 0.6). The prediction of the
remaining 32 LP variables was suboptimal, with a relatively low
test set performance (Q2 of 0.3−0.6). These models were
therefore deemed unreliable and, as a minimum, require
additional data (NMR and corresponding UC data) to be
improved. It is assumed that there are two main reasons
behind these suboptimal predictions: (1) a high uncertainty of
the reference method, UC, related to assay limitations and the
freeze−thaw cycle of plasma samples, and (2) a lack of
variability and/or close to LOD levels of those 32 LP in the
investigated cohort. The uncertainty of the UC method
depends on the molecular type and ranged from 5 to 40%.16
The lowest uncertainties were observed for ApoA (5−15%),
ApoB (6−20%), and chol (5−18%) molecules, while the
highest uncertainties were observed for fchol (8−40%) and tg
(12−30%). The latter is in agreement with our PLSR modeling
results, where these LP molecules were not well predicted
using the
1H NMR spectra. Monsonis-Centelles et al.16
reported an average within-individual coeﬃcient of variation
(WCV) as high as 12−16% for LDL2tg−LDL6tg using fresh
blood plasma samples duplicated from the same volunteer.
They also showed reduced repeatability for tg using frozen
plasma compared to fresh plasma. Given this, it is speculated
that the main reasons behind the relatively low prediction
performances for LDL2tg−LDL6tg are twofold: sample matrix
disruption due to freeze−thaw cycles and a relatively high
uncertainty of the UC measurements. A similar trend was
observed for fchol molecules in the LDL subfractions16 with a
WCV range of 12−24% for LDL1fchol−LDL6fchol, and
subsequently, the corresponding PLSR models developed in
this study showed a Q2 of 0.2−0.4 for test set samples. In
contrast, chol, chole, and phoslp molecules in LDL subfractions
(LDL1−LDL6) were predicted with moderate to high
prediction performance (test set Q2 range of 0.67−0.81)
with few exceptions.
Validation of the Final LP Prediction Models Using an
Independent Cohort. PLSR prediction models developed in
this study were validated on an independent Swedish cohort15
using externally measured 1H NMR spectra. The predicted LP
concentrations were subsequently compared to the actual
concentrations. The Swedish cohort included 1H NMR spectra
of blood serum from 290 healthy subjects (sex: 210 females/80
males; age: 58 ± 11 years old; BMI: 25 ± 2.6 kg/m2)
measured using a protocol similar to that of the present study.
However, the sample preparation procedure was slightly
diﬀerent wherefore the spectra of the Swedish cohort were
aligned and scaled toward the Danish cohort as described
previously.13 Unlike the present study, the LP concentrations
of the Swedish cohort were quantiﬁed using the HPLC
method21 of LipoSEARCH (Skylight Biotech, Inc., Akita,
Japan). Lipoprotein subfractions were thus not directly
comparable and a direct comparison was therefore only
possible for total concentrations of chol and tg in blood. Figure
4 shows scatter plots comparing actual concentrations (mg/
dL) of total chol and tg as measured by HPLC, with predicted
concentrations from the corresponding PLSR models
developed in this study. The total concentrations of chol and
tg were predicted well with a Pearson’s correlation coeﬃcient
(r2) of 0.94 and 0.97, respectively. In the case of total tg, the
RMSE calculated between HPLC- and PLSR-based predicted
values was as low as 8.2 mg/dL with mean (STD) and relative
mean standard deviations (RTD) of 4.6 and 5.1%, respectively.
However, the PLSR-based predicted concentrations of total
chol were underestimated in the majority of samples. Despite a
high correlation between the actual and predicted values, the
presence of the oﬀset resulted in a relatively high RMSE
(23.9). This may be related to systematic diﬀerences between
the two reference methods. The UC method has been shown
to result in denaturation or degradation of some LP
particles,22,23 which might be the reason for the systematic
underestimation of chol particles when using UC-calibrated
PLSR models.

Rank of the LP Region of the 1H NMR Spectra. To
better understand the feasibility of predicting the concen-
trations of 65 LP variables from a relatively small 1H NMR
spectral region of human blood plasma, the LP region (1.4−0.6
ppm), a rank estimation of the NMR data was performed. It is
known that concentrations of many LP particles co-vary in
blood and that biology makes it extremely diﬃcult to break this
covariation. This phenomenon is known as the “cage of
covariance”.24 In practice, this means that some LP prediction
models rely on information related to highly co-varying LP
particles. The rank estimation allows us to evaluate
intercorrelations present between LP variables and compare
them with systematic variation present in the NMR spectra. To
estimate the rank of the NMR spectra and UC data, a principal
component analysis (PCA)-based iterative method25,26 was
employed. The method is based on comparing the eigenvalues
calculated on the original matrix (e.g., NMR data) with those
obtained from the column-wise permuted matrix (see the
Supporting Information). It is assumed that the mathematical
rank estimated in this way approximates the true chemical
variation present in the NMR spectra. The rank of the LP UC
data used in this study was found to be 33 (Figure 5), which
indicates that there are at least 33 independent systematic
variations present in the data. To further investigate this, the
LP data were subjected to a correlation analysis where each LP
variable of the original UC data (YACTUAL) was correlated to all
other LP variables individually, resulting in a 65-by-65
Pearson’s correlation coeﬃcients matrix. The same correlation
analysis was then performed on the predicted UC data
obtained from PLSR models (YHAT).24 The symmetric heat
map showing Pearson’s correlation coeﬃcients between the 65
LP in YACTUAL and YHAT, and the distribution of the correlation
coeﬃcients (Figure S4) shows that the intercorrelations of LP
variables is similar (symmetric) in YACTUAL and YHAT and
ranged between −0.55 and 0.99. The fact that the correlations
are not increased in YHAT compared to YACTUAL is an indication
of a reliable prediction network that is not overﬁtted by
intercorrelations. Clustering of the correlation matrices
revealed positive correlations between diﬀerent LP molecules
within HDL, LDL, and VLDL particles and negative
correlations between VLDL and HDL particles, and to a
lesser extent between LDL and HDL particles.
In contrast to the UC data, the LP region of the NMR
spectra is complex, and despite alignment, some minor signal
misalignments are still present, which complicates an unbiased
rank estimation. Therefore, the rank of the LP region from the
Danish cohort is estimated without and after binning with bin
sizes of 2−32 (Figure 5). The estimated rank of the LP region
gradually decreases as bin size increases, with a notable decline
after a bin size of 11. Interestingly, the bin size of 11 was the
largest bin size that could be used before losing spectral
resolution to the point that the characteristic shape of the
methyl protons between 0.95 and 0.8 ppm was kept intact, and
before the loss of spin coupling information from signals of
amino acids. Thus bin size of 11 was found to be optimal for
reducing spectral misalignments while keeping the shape and

NMR Spectral Signatures of LP Depend on Particle
Size. Unique spectral signatures of LP were investigated within
and between molecular types using a selectivity ratio obtained
from the PLSR models. Selectivity ratio (SR) can be regarded
as a spectral signature responsible for the prediction of a given
LP. The SR matrix consisting of 65 LP was analyzed using
PCA and ANOVA-simultaneous component analysis
(ASCA)27 (Figure S5A), which conﬁrmed that the largest
variation was related to the LP particle size, while molecular
type was not signiﬁcant. The SRs of main fractions across
diﬀerent molecular types were very similar to r2 of 0.89−0.97
(Figures S5B and S6). The main diﬀerence in the SR of the
four main fractions (VLDL, IDL, LDL, and HDL) were ratios
of the three proton populations including methyl (1.4−5.5),
methylene (0.9−1.8), and cholesterol methyl (1) of LP
particles (Figure S5A).
Implementation of Lipoprotein Prediction PLSR
Models in the Signature Mapping (SigMa) Software.
Based on the
1H NMR spectra and LP data, and the
subsequently PLSR models developed in this study, an open-
access software was developed for future use in biomarker
discovery research. It was developed as an extension of the
SigMa software, originally developed to process complex 1H
NMR metabolomics datasets.2,28 The software is able to
predict concentrations (mg/dL) of 65 LP from human blood
plasma/serum 1H NMR NOESY spectra. This requires that
the input spectra are acquired using a protocol for sample
preparation and 1H NMR spectra acquisition similar to the one
used in the present study. Prior to LP prediction SigMa checks
the compatibility of the user spectra by data size correction and
intensity normalization. SigMa initializes by scaling the input
spectra by the ERETIC signal, aligning the spectra toward the
alanine signal at 1.49 ppm, constraining the spectra to the LP
region (1.4−0.6 ppm), and applying the regression equations
developed here. The SigMa software including the LP
prediction module can be freely downloaded from www.food.
ku.dk/foodomics.
■DISCUSSION
The present study describes, for the ﬁrst time, the entire
workﬂow of LP prediction using 1H NMR spectra, including
spectral processing steps, the optimization and validation of
PLSR regression models, model parameters, and complexity,
and their prediction performances on test set and independent
cohort samples. This study shows that a relatively small interval
of the 1H NMR spectra, the LP region (1.4−0.6 ppm), is the
optimal region for LP prediction. The LP region contains not
only the three most important proton populations representing
LP molecules, including methyl groups of cholesterol (0.75−
0.65 ppm), methyl (0.92−0.8 ppm), and methylene (1.4−1.2
ppm) protons of LP molecules, but also signals of a few amino
acids and lactic acid. Spectral regions representing only the
methyl (0.92−0.8 ppm) or methylene (1.4−1.2 ppm) protons
performed signiﬁcantly worse than their counterpart where the
two proton populations were combined. This suggests that the
entire LP proﬁle information is better preserved in the LP
region.
Using the LP region, it was possible to predict
concentrations of 65 of the 97 LP quantiﬁed in the Danish
cohort. Suboptimal PLSR models for the remaining 32 LP can
be explained by relatively high uncertainty of the reference
measurements and/or limitation of the variability in the
cohort. Optimized PLSR models require 4−17 LV, and the
complexity depends on LP particle type; the smaller the
particle size, the more LVs were needed. It can be assumed
that the optimal number of LVs is determined by the
complexity of the signals, the presence of spectral interferences,
the size and heterogeneity of the cohort, and the concentration
span of LP particles. An average diﬀerence in RMSE of the
training and test set models was <5%, which indicates the
robustness of the PLSR models. The models were further
validated externally on an independent Swedish cohort and
showed high accuracy in the prediction of the LP variables that
were comparable between the two cohorts.
In conclusion, this study describes a protocol and open-
access data to build PLSR models for predicting LP
concentration from 1H NMR spectra of human blood plasma
or serum. The PLSR models are optimized to use the most
informative and reproducible spectral region and are based on
a relatively large and heterogeneous cohort. Finally, the PLSR
models were implemented in the SigMa software, which is
freely available, and will be improved/upgraded in the future
when new datasets with coherent 1H NMR spectra and UC
data become available. This will signiﬁcantly increase the
coverage of the LP prediction models.
