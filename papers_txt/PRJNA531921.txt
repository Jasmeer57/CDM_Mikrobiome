Abstract 
19 
Infections caused by multidrug-resistant organisms (MDRO) lead to considerable morbidity and 
20 
mortality. The elderly population residing in nursing homes are a major reservoir of MDRO. Our 
21 
objective was to characterize the fecal microbiome of 82 elderly subjects from 23 nursing homes and 
22 
compare their resistome to that of healthy young persons. Comparisons of microbiome composition 
23 
and the resistome between subjects who acquired MDRO or not were analyzed to characterize specific 
24 
microbiome disruption indices associated with MDRO acquisition. An approach based on both 16S 
25 
rRNA amplicon and whole metagenome shotgun sequencing data was used. The microbiome of the 
26 
study cohort was substantially perturbed, with Bacteroides, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria 
27 
predominating. Compared to healthy persons, the cohort of elderly persons had an increased number, 
28 
abundance, and diversity of antimicrobial resistance genes. High proportions of study subjects 
29 
harbored genes for multidrug-efflux pumps (96%) and linezolid resistance (52%). Among the 302 
30 
antimicrobial resistance gene families identified in any subject, 60% were exclusively detected within 
31 
the study cohort, including Class D beta-lactamase genes. Subjects who acquired MDRO or not had 
32 
significant differences in bacterial taxa; Odoribacter laneus, and Akkermansia muciniphila were 
33 
significantly greater among subjects who did not acquire MDRO whereas Blautia hydrogenotrophica 
34 
predominated among subjects who acquired MDRO. These findings suggest that specific microbiome 
35 
disruption indices may identify persons at high risk of acquiring MDRO.

Running Title 
 
3 
1 
Introduction 
38 
Rates of infections caused by multidrug-resistant organisms (MDRO), including methicillin-resistant 
39 
Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) and multidrug-resistant 
40 
gram-negative bacteria (MDRGN) continue to increase worldwide (Diekema et al., 2019). In the 
41 
United States, data from 2008-2011 showed that over 2 million infections were caused by MDRO 
42 
annually with 23,000 attributable deaths, which is considered a critical public health threat (Marston 
43 
et al., 2016). Current preventive efforts to curtail MDRO infections in the hospital setting focus on 
44 
preventing cross-transmission and acquisition by healthcare workers by donning gowns and gloves and 
45 
improving hand hygiene compliance (Siegel et al., 2004). Since exposure to antimicrobials is among 
46 
the greatest risk factor for the emergence and spread of MDRO (Joint Commission on Hospital 
47 
Accreditation, 2016), decreasing unnecessary antimicrobial use is a major focus. Despite such 
48 
interventions, MDRO rates continue to rise, and therefore novel approaches are needed.  
49 
There is a strong association between imbalances of the intestinal microbiome, also referred as 
50 
dysbiosis, and increased risk of MDRO colonization and infections (Iacob et al., 2019; Pamer, 2016). 
51 
Intestinal domination by Enterococcus spp. and Proteobacteria, defined as >30% of the microbiome,  
52 
increased the risk of vancomycin-resistant enterococcal bacteremia by 9-fold and gram-negative rod 
53 
bacteremia by 5-fold, respectively (Taur et al., 2012). Conversely, particular taxa, such as  
54 
Lactobacillus spp. have been associated with protective effects against MDRO colonization (Araos et 
55 
al., 2016). Since manipulating the gut microbiome presents a potential target for infection prevention, 
56 
greater understanding of its structure and composition can identify subjects at high risk of MDRO 
57 
colonization or infection. For example, a recent randomized double-blinded, placebo-controlled trial 
58 
of a microbiota-based drug, RBX2660, was promising in the prevention of recurrent Clostridioides 
59 
difficile infection (Dubberke et al., 2018). 
60 
 
Rates of MDRO colonization and infection are among the highest in elderly persons residing 
61 
in nursing homes (Mody et al., 2018). In a 5-year study of MDRO colonization and acquisition rates 
62 
among 362 residents with advanced dementia, 46% were colonized with at least one multidrug-resistant 
63 
Gram-negative bacteria (MDRGN) at enrollment, and 36% acquired at least one MDRGN over the 12-
64 
month study period (Mitchell et al., 2014). A better understanding of the overall fecal microbiome 
65 
among the elderly with advanced dementia and developing specific microbiome disruption indices 
66 
(MDI) that can identify subjects at high risk of MDRO acquisition are needed. Since this patient 
67 
population is a major reservoir of MDRO, investigating their fecal resistomes to characterize and 
68 
quantify fecal antimicrobial-resistance (AR) genes is needed (van Schaik, 2015). As such, the goals of 
69 
this study were to characterize the fecal microbiome and resistomes in a cohort of elderly subjects with 
70 
advanced dementia and to identify the MDI associated with MDRO acquisition.

2 
Methods 
73 
In this metagenomic study of subjects with advanced dementia, four analyses were performed.  First, 
74 
the structure and composition of the fecal microbiome were characterized within the entire cohort.  
75 
Second, the diversity and abundance of predicted AR genes in the subjects’ fecal resistomes were 
76 
analyzed and compared to the resistomes of 82 healthy persons reported by Kaminski et al., whose 
77 
fecal samples were sequenced using a similar approach (Human Microbiome Project, 2012; Kaminski 
78 
et al., 2015).  Third, to identify unique characteristics of the fecal microbiome that may predict MDRO 
79 
acquisition, the microbiome composition between residents who acquired MDRO or not, was 
80 
compared. Lastly, the resistomes of study subjects were compared between those who acquired MDRO 
81 
or not. The taxonomical analyses of the fecal microbiomes were performed using both 16S rRNA 
82 
amplicon and whole metagenome shotgun (WMS) sequencing data and results were compared. The 
83 
institutional review board at Rhode Island Hospital, Providence, RI, approved this study. 
84 
2.1 
Patient population and sample collection 
85 
Patient data and rectal samples were obtained from The Study of Pathogen and Antimicrobial Use in 
86 
Dementia (SPREAD), a prospective cohort of 362 residents with advanced dementia in 35 nursing 
87 
homes from the Boston, Massachusetts area, which quantified antimicrobial prescribing patterns for 
88 
suspected infections and the acquisition of MDRO (Mitchell et al., 2014). In this cohort study, data and 
89 
rectal samples were collected at baseline and every three months for up to 12 months or until subject 
90 
death, and included demographics, cause of dementia, comorbidities, functional status, and 
91 
antimicrobial exposures. Inclusion criteria for this study required that subjects from the SPREAD study 
92 
had two or more consecutive rectal samples and were exposed to systemic antimicrobials. MDRO 
93 
acquisition was defined as a negative baseline swab at recruitment and at least one positive subsequent 
94 
swab for MDRO. The follow-up time was defined as the number of days from the baseline swab to the 
95 
first swab positive for an MDRO. A cross-sectional characterization of microbiome disruption indices 
96 
associated with MDRO acquisition, was performed using the rectal swab collected preceding detection 
97 
of MDRO acquisition. For subjects who did not acquire MDRO, the last available rectal swab during 
98 
the study period was analyzed. 
99 
From each subject, sterile double tipped swabs (Starswab II; Starplex Scientific Inc., Ontario, 
100 
Canada) were inserted into the anus. The first swab was used to identify MDRO using culture-
101 
dependent techniques as previously described (Araos et al., 2016; Snyder and D’Agata, 2012), and the 
102 
second was frozen at -80 °C in 20% glycerol for further assessments of microbial communities. MDRO 
103 
identified by culture included VRE, MRSA, and MDRGN. MDRGN were defined as gram-negative 
104 
organisms resistant to ≥3 of the following: ampicillin/sulbactam, or piperacillin/tazobactam, 
105 
ceftriaxone or ceftazidime (ceftazidime only for Pseudomonas aeruginosa), ciprofloxacin, gentamicin, 
106 
or meropenem (Lake et al., 2018). Species identification and susceptibility testing were performed per 
107 
Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute methods (EM100 Connect - CLSI M100 ED29:2019).  
108 
Patient’s characteristics were reported using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were 
109 
described using absolute frequencies with percentages, and continuous variables were described using 
110 
mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR) according to the distribution 
111 
of the variables. Comparisons of clinical and epidemiological variables between subjects that acquired 
112 
MDRO or not were performed using Fisher exact test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for categorical 
113 
variables and continuous variables, respectively. A significance level of P <0.05 was used for all 
114 
statistical tests. All statistical analyses performed for the general characterization of the study 
115 
population were performed using Stata software v. 13.0.

2.2 
Assessment of the fecal microbiomes and resistomes 
117 
Frozen rectal swabs retrieved from the SPREAD study sample repository were thawed and 
118 
immediately placed into 96-well microtiter plate. Fecal DNA was extracted using the PowerSoil DNA 
119 
Isolation Kit (MOBIO, West Carlsbad CA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The 
120 
concentration of extracted DNA was determined by Nanodrop 1000 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham 
121 
MA), and DNA was stored at -20°C until used. Subsequently, samples underwent 16S rRNA amplicon 
122 
sequencing and WMS, as described below. 
123 
2.2.1 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing and analyses  
124 
All samples were amplified and barcoded for multiplex pyrosequencing using primers targeted to the 
125 
V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene under uniform polymerase chain reaction (PCR) conditions 
126 
that included 3 min at 94°C and 45 cycles of 45s at 94°C, 60s at 50°C, and 90s at 72°C with final 
127 
extension for 10 min at 72°C (Caporaso et al., 2010). We used forward primer (AAT GAT ACG GCG 
128 
ACC ACC GAG ATC TAC ACT ATG GTA ATT GTG TGC CAG CMG CCG CGG TAA) that 
129 
includes a 5’ Illumina adaptor, forward primer pad, 2bp linker, and the 515F 16S rRNA primer, and 
130 
reverse primer (CAA GCA GAA GAC GGC ATA CGA GAT NNNNNNNNNNNN –AGT CAG TCA 
131 
G-CC-GGA CTA CHV GGG TWT CTA AT) that includes the Illumina 3’ adapter with a 12-nt error-
132 
correcting Golay barcode, reverse primer pad, 2bp linker and the 806R 16S rRNA primer. PCRs were 
133 
run in triplicate using 0.2µM of the primers, 1µl of template and 1X HotMasterMix (5 PRIME, 
134 
Gaithersburg, MD), and the products were cleaned using a PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen) after pooling. 
135 
Cleaned PCR products were quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen™, Eugene 
136 
OR), then adjusted to optimal molarity, as described (Iizumi et al., 2016). Sequencing was performed 
137 
using the Illumina MiSeq platform in the New York University Langone Medical Center (NYULMC) 
138 
Genome Technology Core. 
139 
For the overall assessment of the structure and composition of the fecal microbiomes and the 
140 
comparison of microbiomes between subjects who acquired MDRO or not, demultiplexed sequences 
141 
were curated and analyzed using the DADA2 pipeline (Callahan et al., 2016). All sequences were 
142 
filtered to truncate the paired reads to 150 nt and eliminate reads with quality values <2. Error rates 
143 
were estimated and corrected by pooling all the reads from the sequencing run, with default parameters. 
144 
The one sample that yielded less than 1,500 reads was excluded from downstream analyses. Taxonomy 
145 
was assigned using the SILVA 123 database. Resulting amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were 
146 
analyzed using the Phyloseq package in R (McMurdie and Holmes, 2013). Within-sample diversity 
147 
(alpha diversity) was estimated by calculating the Shannon diversity index (evenness) and the total 
148 
number of observed ASV (richness) and then compared between subjects that acquired MDRO or not 
149 
using non-parametric testing (Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Correlations between alpha diversity and 
150 
relevant subject characteristics such as age, duration of follow-up and antimicrobial exposure were also 
151 
analyzed based on the Spearman coefficient. Between-sample diversity (beta diversity) was determined 
152 
by estimating UniFrac distances between samples. Between sample distances were visualized using 
153 
principal coordinates analysis and sample-clustering patterns were investigated using the ADONIS test 
154 
according to the metadata of interest. Differentially abundant features between subjects that acquired 
155 
MDRO or not were compared using DESeq2, setting an alpha value £ 0.05 and correcting for multiple 
156 
comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method with a false discovery rate of 5%.

2.2.2 Whole metagenome shotgun sequencing and analyses 
159 
Fecal samples were used for WMS sequencing, with 150-bp paired-end reads (2 x 150), across 1-2 
160 
lanes of an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform. Quality filtering was performed by removing host-
161 
contaminated sequences using KneadData and applying conservative quality threshold criteria of Phred 
162 
score > 20 and minimum sequence length > 105-bp, using Cutadapt (Martin, 2011). Following quality 
163 
filtering, the marker-based tool, MetaPhlan2 (v.2.6.0) was used to profile the composition of microbial 
164 
communities, which utilized a database of ~1 million clade-specific markers from > 17,000 reference 
165 
genomes (Truong et al., 2015). The biomarker identification tool, LEfSe (Linear discriminant analysis 
166 
Effect Size) was used to identify differentially abundant features between subjects that acquired 
167 
MDRO or not, implemented by a non-parametric factorial Kruskal-Wallis sum-rank test using default 
168 
parameters (alpha level of 0.05 and LDA score ³2) (Segata et al., 2011).  
169 
 
To quantify antibiotic resistance protein sequences in the metagenomic data, high-quality 
170 
sequences were aligned against a curated resistome database using the protein marker alignment tool, 
171 
ShortBRED (Short, Better Representative Extract Dataset), with the 151bp antibiotic resistance 
172 
markers used as the alignment reference (Kaminski et al., 2015) and default parameters of >95% 
173 
sequence identity and 95% of the query sequence length. Sample abundance tables were normalized 
174 
by default to Reads Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (RPKM) to account for 
175 
differences in gene length and sampling depth. Figures were generated using R-programming language 
176 
and R-library ggplot2 (Wickham, 2009) and Prism 8. The abundance and diversity of AR genes in the 
177 
study population were compared to the HMP cohort by using AR gene data directly from the 
178 
ShortBRED publication to re-calculate descriptive statistics (Kaminski et al., 2015). Finally, 
179 
differentially abundant AR genes between subjects that acquired MDRO or not were investigated using 
180 
LEfSe with default parameters (Segata et al., 2011).

Results 
182 
3.1 
Characteristics of the study population 
183 
Among 362 subjects in the SPREAD study, 87 subjects, from 23 different nursing homes, met the 
184 
inclusion criteria. Fecal samples from five subjects could not be successfully sequenced, resulting in a 
185 
cohort of 82 subjects. Demographics, clinical characteristics, type of dementia and details on 
186 
antimicrobial exposure are shown in Table 1.  
187 
3.2 
Microbial diversity and composition of the fecal microbiomes of the study population using 
188 
16S rRNA amplicon sequencing data 
189 
Eighty-two samples underwent 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing and yielded 624,291 high-quality 
190 
sequence reads, with a median (IQR) of 7,816 (6,830-8,885) reads per sample. One sample was 
191 
excluded from downstream analysis as it provided less than 1,500 reads. A total of 1,345 ASVs present 
192 
in at least one sample were inferred from the data set. The median microbial diversity (IQR) estimated 
193 
using the Shannon index (evenness) and the observed number of ASVs (richness) among the study 
194 
population was 3.27 (2.67-3.7) and 168 (116-213), respectively. There was no significant correlation 
195 
between the Shannon index and subject age, duration of follow-up, or the number of antimicrobial 
196 
courses before fecal sample collection (P >0.05).  In total, 13 phyla, 70 families, and 245 genera were 
197 
detected (Table 2), with a predominance of taxa associated with human infections. Notably, 
198 
Proteobacteria was the second most abundant phylum and Enterobacteriaceae was the most abundant 
199 
family in the fecal microbiome of the study cohort.

Microbial diversity and composition of the fecal microbiomes of the study population using

WMS sequencing data 
202 
Whole metagenome shotgun sequencing of the decontaminated and quality-filtered set of the 82 rectal 
203 
swabs (4 samples sequenced on a single lane, and 78 on 2 lanes), yielded average sample depth of 6.1 
204

207 
included in downstream analyses. 
208 
Similar to the findings of the 16S rRNA gene-targeted sequencing, overall within-sample 
209 
microbial diversity was low. The median (IQR) Shannon index and the number of observed ASVs were 
210 
2.77 (2.18-3.1) and 102 (83-118), respectively. The fecal microbiome of the study population was 
211 
dominated by Bacteria, representing 95.1% of the microbial community, followed by viral sequences 
212 
(4.3%), Archaea (0.6%) and Fungi (0.02%). Taxa at the Phylum and Family level at mean relative 
213 
abundance >1% and the more prevalent Genera (³75% of samples with a mean relative abundance 
214 
>1%) are shown in Table 2.  Again, a predominance of Proteobacteria and microbiota belonging to the 
215 
Enterobacteriaceae family was observed.

3.4 
Abundance and diversity of AR genes in the fecal microbiomes of the study cohort 
217 
The relative abundance of predicted AR genes in the fecal microbiome of the study population 
218 
normalized to the total number of reads was 114,740 RPKM that grouped into 295 families. The 
219 
majority of AR genes were observed at very low relative abundances, with only 50 AR gene families 
220 
present in relative abundances >1%. The 295 AR gene families were clustered into more inclusive 
221 
superclasses according to their hypothetical phenotype. The content of AR gene superclasses was 
222 
highly heterogeneous. The most abundant AR gene superclasses (overall total number of RPKM) were 
223 
tetracycline ribosomal protection (16,883), RND antibiotic efflux (15,183), MFS antibiotic efflux 
224 
(12,272), aminoglycoside phosphotransferase (8,564), and class A beta-lactamase (8,416) (Figure 1). 
225 
These highly abundant AR gene superclasses were highly prevalent in the study population: every 
226 
subject had at least one tetracycline resistance determinant, 98.8% had at least one AR gene classified 
227 
as class A beta-lactamase, 98.8% as RND antibiotic efflux, 96.3% as MFS antibiotic efflux, and 92.7% 
228 
as aminoglycoside phosphotransferase. Other AR gene superclasses present in >90% of the samples 
229 
included mutational quinolone resistance, rRNA methyltransferase, ABC antibiotic efflux, and gene 
230 
modulating antibiotic efflux. A comprehensive list of the AR genes found in the fecal microbiome of 
231 
the study participants, grouped by family, class, and superclass, is shown in Supplementary Table 2. 
232 
The total abundance of AR genes (RPKM) was not significantly correlated with the observed 
233 
taxonomical diversity. Beta diversity was assessed by estimating the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity index 
234 
between samples according to their AR gene content. The ordination analysis did not show any specific 
235 
clustering pattern according to the clinical metadata examined, which included the presence of 
236 
dementia, congestive heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes mellitus, as well as 
237 
the nursing home of origin. In total, these data indicated that the fecal microbiome of the study 
238 
population carries a large and diverse resistome.

onderdonkii and finegoldi), and Bacteroides spp. (cellulosilyticus, salyersiae, and nordii), as well as 
298 
the Proteobacteria Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and the Actinobacteria Corynebacterium amycolatum. 
299 
Conversely, the relative abundances of Firmicutes such as Enterococcus faecalis, Blautia 
300 
hydrogenotrophica, and Peptoniphilus spBV3AC2, as well as the Bacteroidetes Bacteroides caccae, 
301 
and Synergistetes Synergistes sp. 3_1_syn1 were significantly higher among subjects who acquired 
302 
MDRO (Figure 7). These findings show particular signatures in the composition of the fecal 
303 
microbiome that can be consistently identified among subjects that acquire MDRO or not using 
304 
different sequencing approaches. 
305 
3.7 
Analysis of the fecal resistomes with respect to acquiring MDRO  
306 
Finally, we asked whether or not the a priori abundance of AR was predictive of those who would 
307 
acquire MDROs. In fact, we found that the median (IQR) absolute abundance of AR genes among 
308 
subjects who did not acquire MDRO was significantly higher than in those acquiring MDRO [934 
309 
(625-1997) vs. 761 (540-932), respectively; P= 0.04]. The diversity of AR genes estimated by their 
310 
within-sample richness was similar between the two groups. Beta diversity, as assessed by estimating 
311 
the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity index between samples according to AR gene content did not show any 
312 
specific clustering pattern according to MDRO acquisition or not. Although the rank order of AR gene 
313 
superclasses within each group varied, no differentially abundant features regarding particular AR 
314 
genes were observed between groups at the superclass level.  
315 
4 
Discussion 
316 
Using complementary approaches based on the analysis of 16S rRNA amplicon and WMS sequencing 
317 
data (Schloss, 2018), we conducted a cross-sectional, metagenomic study to evaluate the fecal 
318 
microbiome and its resistome among the elderly with advanced dementia, a population at high-risk for 
319 
MDRO infections. We observed that the microbiome of this cohort was severely dysbiotic and, 
320 
harbored a large number of diverse and unique AR genes compared to healthy controls. Moreover, we 
321 
identified specific microbial signatures among subjects who acquired MDRO compared to those who 
322 
did not acquire MDRO, suggesting that specific gut taxa may predispose to, or protect against, MDRO 
323 
colonization.  
324 
 
The fecal microbiome of our study cohort was characterized by reduced microbial diversity and 
325 
enrichment with pathogenic bacteria, suggesting that significant imbalances occur in the gut 
326 
microbiome of elderly subjects exposed to antimicrobials. Since dysbiosis is known to increase with 
327 
age and antimicrobial exposure (Biagi et al., 2010; Taur et al., 2012), it is not surprising that in our 
328 
elderly population with universal antimicrobial exposure,  fecal microbiome alterations were detected. 
329 
Although Bacteroides and Firmicutes, the predominant phyla among healthy aged persons have been 
330 
reported to represent >90% of the fecal microbiome (Biagi et al., 2010),  these phyla represented only 
331

62.4% in the group we studied. The lower percentage was due to the predominance of Proteobacteria, 
332 
a well-described feature of the microbiome of hospitalized subjects exposed to antimicrobials (Araos 
333 
et al., 2016), and in several disease states (Harris et al., 2016; Taur et al., 2012). High Proteobacteria 
334 
abundance has been associated with an increased risk of subsequent infections caused by gram-
335 
negative bacteria (Taur et al., 2012). This Proteobacteria abundance may explain the predominance of 
336 
gram-negative infections in the elderly population residing in nursing homes (O’Fallon et al., 2009). 
337 
 
The abundance of AR gene families and the number of different AR superclasses in the study 
338 
cohort was four-fold and two-fold higher, respectively, than in the resistome of healthy younger 
339 
persons. Clinically relevant AR genes also were very prevalent. Nearly all of the elderly subjects 
340 
harbored AR genes for multidrug-efflux pumps and cfr, a highly mobile plasmid-mediated gene with 
341 
the potential of conferring resistance to linezolid, was present in 52% compared to only 8.5% of healthy 
342 
controls (Doern et al., 2016). Although the presence of the cfr gene may not have direct clinical 
343 
relevance since it can be harbored by commensal microbiota, it still poses a risk via horizontal gene 
344 
transfer to pathogenic bacteria. More than half of the AR gene families detected were identified only 
345 
in the study cohort, including three-OXA-type Class D beta-lactamases which may confer resistance 
346 
to narrow- and extended-spectrum cephalosporins, and carbapenems (Jeong et al., 2009), and may not 
347 
be detected by standard phenotypic tests (Antunes et al., 2014; Poirel et al., 2010). Current methods 
348 
for assessing the resistome may underestimate the contribution of the anaerobic commensal microbiota 
349 
to the intestinal reservoir of AR genes, and the predominance of Proteobacteria in our cohort indicates 
350 
a relationship of bacterial phylogeny with AR gene distribution consistent with previous data (Feng et 
351 
al., 2018; Gibson et al., 2015). As such, we hypothesize that the enrichment of Gammaproteobacteria 
352 
among subjects who did not acquire MDRO may explain the higher AR gene abundance in this group 
353 
compared to those who acquired MDRO. 
354 
This large and diverse resistome, including genes associated with phenotypic resistance to 
355 
antimicrobials of last resort, such as carbapenems and linezolid, demonstrates that the population of 
356 
elderly persons, residing in long-term care facilities, serve as major reservoirs of AR genes. The 
357 
predominant human gut resistome consists of chromosomally encoded AR determinants, with a 
358 
relatively low capacity for dissemination (Ruppé et al., 2019). However, our finding of highly prevalent 
359 
AR determinants associated with mobile genetic elements, such as cfr and qnr to a lesser extent, 
360 
suggests that this population of elderly subjects with advanced dementia and frequent antimicrobial 
361 
exposure represents a group where opportunities for horizontal gene transmission between commensal 
362 
bacteria and human pathogens are increased (Penders et al., 2013). Ways to better steward 
363 
antimicrobial use in this population become increasingly important (Boutrot et al., 2019).   
364 
 
Comparing differences between the microbiomes of subjects who acquired MDRO or not, we 
365 
observed a significantly greater relative abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila among subjects in the 
366 
latter group. These organisms have been considered biomarkers for a healthy intestine due to an inverse 
367 
relation in abundance and intestinal disease, including Crohn’s and ulcerative colitis (Png et al., 2010).  
368 
A. muciniphila also may have a protective role as efficient mucus layer colonizers outcompeting 
369 
pathogen colonization  (Belzer and de Vos, 2012). Thus, these bacteria may provide a protective role 
370 
against MDRO gut colonization.  
371 
Based on both 16S rRNA and WMS sequencing data, we observed a significantly higher 
372 
relative abundance of Blautia hydrogenotrophica, a human gut acetogen, among subjects who 
373 
subsequently acquired MDRO. This finding contrasts with prior data suggesting that other Blautia 
374 
species (in particular, B. producta) may contribute to human intestinal colonization resistance against 
375 
VRE and C. difficile (Caballero et al., 2017; Jenq et al., 2015). More studies are needed to determine 
376

whether B. hydrgenotrophica may be useful as a microbial index of a dysbiotic microbiome, predicting 
377 
MDRO acquisition. Further investigation is needed to clarify the potential role of other differentially 
378 
abundant features, such as Odoribacter laneus, and their utility as biomarkers associated with the risk 
379 
of MDRO acquisition. 
380 
 
Our study has several limitations. First, we performed a cross-sectional study nested in a 
381 
longitudinal cohort. Therefore, the associations described between the distribution of exposures 
382 
(microbiome features) and outcomes (MDRO acquisition) in our population cannot be considered as 
383 
necessary for the causal chain. Nevertheless, our study describes a unique group of subjects at high-
384 
risk for MDRO acquisition and transmission with implications for improving infection control. Future 
385 
studies should include additional control groups to better understand the role of age and antimicrobial 
386 
exposure on the intestinal microbiome and resistome. Second, metagenomic prediction of AR genes 
387 
has limitations inherent to the method, including falsely positive identifications, and lack of 
388 
information at the functional level, as predictions may not translate into phenotypic antimicrobial 
389 
resistance (Ruppé et al., 2019). However, we ameliorated these problems by using ShortBRED, a 
390 
highly specific tool for predicting protein families including AR genes from WMS data (Kaminski et 
391 
al., 2015). Third, microbiome assessment methods have several weaknesses that may increase the risk 
392 
of misclassification bias. In particular, 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing is subject to primer and 
393 
amplification bias which may lead to misrepresentation of the relative abundances of the community 
394 
members. To overcome some of these limitations, we included a dual approach using 16S rRNA 
395 
amplicon and WMS sequencing data (Munafò and Davey Smith, 2018; Schloss, 2018). Importantly, 
396 
the assessments of the microbiome structure and composition were consistent between methods, but 
397 
differences remained. As such, the variation observed in the analysis of differentially abundant features 
398 
between subjects who acquired MDRO or not may have been determined by the different analytic 
399 
approaches applied to each set of data (DESeq2 and LEfSe for 16S rRNA and WMS data, respectively). 
400 
In conclusion, this study analyzed the fecal microbiome and its resistome in elderly subjects residing 
401 
in nursing homes who were exposed to antimicrobials. Our results revealed that this patient 
402 
population has a substantially dysbiotic microbiome and its resistome is composed of a large and 
403 
diverse set of AR genes. The predominance of several taxa associated with acquiring MDRO or not 
404 
suggests that there may be strategies targeting the microbiome which could prevent MDRO 
405 
acquisition.
