These must be made available to editors and referees at the time of submission and be shared with the scientific community at final publication. This guide describes our policy for sharing them. Scientific Data ’s data policies are compatible with the standardised research data policies set out by Springer Nature , and the requirements of the Data Policy Standardisation and Implementation Interest Group of the Research Data Alliance . Please see these specific policies and contact us if you would like additional advice on how best to meet these requirements for your own data: Data types and rawness Repositories Requirements for initial submission Requirements for later rounds and publication Human Data Data compilations Data preservation Dataset updates Data citation Data types and rawness Scientific Data considers datasets on any area of research – biological, physical, environmental, medical, social, or engineering sciences – however data must be considered suitable for some form of analysis. Objects like raw documents, unannotated images, or purely qualitative observations may only be considered if efforts have been made to support machine readability and analysis. Authors should provide their data at a level 'rawness' that allows it to be re-used in alignment with accepted norms within their community. It may be advantageous to release some types of data at multiple levels to enable wider reuse – for example, proteomics data may best be released as ‘raw’ spectra as well as more processed peptide- or protein-level data – however this is not mandated if the level of 'rawness' is sufficient for some potential use. Authors may submit supplementary information files – including code (also see our code availability policy ), models, workflows and summary tables – however we strongly encourage deposition in repositories as a first preference, especially for primary data, which should not be submitted as supplementary information. All decisions on rawness fall within our general checks to assess the utility of the data, made by reviewers or handling editors. Repositories Although Scientific Data mandates the release of the datasets that accompany our manuscripts, we do not ourselves host data, and require all authors use a repository to host data. Our policies for this may be found on our data repository guidance page . Authors often present more than one kind of data in a Data Descriptor and use more than one kind of repository, so please structure your data in a format that you believe suits its use. Archiving data on personal or laboratory websites or informal cloud platforms is not sufficient for submission of a Data Descriptor unless the self-built platform meets our requirements , however this rule is waived for large datasets (>1 Tb) due to the challenges in finding suitable repositories to host them. Requirements for initial submission For non-sensitive (non-human) data: For first round review we require that data are available for download and review by reviewers via any URL that allows seamless and anonymous access. This can be the final, intended repository link if authors wish to deposit the data from the start, or a temporary location such as cloud storage if you wish to delay or embargo data during the first round of review. If private links or passcodes are being used please ensure the full (temporary) instructions are available in the Data Record section so reviewers can use them and note again they must facilitate practical download. Reviewer links that allow reviewers to see a file catalogue, but not download material, are not supported, such as those provided by NCBI SRA. Users of reviewer links from Zenodo should be aware these are very long strings of characters, which can break due to page breaks or pdf conversion (we suggest using a shortlink service, such as bit.ly, if possible). Generally, while embargoing data during first round peer review is supported, the journal does not require or suggest its use. For sensitive (human, non-anomyised) data: Those sharing sensitive human data that require access controls need to provide a method for seamless and anonymous access by reviewers within a timeframe that aligns with peer review. Download is still required, but this can include sharing a non-sensitive, representative sample of the data in a temporary location, or a method of bypassing access controls, depending on the sensitivity of the data. While the journal keeps records of all invited and agreed reviews we cannot provide additional vetting, supply reviewer names to the authors, or selectively distribute passwords on a per-reviewer basis. Details for temporary access methods should be clearly detailed within the paper, either as a temporary not in the Data Record section, or a cover page appended to the front on the manuscript. Requirements for later rounds and publication From the second round of review onwards we require that data are public and available in a formal data repository that meets our policy , in preparation for any final publication. Sharing is also required for Articles if relevant to the results of the work. During the peer-review process, Editors, Editorial Board Members and referees are asked to evaluate whether the data repository(s) selected by the authors is appropriate and may deem it necessary for authors to archive their data in additional repositories prior to publication. Note that while we aim to address this as early as possible, changes required to comply with our data policy may be requested at any time prior to final acceptance. Human Data Our policy for managing human data is designed to support the sharing of data that cannot be shared openly. We consider sensitive human data to be information from human participants or patients where sharing and use may put those individuals at risk of harm. This is in contrast to data that is simply of human origin but not subject to specific risks. The most common consideration for sensitivity is anonymity . Any partial or non-anonymised data should be assessed against the risk of harm to individuals that may result from its disclosure and use. Of particular risk are identifiable data shared in combination with sensitive fields, such as religious beliefs, racial identity, health information, sexual identity, or political views. Data from minors or other vulnerable groups may also be higher risk. All authors who are submitting a human-derived dataset must complete our Human Data Checklist and provide it as a related manuscript file during the submission process. Common sharing routes Fully open data Data are fully anonymised or exhibit extremely low risks of re-identification and/or do not contain any sensitive fields* No controls on use are needed Open download without controls, as per our standard repository policy Open licences (CC-BY / CC0, or equivalent) Controlled access data Data are not fully anonymised and/or there is risk to participants/patients based on disclosure of any sensitive human data field. All users must register to access data. Identities may need to be validated and the names and institutions of users accessing the data usually recorded A Data Usage Agreement (DUA) or other contact stipulates the limitations on use, and all users sign it *Note: In rare cases participants can consent to open sharing of their identifiable data, but this is only supported where participants can be shown to be fully informed about potential risks; identification is scientifically necessary or unavoidable; and no other sensitive fields are shared. Otherwise, controlled access is expected. Secondary human datasets Both the above options apply to data that have been first shared by the data owner – primary data sharing of material that is not previously available for public download. Secondary data sharing, where datasets are derived from data that is already available in public databases, is not usually subject to our human data sharing policy as the responsibility for data management sits with a third party. Users must ensure they have complied with the terms of the primary owner. The most important aspect to check is whether you have the right to re-distribute the data, especially if it is under a DUA containing a non-derivative or non-disclosure clause. Ethics approval for secondary use is not required by this journal but may be a requirement of the primary data owner or your institution. Expectations and checks for all human datasets (open or controlled access) Consent has been obtained from human participants or patients OR an institutional review board (IRB) has waived the requirement. Waivers are only possible for fully anonymised, non-biometric data, collected from non-vulnerable adults where it was not possible to obtain consent at the time. Waivers cannot be used to share data for children or vulnerable adults, regardless of the sensitivity/anonymity of the data, meaning consent for data sharing from a suitable parent, guardian, or responsible adult must always be obtained in these cases. An IRB/ethics board has given approval to perform the study AND share the data Expectations and checks for controlled access data only We check whether controls are needed. i.e. why is open data sharing is not possible. Scientific Data follows the general rule of “as open as possible, as closed as necessary”. Openess is assumed as a starting point with restrictions added when there are tangible risks to participants/patients. This risk is dependent on the data type (the files) being shared and the presence of either direct identifiers (e.g. genomes, names, faces) or multiple indirect identifiers that reduce the size of a cohort to a potentially identifiable group (e.g. locations, gender, medical characteristics, occupations, etc). Data which are identifiable AND contain sensitive fields (e.g. political or religious beliefs, personal finance data, health data, sexual or gender identity) are considered the most sensitive. If the Editor cannot reasonably deduce what sensitivities or risks apply researchers may be asked to explain or share more openly. Conversely, if the editor considers the data to be of greater sensitivity than open sharing allows then authors may be requested to change to a controlled access method. We check the controlled access process We recommend that practical controls and application processes should be limited to user registration, identity validation, and ensuring the DUA has been pro-actively signed or agreed to, rather than manual assessments being made of individual suitability. We generally expect all users who can provide verifiable identity information and agree to the terms will be given access to the data and will not be screened on aspects unrelated to data protection, such as their research question, institution type, or nationality. This means it is considered atypical for case-by-case access decisions to be made on individual users, such as via a data access committee. Where manual controls cannot be avoided it should be clearly explained to readers in the paper what questions they will be asked and how decisions will be made. The Editor may ask for hypothetical examples of what the committee expects to pass or fail to test this. Data Usage Agreements (DUAs) typically contain these minimum terms While these may vary in wording and specific requirements, most DUAs state users: Do not attempt to re-identify participants, either directly or via cross-reference with other sources Do not re-distribute the data to any other user not named or signed in the agreement (all users need to obtain the data directly, signing individually or in groups). Should only use data for internal research/analysis only so any outputs or derivatives do not breach the “do not redistribute” clause and there is no external interaction with participants. This implicitly prohibits direct commercial uses (e.g. selling the data) but not commercial R+D (e.g. developing a drug, tool, etc) Promise to host/manage the data securely, with sufficient controls, training, etc Other standard clauses such as: descriptions of liability; what to do in the event of a data breach; intellectual property notifications (limited to re-iterating existing or standard protections); the requirement to cite the dataset/paper; or any clause included within standard open licences such as CC-BY, are allowed. Clauses that mandate users provide proof of training should only do so where training is known to practically reduce participant risk and is practically accessible to the intended (international) audience. We may request terms that do not look relevant to data protection be modified or removed For example: claims of ownership of derived i.p. or other outputs; requirements to share publications with the data owner in advance; requests for co-authorship or other attribution beyond standard citation; limiting commercial R+D; or any other term not clearly relevant to participant protection. Limitations on use and consent management The journal strongly recommends that promises made to patients/participants regarding limitations on use are relevant and restricted to preventing uses that cause specific harms, such as data disclosure or identification. Beyond that, we recommend that patients/participants are asked to consent to any legitimate, safe, internal use within an academic or commercial research setting. We do not recommend usage-specific consent statements beyond this, e.g. academic-only; brain research only; etc as this places an unnecessary burden on compliance checks, and could limit the usage or utility of data, to enforce promises that may not be relevant to patients/participants safety or practically affect them in other ways. In cases where initial consent was considered too restrictive or onerous to support safe data sharing for any legitimate research use, we recommend participants are contacted for re-consent or data owners apply for a retrospective waiver of consent where institutional review boards consider it to be safe and appropriate. Note this is only possible for fully anonymised data from non-vulnerable adults. Data preservation Scientific Data believes that researchers, institutions, journals and data repositories have a shared responsibility to ensure long-term data preservation, and we encourage authors to select data repositories with this goal in mind. Authors must commit to preserving their datasets, on their own laboratory or institutional servers, for at least five years after publishing a Data Descriptor. If, during that time, the repository to which the data were originally submitted disappears or experiences data loss, we may ask the authors to upload the data to another repository and publish a correction or update to the original Data Descriptor. If authors remove their data from the original public repository or change access criteria in a manner that is inconsistent with the published Data Descriptor, we may ask authors to publish a correction or, in extreme circumstances, to retract the Data Descriptor publication. Data compilations For datasets compiled from existing literature, Scientific Data ’s policy is that compilers (creators of the secondary compilation dataset and authors of the associated Data Descriptor) are not required by the journal to ask permission from the original authors to extract small amounts of numerical information or other fields. Expected practice is to attribute the original work via citation. Care must be taken to ensure all extracted fields are accurate and that the DOI, URL, (or equivalent) of the source article is clearly visible at the data level. A common construction is a tabular dataset with one row per data point and one column for references/DOIs. This policy does not apply for wholesale reproduction of text, images, video, or other elements that may infringe copyright to reproduce. These cannot typically be re-shared without permission unless the primary work is under a permissive licence (e.g. CC0 or CC-BY). We advise all authors to consult with rights experts within their institutions to decide what forms of re-distribution are legally allowed within their local countries. In addition to listing data sources within the main data file it is highly recommended that credit is provided to the original authors of the work via a citation in the Data Descriptor itself. This can be achieved via a suitable sentence in the Methods section such as "All literature sources for the dataset are listed in the data file and cited in this paper", followed by the relevant reference numbers. We recommend this is done up to a maximum of 500 references, with longer lists not possible due to typesetting constraints (though please still cite those items in the data file). This is only suggested for sources with formal metadata (e.g. literature articles, usually with DOIs). No records in secondary data compilations should be marked as "unpublished" without a listed source. Aggregated datasets must be compiled from existing, visible sources to ensure there is some provenance to the information. It is at the compilers' discretion on whether sources are peer reviewed publications, preprints, or other materials based on the suitability of the source for their needs and assessment on the trustworthiness of the source. Any included data that has not been recorded in an existing public document is considered primary, meaning authors must explain full methods for how they were collected in the Data Descriptor (with a reference in the data file such as "newly reported in the associated data descriptor"). The creators of these data points would typically be listed as authors on the paper, or otherwise formally acknowledged, as with any first disclosure. Where viable document sources are available, the journal does not support the policy of listing the authors of those primary sources as authors of the secondary compilation, in the same way the data sources of literature reviews and book chapters are managed. Attribution, not authorship, is the required practice as outlined above. Note this policy does not apply if those authors made a new, active contribution of the work, or for efforts such as crowd sourcing or citizen science where all data points are assumed to be newly disclosed. It is up to the project leaders of those works to decide what form of credit is appropriate in those cases. Similar considerations should be made for facts and data extracted from other documents, such as news articles, print works, patents, or grey literature. Note that while human information taken from these sources should be treated with sensitivity and tact, especially for reports of sensitive topics such a health information, political stances, or crimes, information taken from established news stories do not typically fall within our human data policy if no new information is being disclosed. Conversely, we consider social media outputs, such as tweets, data related to handles, and other posts, are classed as human data and cannot typically be indexed or re-published without participant consent. This is applied regardless of the copyright status of the material or terms and conditions set by the platform owner, although those may also restrict use. This is due to a grey area of some forms of social media output being reasonably considered personal data or correspondence, or not knowingly made publicly, even if available to all. Some exemptions may be granted for clear outputs such as corporate communications or 'official' accounts, although providing a clear distinction may be challenging. Re-sharing data from more transactional outputs, such as classified advertisements, or messaging apps is rarely supported even when such outputs are publicly available. Aggregated statistics or data considered non-personal (e.g. counts of tweets) are typically allowed as non-sensitive data. Dataset updates Scientific Data understands that researchers may wish to update datasets after publication but needs to align this use case with the static nature of the scientific literature and time-specific nature of peer review. Authors are asked to only describe finished projects and extant datasets within the main body of the article. We cannot pro-actively assess data or projects that will be completed later, even if these use the same methodology. If updates are planned, or researchers wish to describe an associated continuous resource alongside a static one, we require you deposit a static version of the data to an appropriate repository that represents the version that was peer reviewed. This should be described in the Data Record section, cited in the reference list with a permanent DOI, and not changed or modified post acceptance. Any future or dynamically updated version should be hosted separately from this as via a project-specific resource or website, allowing users to find the latest data. This should be described in the "Usage Notes" section to distinguish it from the version of record. Some repositories may support publication of later updates under new or updated DOIs allowing these to diverge from the cited version. Where such updates are made it should be clear on any new dataset page how these data differ from the original version (e.g. via a update log, or similar) and to note that they were created after review of the peer reviewed version. We encourage authors to discuss the versioning mechanisms available with the maintainers of the repository they have chosen to host their data. No additive updates to papers can be made to accommodate data updates, e.g. by modifying DOIs or linking out to newer sources. Corrective updates to articles and/or datasets are acceptable as per our standard corrections policy. Users of our integrated peer review system, where depositions are made to Figshare via the manuscript submission system on your behalf, will not be re-assigned admin rights to update the data later. While this policy is set due to technical and resourcing limitations, it aligns with the wider assumption that no updates should be made to the peer-reviewed dataset (again, unless a data correction is required). If you do require ongoing admin rights we suggest an alternative repository is used (see https://www.nature.com/sdata/policies/repositories#general). If Figshare is specifically required, please note the service is available to any user to register via figshare.com. There is no requirement to use the submission system upload method and the final functionality is the same as via the public website. Scientific Data is glad to consider manuscripts that provide updates on important datasets previously published at Scientific Data or other journals – especially if a new project milestone is reached or if there are important changes in the data collection methods that merit a new publication. New Data Descriptors should refer to common datasets via formal data citation in their reference sections and subject to standard peer review and submission processes. Reviewers will generally look to see if significant changes to the methods, validation, or data description are present to warrant the publication of a new paper. Data citation Authors are required to formally cite any datasets stored in external repositories that are mentioned within their manuscript, including the main datasets that are the focus of the submission, as well as any other datasets that have been used in the work. For previously published datasets, we ask authors to cite both the related research articles and the datasets themselves. Appropriate citation of data is checked and enforced by Scientific Data staff prior to publication. For more information on how to cite datasets in submitted manuscripts, please see our Submission Guidelines .
